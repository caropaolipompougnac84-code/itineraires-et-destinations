Lesson 1. Look Before You Judge

A village, a loon and a fish, and a sacred bond with the land.

Timing note. The source lesson ran well over 52 minutes. This official page is recut: the 60-to-80-word paragraph is finished for homework, and both paintings are described so the work can be done without the images. Teachers who have permission may project their own copy.

Road map

Lesson of 55 minutes, timed for 52 minutes. Eight lessons in this sequence.

  1. Recap: what you already know about Canada 0 to 4
  2. Goal and word box 4 to 8
  3. Jim Logan’s painting, from a description 8 to 18
  4. The First Peoples of Canada; treaty land and unceded land 18 to 26
  5. Norval Morrisseau’s painting, lines of communication 26 to 36
  6. Kin or resource, then a one-minute speaking turn 36 to 44
  7. Written record 44 to 48
  8. Homework 48 to 52
  • Written record. Two sentences: one about the three peoples recognised by the Constitution, and one about land as kin or as a resource.
  • Participation. Everyone speaks for about 60 seconds, or at least one full sentence, on the bond with the land.
  • Homework for next lesson. Learn both vocabulary boxes. Finish the 60-to-80-word paragraph. Write five sentences that describe Jim Logan’s painting, using two place expressions and two modals (might, must, may, can’t).

Stopping point. Minute 44, after the speaking turn. If time runs short, go straight to the written record and the homework.

Emergency exit · 8 min. See the box at the end of the lesson.

Recap · 0 to 4 min

What do you already know about Canada?

  1. What are the two official languages of Canada?
  2. What is the capital of Canada?
  3. Who were the first people to live on the land that is now Canada?

Answer key

  • English and French.
  • Ottawa.
  • Indigenous peoples. Today the Constitution recognises three groups: First Nations, Inuit and Métis.

8 to 18 min

Look at Jim Logan’s painting

Image not reproduced. Jim Logan, No One to Play with but his Dog, © Jim Logan. This official site does not host the work. Teachers who have the artist’s permission may project their own copy. You can do the task from the description below. Artist’s page: jim-logan.net.

In the foreground, a boy with long black hair and a red jacket runs through tall yellow grass with a blue dog. On the right, there is an old pink car with no wheels, and a white object is lying in the grass. In the background, there are small wooden houses painted in bright colours, electricity poles, smoke coming out of the chimneys, a yellow pick-up truck and dark blue hills.

Cover the title in your mind at first. Then answer:

  1. What can you see in the foreground? In the background? On the right?
  2. What is the boy doing? Who is with him?
  3. Where do you think this village is? Why?
  4. What seems to be missing in this village?

Sentence starters: In the foreground, I can see… The village might be in the north of Canada, because… There must be… / There can’t be…, because… What strikes me is that nobody…

Answer key

  • In the foreground, a boy with long black hair and a red jacket runs through tall yellow grass with a blue dog. On the right, an old pink car with no wheels, and a white object in the grass. In the background, small wooden houses in bright colours, electricity poles, smoke, a yellow pick-up truck and dark blue hills.
  • He is running; a blue dog is with him. Nobody is playing with him. The other people, when we imagine the rest of the village, are adults at work or walking.
  • The village might be in the north of Canada: the houses are small, the roads are dirt roads and the smoke suggests that it is cold. Other justified hypotheses are welcome.
  • Possible answers: other children, a playground, a school, a shop, paved roads.

The title and the artist

The title is No One to Play with but his Dog. Does it confirm your hypotheses?

Jim Logan was born in 1955 to a Métis family in Port Coquitlam, British Columbia. He spent time with the Kwanlin Dün First Nation, near Whitehorse, in the Yukon, and later worked as the Indigenous curator of the Art Gallery of Nova Scotia. He calls himself “a social statement artist”.

“It’s my way of communicating in a polite and respectful way the realities of poverty, of living within a hegemonic society and reconciling what ‘reconciliation’ is or if one can really ever achieve such [a thing].”

Jim Logan, quoted by the Canada Council Art Bank, July 2024.

  1. What is a “social statement artist”?
  2. Which word does Jim Logan put in inverted commas? Why, in your opinion?
  3. Does the painting look sad or happy? Can it be both?

Answer key

  • An artist whose work says something about society and its problems.
  • “reconciliation”. He seems to ask what the word really means, and whether reconciliation is possible.
  • Both answers are possible: the colours are bright and the boy is running, but he is alone and the village looks poor.

Sources: Canada Council Art Bank, Dialogues with Jim Logan, July 2024 · Jim Logan, official website

18 to 26 min

Key facts: the First Peoples of Canada

  • First Nations. Many different nations, such as the Cree, the Anishinaabe or the Wet’suwet’en. Many First Nations people live on reserves: land set apart for a First Nation under the Indian Act, with an elected band council.
  • Inuit. The Indigenous people of the Arctic, for example in Nunavut or in Nunatsiavut, in Labrador.
  • Métis. A distinct people, with its own history, culture and language.

According to the 2021 Census, more than 1.8 million people in Canada identify as Indigenous: 5 per cent of the population. Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982 recognises the rights of the “Indian, Inuit and Métis peoples of Canada”.

Treaty land or unceded land? In much of Canada, the Crown signed treaties with First Nations: treaty land. In British Columbia, most of the land was never ceded or surrendered by treaty: it is unceded land. This difference will matter in lessons 4 and 5.

  1. What are the three Indigenous peoples recognised by the Constitution?
  2. What is the difference between treaty land and unceded land?

Answer key

  • First Nations, Inuit and Métis.
  • Treaty land was the subject of a treaty between the Crown and a First Nation. Unceded land was never given up by treaty.

Sources: Justice Laws, Constitution Act, 1982, section 35 · Crown-Indigenous Relations and Northern Affairs Canada, Indigenous peoples and communities

26 to 36 min

Anishinaabe art: lines of communication

Image not reproduced. Norval Morrisseau (Anishinaabe, 1931 or 1932 to 2007), Loon and Fish (Woodland style; title used descriptively), © Norval Morrisseau Estate. This official site does not host the work. Teachers who have the estate’s permission may project their own copy. Estate information: officialmorrisseau.com.

The painting uses heavy black outlines, bright colours, and an “x-ray” view that shows the inside of animals. In the top left-hand corner there is a sun. On the left, a loon; on the right, a fish. Red lines join the sun to the loon, and the loon to the fish. In the bottom right-hand corner, a circular signature in Indigenous syllabics (often called Cree syllabics), with a small date mark. The Art Canada Institute explains that Morrisseau’s “lines of communication” join animals and people: “They mark relationships”.

We do not claim one official meaning for this single painting. We use careful language: may represent, is often read as, in Woodland art more generally.

  1. Name the beings and the elements you can see. Where do the red lines start and end?
  2. What does the “x-ray” view let you see?
  3. The loon and the fish share water and air. What kind of relationship might the painting be inviting us to see?
  4. If the x-ray style shows an inner life, how might that change the way we look at an animal: as an object, or as a being?
  5. Compare with Jim Logan’s painting: which one shows people, which one shows a link with nature?

Answer key

  • A sun, a loon, a fish, red lines, a circular signature. One line joins the sun to the loon; another joins the loon and the fish.
  • Inner segments of colour that look like organs or a life force: the outside and the inside are shown together.
  • Possible answer: a relationship of interdependence: what happens in the water touches the bird, and what happens under the sun touches both.
  • Possible answer: if we see an inner life, the animal is harder to treat as a mere thing; it asks for respect.
  • Jim Logan shows a village and people; this painting shows animals and their relationships. Both say something about life on the land.

Sources: Art Canada Institute, Norval Morrisseau, Style and Technique · The Canadian Encyclopedia, Norval Morrisseau

36 to 44 min · stopping point at 44

Connect, then speak: kin or resource?

Hold two ways of seeing the same river or the same valley:

  • Land and water as kin: living relatives, sacred places, a responsibility of stewardship toward future generations.
  • Land and water as property or resource: something to buy, sell, dam or pipe for energy and money.

Later lessons will meet dams (James Bay, Muskrat Falls, Site C) and pipelines (Coastal GasLink, Trans Mountain). The conflict is often not only about jobs or kilowatts: it is also about whether a place can be treated as a resource when, for many First Nations, it is kin.

Speak, about 60 seconds. Starting from Morrisseau’s painting, explain why the bond with the land can feel almost sacred, and why that matters when people debate dams or pipelines. A partner listens and asks one question, then you swap. If you work alone, say the turn aloud and write one sentence you would keep.

Useful language: When I look at the loon and the fish, I see… The lines may mark… If land is a living relative, then… That is why who decides about the water…

Written record · 44 to 48 min

Copy two sentences into your notebook, in your own words:

  1. The Constitution recognises three Indigenous peoples: First Nations, Inuit and Métis. Some land is treaty land; much of British Columbia is unceded land.
  2. For many First Nations, land and water are kin, not only a resource. That changes the question: who decides what happens to them?

48 to 52 min

Grammar spotlight and homework

To say where: in the foreground / in the background; in the top left-hand corner; in the bottom right-hand corner. To make a hypothesis: might, must, may, can’t + the base verb, without to. The village must be in the north. He might be lonely.

Building your vocabulary

Homework. Learn both vocabulary boxes. Write a short paragraph (60 to 80 words): from Morrisseau’s painting to the land, why can nature hold a sacred place in Indigenous cultures, and how does that change the way we hear debates about dams and pipelines? Use at least three of these words: kinship, sacred, interconnection, living relative, resource, stewardship. Hedge your claims (may, might, is often read as). Also write five sentences that describe Jim Logan’s painting: two expressions of place, two modals.

Answer key · sample paragraph, about 75 words

In Morrisseau’s Woodland painting, the loon, the fish and the sun are joined by lines that are often read as relationships. The x-ray style may suggest that animals have an inner life, not only an outer shape. For many Anishinaabe and other First Nations teachings, land and water are living relatives, almost sacred. If a river is kin, a dam or a pipeline is never only a resource project: who decides must also hear that spiritual bond and the duty of stewardship.

Emergency exit · 8 min

If time runs short, replace the unfinished plan with this condensed ending (inside the 52 minutes, not 52 plus 8).

  • 0 to 2. Everyone says this sentence aloud: “Land and water can be kin, not only a resource.”
  • 2 to 5. Copy the written record: The Constitution recognises First Nations, Inuit and Métis. Some land is unceded.
  • 5 to 7. Write down the homework: Finish the paragraph and learn the word boxes.
  • 7 to 8. Check one answer in the answer key.
