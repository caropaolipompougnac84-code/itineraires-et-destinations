Lesson 4. Images, Claims and Reported Speech

How can we tell evidence, interpretation and persuasion apart?

Road map

Lesson of 55 minutes, timed for 52 minutes. Lesson 4 of 7. Minutes 52 to 55 stay free for collecting work, moving and the change of room.

  1. Paired comparison of Puerto Rico and Washington, D.C. 4 min (minutes 0 to 4)
  2. Six-point midpoint check, Exercise 4A 6 min (minutes 4 to 10)
  3. Immediate correction 4 min (minutes 10 to 14)
  4. Observe Stump Speaking and read Source D 7 min (minutes 14 to 21)
  5. Classify the statements, Exercise 4B 7 min (minutes 21 to 28)
  6. Reported speech, Exercise 4C 7 min (minutes 28 to 35)
  7. Candidate and reporter exchange, Exercise 4D 9 min (minutes 35 to 44)
  8. Written record 4 min (minutes 44 to 48)
  9. Exit ticket: one attributed statement 2 min (minutes 48 to 50)
  10. Homework 2 min (minutes 50 to 52)
  • Written record. Images and messages can inform and persuade at the same time; a promise is not an outcome; a cautious rewrite must not add new, unverified facts.
  • Participation. Everyone speaks at least once: each partner plays the candidate and the reporter in 4D.
  • Homework for next lesson. Using a teacher-approved election report, record its publisher, title, date and URL, paraphrase one factual claim and identify one interpretation or prediction; make two flashcards.

Stopping point. Minute 44, after both partners have acted as reporter.

Emergency exit · 8 min. If only eight working minutes remain, this replaces the unfinished plan; it is not added to it.
  1. Minutes 0 to 2: each partner reports one invented classroom statement.
  2. Minutes 2 to 5: write the written record.
  3. Minutes 5 to 7: record the homework.
  4. Minute 7 to 8: name one piece of evidence needed to test a promise.

Objectives

Analyse a second painting, report statements accurately and avoid inventing neutral-sounding facts.

Language: reported speech, attribution (according to…), cautious interpretation.

Source D

Stump Speaking

Reference. George Caleb Bingham, Stump Speaking, 1853 to 1854, oil on canvas, Saint Louis Art Museum, accession 43:2001. Museum record.

Student text, newly written from the museum record.

A candidate addresses a rural crowd. Another man sits behind him with notes, waiting for his turn. The museum identifies the speaker as Erasmus Darwin Sappington, Bingham’s opponent in a contested election.

The museum also explains that Bingham intended the painting to celebrate democratic elections for a wider national audience. This is a sourced interpretation of the artist’s purpose. It should not be confused with something we can establish from the visible image alone.

A painting can therefore be an artistic construction, a historical source and a representation shaped by personal experience. It is not a neutral recording of everything that happened.

Teacher explanation or extension

According to the museum, Bingham ran as a Whig candidate for the Missouri state legislature in 1846. He won by three votes, but the result was contested and the seat was given to his opponent, Sappington. Keep this story out of the compulsory reading; use it to ask B2 students how personal experience may shape a painting.

Exercise 4A

Midpoint check · six points

One point each.

  1. Give the artist of The Verdict of the People.
  2. Name the two chambers of Congress.
  3. Which chamber considers many presidential nominations?
  4. Give one power of Puerto Rico’s Resident Commissioner.
  5. Which has presidential electors: Puerto Rico or Washington, D.C.?
  6. Complete: “The bill must approved by both chambers.”

Answer key

  1. George Caleb Bingham.
  2. The House of Representatives and the Senate.
  3. The Senate.
  4. Introduce bills, take part in debate, or serve and vote on committees.
  5. Washington, D.C.
  6. be.
Exercise 4B

Classify the statement, not just its tone

V: verifiable institutional statement. J: judgement. P: prediction or promise. A: attributed interpretation. Some statements may need a different label: say why.

  1. The House has 435 voting seats.
  2. This is the only candidate who cares about ordinary people.
  3. The proposal will solve the problem.
  4. According to the museum, Bingham intended a broader celebration of elections.
  5. The painting contains several figures listening to a speaker.
  6. The election result proves that every voter supports the winning party’s entire programme.

Then repair statement 6 without inventing a result.

Answer key

  1. V.
  2. J.
  3. P.
  4. A.
  5. A visual description that anyone can check in the painting.
  6. An unsupported interpretation.

Possible repair: “The result identifies the winner under the election rules. It does not by itself show agreement with every policy.”

Exercise 4C

Reported speech

These are invented classroom statements, not historical quotations.

Common B1+ route

  1. A fictional candidate says: “I support the proposal.” Begin: Yesterday, the candidate said…
  2. Two fictional campaigners say: “We will publish the cost.” Begin: They told the audience…

Towards B2

  1. A fictional reporter asks me: “Did you check the source?” Begin: The reporter asked me…
  2. A fictional student says: “The source is incomplete.” Begin: The student explained…
  3. Explain why a reporter might keep the present tense when describing a claim that is still current.

Answer key

  1. “…that they supported the proposal.”
  2. “…that they would publish the cost.”
  3. “…if (or whether) I had checked the source.”
  4. “…that the source was incomplete.”
  5. A present-tense report can be justified when the reporter deliberately presents the statement as still true. The choice should keep the intended meaning.

Accept a suitable pronoun (he, she, they). Discuss justified alternatives without backshift rather than marking them wrong automatically.

Exercise 4D

Candidate and reporter (pairs)

Partner A gives a 30-second fictional school-council message with one proposal, one reason and one promise.

Partner B reports it and asks: “What evidence would help us evaluate that promise?” Swap roles.

Do not impersonate a real politician or invent historical speech.

Answer key

  • The report keeps the original meaning.
  • A promise is not presented as an achieved result.
  • The follow-up question asks for relevant evidence.
  • Both students take both roles.
Exercise 4E · main production

Accurate and persuasive?

Can a message contain accurate information and still try to persuade? Explain with one classroom example.

Common B1+ route: four connected sentences.

Towards B2: explain how selection or emphasis can shape interpretation even when the stated information is accurate.

Answer key

Possible answer: “A message can give accurate information and still try to persuade. It may select the most favourable details and leave other relevant information out. For example, a campaign message may describe a proposal without explaining its cost. We therefore need to examine both accuracy and selection.”

Quick exercise. Choose the best verb: “We should describe / verify whether the figure matches the original source.”

Answer key

verify.

Written record

Images and messages can inform and persuade at the same time. We must identify who makes a claim and what supports it. A promise is not an outcome. A cautious rewrite must not introduce new, unverified facts.

Exit ticket

Write one attributed statement about Stump Speaking, starting with “According to the museum,…”.

Homework

Using a teacher-approved election report (or the printed report supplied by your teacher):

  1. Record the publisher, title, date and URL.
  2. Paraphrase one factual claim.
  3. Identify one interpretation or prediction.
  4. Do not reproduce the article in full.

Make two flashcards.
