Why this lesson matters

This lesson helps you analyse a line or an incident in Shakespeare, and show how it reveals a character, provokes a decision or moves the action forward. The spirale de Gorse leads you, tour by tour, to a coherent and fair paragraph, built from what you have found in the text. That same work, including the reading aloud, the phonology exercise, and the question of who pulls the strings in the theatre, prepares you for the written and oral papers of the baccalauréat.

Approach: Going further in Shakespeare's writing

This lesson plan is built on one reading skill: showing how a line or an incident can reveal a character, provoke a decision or move the action forward. Each play explores one of these three dynamics, with short extracts, an analysis and a sentence frame to help students write their answer.

The "I can" objective: "I can refer to the text and find examples that support my thoughts."

  • Argument writing: a claim, evidence from the text, an explanation.
  • The lesson structure: warm-up, whole-class annotation, guided practice in small groups, individual exit question.
  • Support: sentence frames, partner work, visual aids.

The key standard in the grid says that a line or an incident can reveal a character, provoke a decision or move the action forward. Each play below follows one of these three dynamics.

Othello: a line that reveals a character (Iago)

Extract 1 (Act 1, Scene 1)

"I am not what I am."

Extract 2 (Act 1, Scene 3)

"I hate the Moor,
And it is thought abroad that 'twixt my sheets
'Has done my office. I know not if 't be true,
But I, for mere suspicion in that kind,
Will do as if for surety."

Extract 3 (Act 3, Scene 3)

"O, beware, my lord, of jealousy! It is the green-eyed monster which doth mock
The meat it feeds on."

Analysis

In five short words, Iago tells us he is two-faced. In his soliloquy, he admits that a rumour is enough to make him hate: "mere suspicion" becomes "surety". Then he warns Othello against jealousy, the very poison he is pouring into his ear. Students can see the gap between what a character says to others and what he confides to the audience.

Sentence frame: "This line reveals that Iago is… because he says…"

Hamlet: a line that provokes a decision

Extract 1 (Act 1, Scene 5), the Ghost

"Revenge his foul and most unnatural murder."

Extract 2 (Act 1, Scene 5), Hamlet

"The time is out of joint. O cursèd spite,
That ever I was born to set it right!"

Extract 3 (Act 2, Scene 2), end of the soliloquy

"The play's the thing
Wherein I'll catch the conscience of the King."

Analysis

The Ghost's command forces a decision on Hamlet, but he feels it as a burden ("cursed spite"). He does not decide to kill at once. First, he decides to make sure. The arrival of the players provokes his real decision: to set a trap with a play. Students can follow the path from the order he receives, to his doubt, to his final choice.

Sentence frame: "After hearing…, Hamlet decides to… This shows that…"

Analysis

Each line pushes the plot one step further. The prophecy awakens Macbeth's ambition. When he hesitates, Lady Macbeth relaunches the action by questioning his manhood. His final "I am settled" closes the door: the murder will happen. Students can draw an arrow across Act 1: prophecy, hesitation, pressure, decision.

From the line to the voice: a phonology exercise

Shakespeare writes most of his verse in iambic pentameter: five beats, each made of an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed one (da-DUM, five times). English is a stress-timed language: the stressed syllables give the line its rhythm, and the unstressed ones fit in between. French verse works differently. The alexandrine counts twelve syllables, with a pause, the caesura, after the sixth.

Line A, Shakespeare (Hamlet, Act 2, Scene 2)

"Wherein I'll catch the conscience of the King."

Line B, Jean Racine, Athalie, 1691, Act 1, Scene 1

"Oui, je viens dans son temple adorer l'Éternel."

  1. On line A, mark the five stressed syllables. Then read the line aloud.
  2. On line B, count the twelve syllables and mark the caesura, after the sixth. Then read the line aloud.
  3. What is the difference between English scansion, based on stress (l'accentuation), and the French alexandrine, based on the number of syllables?

From the beat to the song

That stress is also why songs in English often sit so naturally on the music: the stresses fall on the beat, and that is the best way to place your voice and to make a song. French, which gives a more equal time to each syllable, sits on a beat in another way.

  1. Tap or clap a regular beat. Read line A again, so that each stress falls on a tap.
  2. Then chant the same line on that simple beat, as if you were starting a song.
  3. Optional. Do the same with this short public-domain song line, from Shakespeare, The Tempest: "Full fathom five thy father lies."
Answer key (teacher): phonology exercise, questions 1 to 6

Line A, scansion. Where-IN I'll CATCH the CON-science OF the KING. Five iambs: an unstressed syllable, then a stressed one, five times. "Conscience" is sounded in two syllables in this line (CON-science).

Line B, scansion. Oui / je / viens / dans / son / tem- // ple / a- / do- / rer / l'É- / ter-nel. Twelve syllables, caesura after temple (the sixth syllable).

The difference. English scansion is based on stress (l'accentuation): we count five beats, not a fixed number of equal syllables. The French alexandrine is based on the number of syllables: twelve syllables, with a caesura at the hemistich, after the sixth. That is why the two lines do not sit on the voice in the same way.

From the beat to the song. Because English is stress-timed, the stresses fall at regular intervals and can land on the beat of a song. That is why songs in English often sit so naturally on the music, and why stress is the best way to place one's voice. French is more syllable-timed: each syllable takes a more equal time, so the alexandrine sits on a beat in another way. A shared sonnet: Romeo and Juliet, Act 1, Scene 5

In this meeting, Romeo and Juliet compose one sonnet together. The two voices answer each other, like birdsong: that shared rhythm is part of the beauty of the scene. In class, you may watch this meeting in Franco Zeffirelli's film Romeo and Juliet (1968), from the original source only. The film is not hosted here.

William Shakespeare, Romeo and Juliet, Act 1, Scene 5. Text: Folger Shakespeare Library Digital Text.

ROMEO
If I profane with my unworthiest hand
This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this:
My lips, two blushing pilgrims, ready stand
To smooth that rough touch with a tender kiss. JULIET
Good pilgrim, you do wrong your hand too much,
Which mannerly devotion shows in this;
For saints have hands that pilgrims' hands do touch,
And palm to palm is holy palmers' kiss. ROMEO
Have not saints lips, and holy palmers too? JULIET
Ay, pilgrim, lips that they must use in prayer. ROMEO
O then, dear saint, let lips do what hands do. They pray: grant thou, lest faith turn to despair. JULIET
Saints do not move, though grant for prayers' sake. ROMEO
Then move not while my prayer's effect I take.

[He kisses her.]

  1. Show how the two voices share the quatrains, the rhymes and the final couplet. Who speaks the first four lines? Who speaks the next four? How are the last six lines shared?
  2. Scan a few lines: mark the stresses in Romeo's first line and in Juliet's first line, then in the final couplet.
  3. In pairs, read the sonnet aloud, answering each other, like birdsong.
Answer key (teacher): shared sonnet, questions 7 to 9

The shared sonnet, structure. Folger Shakespeare Library Digital Text, Romeo and Juliet, Act 1, Scene 5. It is one English sonnet, fourteen lines, spoken by two voices.

  • Lines 1 to 4, Romeo: first quatrain, rhymes hand / stand, this / kiss.
  • Lines 5 to 8, Juliet: second quatrain, rhymes much / touch, this / kiss. She answers him, and she shares the rhyme on kiss.
  • Lines 9 to 12, shared: Romeo (line 9), Juliet (line 10), Romeo (lines 11 and 12). Rhymes too / do, prayer / despair.
  • Lines 13 and 14, the couplet, still shared: Juliet (sake), Romeo (take). The stage direction follows: [He kisses her.]

The two voices answer each other, like birdsong. That fluidity is part of the beauty of the scene. From the line to the voice: a phonology exercise

Read one step. Then do the next step.

English verse has a beat. The strong syllables are the beat: da-DUM, five times. That is iambic pentameter. French verse counts twelve syllables. That is the alexandrine. There is a short pause, the caesura, after syllable six.

Line A, Shakespeare (Hamlet, Act 2, Scene 2)

"Wherein I'll catch the conscience of the King."

The strong syllables are: in, catch, con, of, King. Say: Where-IN I'll CATCH the CON-science OF the KING.

Line B, Racine, Athalie, Act 1, Scene 1

"Oui, je viens dans son temple adorer l'Éternel."

  1. Tap when you say a strong syllable in line A. Read the line slowly.
  2. On line B, count twelve syllables. Draw a short line after temple, the sixth syllable. Read the line slowly.
  3. English counts stresses (l'accentuation). French counts syllables. Write that difference in one sentence.

From the beat to the song

English stresses fall on the beat of a song. That is why songs in English often sit so naturally on the music, and why stress is the best way to place your voice. French gives a more equal time to each syllable, so the line sits on a beat in another way.

  1. Clap a regular beat. Read line A again, so that each strong syllable falls on a clap.
  2. Then chant line A on that same beat, as if you were starting a song.
  3. Optional. Do the same with this public-domain song line, from Shakespeare, The Tempest: "Full fathom five thy father lies."

A shared sonnet: Romeo and Juliet, Act 1, Scene 5

In this meeting, Romeo and Juliet compose one sonnet together. The two voices answer each other, like birdsong: that shared rhythm is part of the beauty of the scene. In class, you may watch this meeting in Franco Zeffirelli's film Romeo and Juliet (1968), from the original source only. The film is not hosted here.

William Shakespeare, Romeo and Juliet, Act 1, Scene 5. Text: Folger Shakespeare Library Digital Text.

ROMEO
If I profane with my unworthiest hand
This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this:
My lips, two blushing pilgrims, ready stand
To smooth that rough touch with a tender kiss. JULIET
Good pilgrim, you do wrong your hand too much,
Which mannerly devotion shows in this;
For saints have hands that pilgrims' hands do touch,
And palm to palm is holy palmers' kiss. ROMEO
Have not saints lips, and holy palmers too? JULIET
Ay, pilgrim, lips that they must use in prayer. ROMEO
O then, dear saint, let lips do what hands do. They pray: grant thou, lest faith turn to despair. JULIET
Saints do not move, though grant for prayers' sake. ROMEO
Then move not while my prayer's effect I take.

[He kisses her.]

  1. Look at who speaks. Romeo speaks the first four lines. Juliet speaks the next four. Then they share the lines, one after the other, until the last two lines (the couplet).
  2. With a partner, read the sonnet aloud. One of you is Romeo, the other is Juliet. Answer each other, like birdsong.
  3. Mark the strong syllables in Romeo's first line and in Juliet's first line. Tap as you read them.

Master of Puppets: Iago, and Shakespeare himself

In class, listen to "Master of Puppets," by Metallica (1986), from an official source only (the band's official channel or a listening platform). The song is not hosted here, and no link is given. The lyrics are not copied here, because they are protected. The title is enough.

Inside the play, Iago is a master of puppets: he pulls the strings of Othello, and his soliloquies take us into his plan. Beyond the characters, Shakespeare himself is the ultimate master of puppets: he writes Iago, the Ghost, Lady Macbeth, and the audience. When the play is performed before our eyes, we are consenting victims. We came to be moved. The title can be heard as a hymn to the manipulation that theatre offers.

Les citations

L'édition de référence est le Folger Shakespeare Library Digital Text. Les actes et les scènes indiqués dans le texte de préparation sont exacts. Quelques leçons imprimées donnent une leçon un peu différente ; le Folger a été suivi sur ces points :

  • Othello, I, 3 : « 'Has done my office » (et non « He has done my office ») ; « if 't be true » (et non « if't be so »).
  • Othello, III, 3 : « jealousy! », avec un point d'exclamation.
  • Hamlet, I, 5 : « O cursèd spite », avec l'accent du Folger.
  • Hamlet, II, 2 : « the King », avec une majuscule.
  • Macbeth, I, 7 : « sticking place », sans trait d'union ; « I am settled and bend up », sans virgule après « settled ». Dans le texte de l'analyse, « cursed spite » et « I am settled » restent tels quels, puisqu'ils appartiennent au commentaire, non à l'extrait.

La spirale de Gorse

Une spirale par pièce, en cinq tours, puis la pointe : I notice, I spot, I understand, I anticipate, I find the meaning. À la pointe, les élèves peuvent s'appuyer sur « The point is… » et, en alternance, sur l'une des deux formules de recentrage : pour Othello, « I keep to the text » ; pour Hamlet, « I stay focused on » ; pour Macbeth, « I keep to the text ». Chaque spirale se termine par : « Beyond this, other questions may arise and open up further exploration. »

La spirale de Gorse facilite l'écriture d'un paragraphe par concaténation des idées : chaque tour fournit une phrase reliée à la suivante. Les cinq réponses de l'exemple, mises bout à bout avec des mots de liaison (First, Then, Next, So, In the end), forment le paragraphe modèle. Ce paragraphe sert à exprimer une analyse cohérente et juste, construite à partir de ce que l'élève a trouvé à chaque tour.

Avant « I notice », le professeur lit les extraits de la pièce à voix haute, en anglais. Les élèves font d'abord la spirale modèle sous les yeux, puis remplissent la spirale vierge. Ils peuvent s'aider du cadre de phrases déjà donné pour cette pièce. Le professeur pose les questions ; il ne donne pas la réponse.

Minutage proposé, en trois leçons de 52 minutes

Leçon 1. Accueil et regard sur l'œuvre. Lecture de l'approche et de l'objectif « I can ». Othello : lecture à voix haute, annotation en classe entière, spirale de Gorse, cadre de phrases.

Leçon 2. Hamlet, puis Macbeth : même geste, en petits groupes. Question de sortie, individuelle, si le temps le permet.

Leçon 3. Exercice de phonologie, sonnet partagé de Roméo et Juliette, écoute de « Master of Puppets » depuis une source officielle, spirale et débat. Question de sortie, si elle n'a pas encore été faite.

Issue de secours. Si le temps manque, on traite une pièce en classe et les deux autres à l'écrit, ou l'on garde, pour chaque spirale, « I notice », « I spot » et « I find the meaning ». La phonologie et « Master of Puppets » peuvent ouvrir une leçon suivante. La question de sortie peut aussi ouvrir la leçon suivante.
