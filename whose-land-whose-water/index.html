Whose Land, Whose Water?

When a government makes promises to Indigenous peoples, who decides what happens to the land and the water, and how can citizens check whether those promises are kept?

Road map

Eight lessons of 55 minutes, each timed for 52 minutes. Lesson 8 is the final task: a citizens’ hearing and an open letter.

  1. Lesson 1. Look Before You Judge 52 min
  2. Lesson 2. 1969: the White Paper and the Red Paper 52 min
  3. Lesson 3. Rivers into Reservoirs 52 min
  4. Lesson 4. Dams Today: Muskrat Falls and Site C 52 min
  5. Lesson 5. Pipelines on Unceded Land 52 min
  6. Lesson 6. Promises Kept, Promises Broken 52 min
  7. Lesson 7. The Grizzlies: Youth, Land and Hope 52 min
  8. Lesson 8. Final Task: A Citizens’ Hearing and an Open Letter 52 min
  • Written record. Each lesson ends with a short written record, copied in the notebook.
  • Participation. Everyone speaks at least once in every lesson.
  • Homework for next lesson. Each lesson page states the homework; lesson 7 prepares the dossier, and lesson 8 is the final task.

Stopping point. Each lesson page marks a stopping point, so that the end of the lesson can be condensed if need be.

Emergency exit · 8 min. If time runs short, a condensed eight-minute ending (inside the 52 minutes, not 52 plus 8) still leaves every student with the written record, one spoken sentence, and the homework.

The assessment page has the oral grid for the hearing and the written grid for the open letter. The language page (promises and the passive) is used in lessons 2 and 6.

Official wording, in French. Axe 6, « Les aires anglophones américaines », compulsory: « Quelles sont les influences réciproques qui engendrent une perméabilité culturelle, parfois inégale, autour du continent américain ? » Associated axe: axe 2, « Diversité et inclusion », objet d’étude « Représentation politique des Peuples premiers : intégration ou appropriation ? ». The BO states that teachers « peuvent aussi en associer deux, reliés par une problématique commune, dans une même séquence ».

Lexis reused from the BO: « Héritages » (indigenous populations, reconciliation, reparation, to grapple with colonial history, to right wrongs, to come to terms with, to enshrine rights); « Les énergies » (a dam, fossil fuels, to object to something, to work at full capacity); « Discrimination et exclusion » (to deny access to, to be on an equal footing, to wipe out a right); « La nature » (to fall short of); « Art et engagement » (a sense of resilience).

Your mission

Guiding question. When a government makes promises to Indigenous peoples, who decides what happens to the land and the water, and how can citizens check whether those promises are kept?

Dams, pipelines and reservoirs bring energy and money to Canada. Many of them stand on land and rivers where First Nations, Inuit and Métis have lived for thousands of years. Since 1969, Canadian governments have made many promises to Indigenous peoples: some were kept, some were broken, and some are still on the table.

In this sequence you will look at two works of art, read official figures and check them, and always look at both sides: what was done, and what was not done. Videos are optional: every core task can be done from the text on the page.

How we work

  • We talk about real people and real communities. We use the names that peoples give themselves: the Cree of Eeyou Istchee, the Inuit of Nunatsiavut, the Wet’suwet’en.
  • Every figure comes with its source. If you use a figure, you give its source too.
  • People in the same community can disagree. We listen to every voice with respect.

Figures that change. Drinking-water figures are those of Indigenous Services Canada as of 21 October 2026: 158 long-term advisories lifted since November 2015, 39 still in effect, in 37 communities. They were checked against the teaching dossier on 4 November 2026. They change often: verify them before you use them in class. In September 2026 Ottawa offered a collective 15 per cent stake in Trans Mountain; at the date of the dossier that offer was still in negotiation.

At the end of this sequence you will be able to

  • describe a work of art and make hypotheses, with careful language (may, might, is often read as);
  • explain why land and water can hold a sacred place in Anishinaabe and other First Nations cultures;
  • report a promise and judge it with facts and sources;
  • use the passive to say what was done, and was going to for a plan that was not kept;
  • compare figures with precision;
  • defend a point of view in a role play and in a hearing, and write an open letter.

Works of art, videos and a related film study

Lesson 1 studies two paintings, reproduced here with permission: Norval Morrisseau, Loon and Fish, and Jim Logan, No One to Play with but his Dog. Look at the images, then use the tasks on the lesson page. A short quotation by Jim Logan, published by the Canada Council Art Bank in July 2024, is kept with its source.

Lesson 7 stands alone with a synopsis of The Grizzlies and a Telefilm Canada interview (optional video, with a warning about difficult topics). A separate film-study resource for that film is not yet on this official site.

Two related sequences, not yet migrated here, can later extend the work: a Terminale sequence on Indigenous territories in several countries, and a sequence on Canada and the promise of welcome. They are not required for this Première unit.
