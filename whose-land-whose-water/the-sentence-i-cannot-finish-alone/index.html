Your sentence starters, turn by turn

  • Turn 2. We see… We hear… / The camera shows…, so I notice…
  • Turn 3. If the film showed…, I might… / Another detail is…

The Sentence I Cannot Finish Alone

A method for starting the study of The Grizzlies, in lesson 7 of Whose Land, Whose Water?

What this method is for

This method opens the study. It is a way of organising thought, not the whole lesson.

Students begin with wide, personal questions: who would you be, what shocks you, what surprises you, what do you like. They then look at the film’s choices, test their reading against another spectator’s detail, and only then reach the tip of the spiral. At that tip, a political, civilisational or existential problem emerges, and each student writes a claim they can defend, with a limit: what they can argue, and what they cannot claim.

The 52-minute lesson, the speaking circuit, the tick tables and the homework are in the documents below. The film viewing worksheet already on lesson 7 stays in place.

Spiral method devised by Madame Carole Paoli Pompougnac.

The spiral of questions

The spiral is drawn as a real line that tightens towards the tip. At each station, the question sits on the outside and the expected answer sits on the inside, in the dashed box. The student document then gives a table of the same nine stations, with the reasoning step and the language work in play at that moment.

Care. Students enter a situation. They never take on someone else’s identity. Shock is about a moment or an action, never a whole community. Nobody has to share anything personal. Never ask a student to put themselves in the place of someone in a suicidal crisis. One film does not represent every Inuit experience. Inform families before the home viewing, and check that every student can watch the film lawfully.
