Lesson 4. Crowned on camera, 2 June 1953

Did television make the monarchy stronger, or more fragile?

Road map

Lesson of 55 minutes, timed for 52 minutes. 8 lessons.

  1. Recap (marked /3) 5 min
  2. Beaton’s coronation portrait from the official page, and the broadcast 12 min
  3. The sacred and the visible: why the anointing was not filmed 12 min
  4. Debate: stronger or more fragile? 16 min
  5. Exit ticket and preparation for the mid-sequence test 7 min
  • Written record. One reason why television made the monarchy stronger, one reason why it made it more fragile, and a note on the hidden anointing.
  • Participation. Everyone speaks at least once: one argument in the debate, with a concession if possible.
  • Homework for next lesson. Revise the method, the Ditchley key points and the 1947 quotation. Do not prepare a full analysis of the Armada Portrait.

Stopping point. End of this lesson.

Emergency exit · 8 min. If time runs short at this stopping point, condense the end of the lesson: dictate a short written record (two sentences); a quick round so that everyone speaks at least once (one sentence each); write the homework on the board.

You already know, from Who Rules Britain?, that a coronation can be classed as a dignified ceremony. This lesson does not repeat that sort. What is new: the 1953 coronation as a media event. What was shown, what was hidden, and what happens to dignified power once millions of people can watch it at home.

Recap · marked /3
  1. When and where was the 1947 vow broadcast?
  2. What does the antithesis “long or short” do in that speech?
  3. Why is “imperial family” a problem in 1947?

Answer key

  1. 21 April 1947, on the radio, from Cape Town.
  2. It makes the vow total, whatever the length of her life.
  3. It hides domination behind a metaphor of affection, as the Empire was ending.

Cecil Beaton’s official coronation portrait is not reproduced here (still in copyright). Show it in class from the Royal Collection Trust. Apply the five-step method: format, pose, regalia, gaze, and the image of monarchy it constructs. A short extract of the BBC broadcast can be shown from the official BBC or Royal Family channels. No stills from The Crown are used as a source in this lesson: the series is fiction, and is studied as such in lesson 8.

  • The sacred and the visible. The anointing was not filmed. Why keep one moment hidden when everything else is televised? Link this with the idea, already studied, that royalty can depend on mystery as well as on being seen.
  • Debate. Did television make the monarchy stronger or more fragile? Use a concession and, if you can, a hypothesis (If the anointing had been filmed…).

Answer key · key points

Television made millions of people witnesses and created a shared national moment. But it also turned the monarchy into a show, open to judgement: once the Crown depends on images, it can be damaged by images. The hidden anointing tries to keep one last sacred space.

Exit ticket
  1. Give one reason why television made the monarchy stronger, and one reason why it made it more fragile.
  2. Why was the anointing not filmed?

Answer key

  1. Stronger: a shared national moment, a face in every home. More fragile: the Crown becomes a show, open to criticism.
  2. To keep a sacred moment out of the public gaze: not everything dignified can be shown.

Homework

Next lesson is the mid-sequence assessment (an unseen portrait and a short argument on the 1947 vow). Revise the five-step method, the Ditchley key points, and the 1947 quotation. Do not look up a full analysis of the Armada Portrait: it is the unseen document.
