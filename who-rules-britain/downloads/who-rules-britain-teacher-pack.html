Framework

Problem

Is the United Kingdom governed by its King, its Parliament, its courts or its people? And can a country with no single written constitution protect democracy?

Curriculum

Terminale, theme 6 « The United Kingdom and its nations » (a Kingdom still united?), with links to the political speech topic and to citizenship. This is the frame used in The King Reigns, Parliament Rules.

Levels

Tronc commun, Terminale: B2 (the core tasks). Spécialité LLCER anglais: towards C1 (the C1 extensions). In Première, the tronc commun target is B1+ towards B2: use The King Reigns.

Why this version is harder

  • Every session starts from an unadapted primary source: a charter, a statute, a court judgment, a speech or a parliamentary briefing.
  • Students compare competing theories (Dicey and Bagehot) and test them against real crises: Brexit, prorogation in 2019, Scottish independence.
  • The tasks are argumentative: rebuttal, mediation of a source into French, a mock Supreme Court ruling, a debate in the Commons.
  • The vocabulary is the vocabulary of constitutional debate: sovereignty, convention, accountability, legitimacy, devolution, mandate.

Lesson 1 · 52 min

Session 1 · A constitution nobody can print

ReadingDebate
You already know (The King Reigns, session 1 and session 5): the King is impartial and cannot give orders; Parliament makes the laws; Royal Assent is a formality. Now: where do these rules come from, if there is no written constitution?

Question: Can a country be a democracy without a written constitution?

Parliament has "the right to make or unmake any law whatever; and, further, … no person or body is recognised by the law of England as having a right to override or set aside the legislation of Parliament."A. V. Dicey, Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution, 1885, chapter 1 (public domain)
  1. Warm-up (7 min). Students list where the "rules" of France are written (the Constitution of 1958). Then: where would you look for the rules of the UK? Answer: statutes, court decisions, conventions, works of authority.
  2. Reading Dicey (15 min). Identify the two halves of the principle: positive (make or unmake any law) and negative (no one can override it). Why is this both a strength and a danger?
  3. Concept: convention (6 min). A rule that is followed but not legally enforced, such as the monarch giving Royal Assent to every bill passed by Parliament. The last refusal was by Queen Anne in 1708.
  4. Mini-debate (14 min). Motion: "This house believes the UK should adopt a written constitution." Two speakers per side, one minute each; then the floor: every other student asks a question or adds one argument.

Model answer: strength and danger

Strength: the system is flexible; Parliament can respond quickly to change, and the elected Commons has the last word. Danger: a government with a large majority could in theory pass any law, even one that removes rights, because no court can strike down an Act of Parliament. Critics call this an "elective dictatorship", a phrase used by Lord Hailsham in 1976.

  2. The Bill of Rights 1689
  3. The monarch always gives Royal Assent
  4. The Prime Minister must command the confidence of the Commons
  5. R (Miller) v The Prime Minister, 2019
  6. Dicey, Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution
  7. The Constitution of 4 October 1958

Lesson 2 · 52 min

Session 2 · Magna Carta, 1215: myth and law

Primary sourceMediation
You already know (The King Reigns, session 2 and session 3): the story of Runnymede, the rewriting of chapter 29 in today's English, its afterlife in the Fifth Amendment and in 1948. Now: we do not rewrite the clause again; we read it as a legal text, and we ask who turned it into a myth, and why.

Question: Is Magna Carta a founding text of democracy, or a deal between a king and his barons?

"No freeman shall be taken or imprisoned, or be disseised of his freehold, or liberties, or free customs, or be outlawed, or exiled, or any other wise destroyed; nor will We not pass upon him, nor condemn him, but by lawful judgment of his peers, or by the law of the land. We will sell to no man, we will not deny or defer to any man either justice or right."Magna Carta, 1297 version, chapter 29 (clauses 39 and 40 of 1215). Still in force. Source: legislation.gov.uk, 25 Edw 1 cc. 1-25
"Magna Charta is such a fellow, that he will have no sovereign."Sir Edward Coke, speech in the House of Commons, 17 May 1628, during the debates on the Petition of Right (public domain)
  1. Close reading. Who is protected? ("freeman": a minority in 1215.) Who makes the promise? Underline every negative: what does the accumulation suggest?
  2. Myth versus history. The charter was annulled by the Pope within weeks. It was reissued in 1216, 1217 and 1225. Its prestige comes later, in the 17th century, and in the American colonies. In 1628, Sir Edward Coke used it against Charles I: the charter, he said, "will have no sovereign".
  3. Mediation (C1 option). In French, 80 words: explain to a classmate in history why this clause is still quoted today, and why historians are cautious.

Key points

  • The clause establishes due process: the king himself is under "the law of the land".
  • It protected free men, not serfs or women; it was not democratic in the modern sense.
  • Its power is symbolic: it is invoked in the US Fifth Amendment and in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights debates.

Exercise 2.1 · Close reading

  1. How many negative words (no, not, nor) can you find in chapter 29?
  2. Find the words that mean: (a) deprived of his land; (b) declared outside the protection of the law; (c) to delay.
  3. Who is "We"? What is unusual about a king using this pronoun in a promise?
  4. Which two guarantees are given: one for the person, one for justice?

Answer key

1. Six: No, nor, not, nor, no, not. The accumulation shows how complete the protection is meant to be. 2. (a) disseised of his freehold; (b) outlawed; (c) defer. 3. The king himself (the royal "We"): the ruler binds himself by his own words. 4. No arrest or punishment without lawful judgment or the law of the land; justice cannot be sold, refused or delayed.

  1. Magna Carta gave the vote to the people of England.
  2. The Pope declared the charter void a few weeks after Runnymede.
  3. Clause 39 protected every person in England, including serfs.
  4. The charter was reissued several times in the 13th century.
  5. Lawyers in the 17th century used it against the king.
  6. King John signed Magna Carta with a pen.

Answer key

Question: How did a revolution without a revolution change who holds power?

"Whereas the late King James the Second by the Assistance of diverse evill Councellors Judges and Ministers imployed by him did endeavour to subvert and extirpate the Protestant Religion and the Lawes and Liberties of this Kingdome …"Bill of Rights 1689, preamble (original spelling). Source: legislation.gov.uk, 1 Will & Mar Sess 2 c 2
"That the pretended power of suspending of laws or the execution of laws by regall authority without consent of Parlyament is illegall." … "That the freedome of speech and debates or proceedings in Parlyament ought not to be impeached or questioned in any court or place out of Parlyament."Bill of Rights 1689, article 1 and article 9 (original spelling). Source: legislation.gov.uk, 1 Will & Mar Sess 2 c 2
  1. Context. James II, a Catholic king, is replaced by William and Mary. They accept the Bill of Rights as a condition of the throne. The preamble blames "evill Councellors", not the institution of monarchy.
  2. Analysis. Why does the text say "pretended" power? (The king never legally had it.) What does article 9 protect, and why does it still matter for MPs today?
  3. Link. Article 9 was at the heart of the 2019 prorogation case (session 6).

Model answer

The word "pretended" rewrites history: Parliament claims it is restoring old rights, not making a revolution. This strategy gives the change legitimacy. Article 9 protects parliamentary privilege: an MP cannot be sued for what they say in the Commons. It makes Parliament a space of free debate that no court controls.

Exercise 3.1 · How the text justifies itself

  1. In the preamble, who is blamed for the crisis: the monarchy, James II, or his advisers?
  2. What do the verbs "subvert" and "extirpate" mean, and what image do they create?
  3. Why is it clever to call the king's power "pretended" rather than "abolished"?

Answer key

1. James II, helped by "evill Councellors Judges and Ministers": the institution itself is spared, so William and Mary can inherit it. 2. To overthrow; to pull up by the roots. The image is of a king destroying the nation's liberties at the root. 3. "Abolished" would admit that the power existed and that Parliament is innovating; "pretended" says it never existed, so Parliament is only restoring the law.

Exercise 3.2 · Article 9 today

Is the person protected by article 9? Answer yes or no and explain.

  1. During a debate in the Commons, an MP accuses a company of hiding a safety report.
  2. The same MP repeats the accusation, word for word, in a television interview the next morning.
  3. A government asks a court to rule that a speech made in the Commons was illegal.

Answer key

1. Yes: words spoken in proceedings in Parliament cannot be questioned in any court. 2. No: the interview is a "place out of Parlyament", so the ordinary law (for example on defamation) applies. 3. The court would refuse: proceedings in Parliament "ought not to be impeached or questioned in any court".

Session 4 · The dignified Crown

TheorySpeaking
You already know (The King Reigns, session 5): Bagehot's two parts, summed up; the King reigns but does not rule; the Government writes the King's Speech. Now: Bagehot's own words, a harder sort in which some items belong to both columns, and a paradox to defend.

Question: If the King has no real power, what is he for?

The constitution has "first, those which excite and preserve the reverence of the population, the dignified parts … and next, the efficient parts, those by which it, in fact, works and rules." The sovereign has "three rights: the right to be consulted, the right to encourage, the right to warn."Walter Bagehot, The English Constitution, 1867, chapters 1 and 3 (public domain)
  1. Sort. Classify into dignified or efficient: the Coronation (Charles III, 6 May 2023), the King's Speech, Prime Minister's Questions, the weekly audience, Royal Assent, the Cabinet. In The King Reigns you sorted four simple items; here, justify every choice, and say which items belong to both columns.
  2. Discussion. Is "dignified" a compliment or a criticism? Is a symbolic head of state useful in a divided country?
  3. Speaking task. 2 minutes: "The monarchy is the most efficient part of the constitution precisely because it is dignified."

Elements for the paradox

Because the monarch is above party politics, the Crown can embody the nation in moments of crisis or mourning, while the elected government takes the blame for decisions. Its neutrality is a political resource. Counter-argument: it costs money, it is hereditary and so contradicts equality, and support among young people is lower than among older generations.

Exercise 4.1 · The harder sort

Dignified, efficient or both? Give one reason for each.

  1. The Coronation, 6 May 2023
  2. The King's Speech
  3. Prime Minister's Questions
  4. The weekly audience
  5. Royal Assent
  6. The Cabinet

Answer key

1. Dignified: ceremony, reverence, no decision. 2. Both: the King reads it (dignified) but the Government writes it and sets out its bills (efficient). 3. Efficient: the Prime Minister is held to account by MPs. 4. Both: a private ritual (dignified) where the King can be consulted, encourage and warn (Bagehot's three rights). 5. Both: a formality since 1708 (dignified), but without it a bill does not become law (efficient in form). 6. Efficient: it takes the decisions of government.

Exercise 4.2 · Consulted, encourage or warn?

Which of the three rights is used in each situation?

  1. The Prime Minister tells the King about a plan before it is announced.
  2. The King says privately that a new apprenticeship scheme is an excellent idea.
  3. The King asks whether a reform might divide the four nations, and suggests caution.

Answer key

1. The right to be consulted. 2. The right to encourage. 3. The right to warn. All three are used in private: the audience is confidential.

Session 6 · Courts versus government: prorogation, 2019

Court judgmentRole play
New ground. The King Reigns did not study the courts. Link with what you know: the King acts on the advice of the Prime Minister (The King Reigns, session 1), and article 9 of the Bill of Rights (session 3 here).

Question: Can judges stop a Prime Minister?

The decision to advise Her Majesty to prorogue Parliament "was unlawful because it had the effect of frustrating or preventing the ability of Parliament to carry out its constitutional functions without reasonable justification."R (Miller) v The Prime Minister; Cherry v Advocate General for Scotland [2019] UKSC 41, 24 September 2019, the Court's conclusion

Lesson 7 · The facts and the judgment

  1. Facts. In August 2019, Boris Johnson advised the Queen to suspend Parliament for five weeks before the Brexit deadline. The Supreme Court, unanimously (11 judges), declared the prorogation void. Parliament returned the next day.
  2. Discussion. Is this the courts protecting parliamentary sovereignty, or the courts entering politics? Link with Dicey (session 1) and article 9 (session 3).

Lesson 8 · The mock hearing

  1. Mock hearing (C1). Three groups: counsel for the government, counsel for the claimants, the judges. Each counsel speaks 3 minutes; the judges deliver a reasoned ruling. Each member of a group delivers at least one argument or one reason.

Arguments for each side

Government: prorogation is a prerogative power and a political matter; judges have no legitimacy to decide it. Claimants: if the government can silence Parliament, Parliament is not sovereign; the courts only defend a principle the constitution already contains. Ruling: the Court said it was not deciding when Brexit should happen, only protecting Parliament's role.

Question: Is the UK still one country with one Parliament?

The Lords

The House of Lords (Hereditary Peers) Act 2026 received Royal Assent on 18 March 2026. Since the end of the session on 29 April 2026, the House has been made up only of life peers and bishops (Lords Spiritual). Is an appointed chamber more legitimate than a hereditary one?

The Union

  • Scottish independence referendum, 18 September 2014: No 55.3 %, Yes 44.7 %.

  2. Comprehension, Document B (/5): list the main changes proposed and explain the reason the government gives.
  3. Mediation in French (/4): in 100 words, explain to your French parents why judges in the UK were able to cancel a decision of the Prime Minister.
  4. Essay (/6), 250 to 300 words: "In the United Kingdom, the people are sovereign in theory but Parliament is sovereign in practice." Discuss, using at least two examples from the sequence.

In two lessons: questions 1 to 3 in lesson 12, the essay in lesson 13.

Answer key

  1. The Prime Minister advised the Queen to prorogue Parliament for five weeks; the Court found it frustrated Parliament's ability to carry out its functions without reasonable justification; it protected parliamentary sovereignty and accountability of the government.
  2. Voting age lowered to 16 for all UK elections; registration from 14; data protection for young people. Reason: consistency with Scotland and Wales and participation of 1.7 million young people.
  3. Mediation is assessed on accuracy, selection of information and adaptation to the reader; no word-for-word translation.
  4. Expected ideas: Dicey's doctrine; referendums as advisory (Brexit); the paradox of "take back control"; the Scottish case and the 2022 ruling; votes at 16 decided by Parliament, not by referendum.

Lesson 14 · 52 min

Summative part 2 · Final task: "Who rules Britain?"

A step up from The King Reigns. There, the speech lasted 3 minutes and the op-ed 250 to 300 words on Magna Carta. Here, the speech is followed by 2 minutes of questions, the op-ed is longer and on a new question, and a third, C1 option is added.

Students choose one of three tasks. All three require using at least three primary sources from the sequence and answering one objection.

  1. Speech in the Commons (4 minutes + 2 minutes of questions). You are a newly elected MP. Speak for or against the Representation of the People Bill, or for or against a written constitution.
  2. Op-ed (350 to 400 words). For a British newspaper: "Britain needs a written constitution." Argue for or against.
  3. Mock Supreme Court ruling (C1, in pairs). Write and present a short judgment (300 words) on an invented case: a government suspends Parliament for three months during a national emergency.

In lesson 14, op-eds are handed in at the start; speeches and rulings are delivered (about seven per lesson); op-ed writers ask the questions. With a larger group, the remaining speeches open the next lessons, two per lesson.
