Lesson 1. Look Before You Judge

Goal: I can distinguish what I see from what I think, and explain one possible relationship between art, land and water.

Road map

Lesson of 55 minutes, timed for 52 minutes. Lesson 1 of 8. The remaining three minutes allow for arrival, equipment and departure.

  1. Recall: Canada, Ottawa, English and French. 0 to 4
  2. Read the cultural introduction and word box. 4 to 8
  3. Observe Jim Logan’s painting and complete worksheet A. 8 to 18
  4. Observe Morrisseau’s painting and complete worksheet B. 18 to 27
  5. Complete vocabulary and grammar practice. 27 to 34
  6. Protected paired speaking. 34 to 40
  7. Compare interpretations and correct one overstatement. 40 to 44
  8. Written record. 44 to 49
  9. Homework and exit check. 49 to 52
  • Written record. Copy the written record into your notebook.
  • Participation. Everyone speaks at least once: each student speaks for 45 seconds about both paintings, then answers the partner’s question.
  • Homework for next lesson. 60 to 80 words comparing the paintings, and two flashcards from today’s word box.

Stopping point. Minute 44. If necessary, the emergency exit replaces everything remaining from minute 44.

Emergency exit · 8 min. This eight-minute ending replaces unfinished work inside the 52 minutes; it is not added to them.
  • 44 to 46: both partners say one observation and one interpretation.
  • 46 to 49: copy the first three written-record sentences.
  • 49 to 51: record the homework.
  • 51 to 52: correct “might to show”.

Student text

Read

First Nations, Inuit and Métis are distinct Indigenous peoples recognised in Canada’s Constitution. These names do not describe one single culture.

Jim Logan and Norval Morrisseau offer different artistic perspectives. Logan’s painting shows a boy and a dog in a village. Morrisseau’s painting connects a loon, a fish and a sun through lines.

A painting can invite an interpretation without proving a geographical location or a social condition. We should say what we see before explaining what it might mean.

The artist’s own words

“It’s my way of communicating in a polite and respectful way the realities of poverty, of living within a hegemonic society and reconciling what ‘reconciliation’ is or if one can really ever achieve such [a thing].”

Jim Logan, quoted by the Canada Council Art Bank, July 2024.

Jim Logan, born in 1955 to a Métis family in Port Coquitlam, British Columbia, calls himself “a social statement artist”. These are his words about his own work: quote them and attribute them to him, rather than turning them into a general statement about every community.

Sources: Canada Council Art Bank, Dialogues with Jim Logan, July 2024 · Jim Logan, official website · Art Canada Institute, Norval Morrisseau, Style and Technique

Grammar exercise

Use is, might be or does not prove.

  1. The dog blue in the painting.
  2. The boy lonely.
  3. A painted village that the location is a reserve.
  4. The connection between the animals important.

Correct:

  1. “The lines might to show relationships.”

Protected spoken turn

Prepare for one minute.

Student A speaks for 45 seconds:

In Logan’s painting, I can see…
This might suggest…
In Morrisseau’s painting…
I would be careful not to claim…

Student B listens without interruption, then asks:

What in the painting supports your interpretation?

Swap roles.

A student working alone says the turn aloud and writes the question they would expect.

  • □ I gave a complete spoken answer.
  • □ I listened without interrupting.
  • □ I asked or answered a question.

Answer key

Worksheet A:

  • Boy and dog: observation.
  • Loneliness: interpretation, supported by the boy’s apparent isolation and the title.
  • Reserve location: unsupported assertion.
  • Bright colours: observation.

Worksheet B: sun, loon, fish; connecting lines; relationships or interdependence are possible interpretations, not compulsory meanings.

Vocabulary: 1c, 2d, 3a, 4b.

Grammar:

  1. is;
  2. might be;
  3. does not prove;
  4. might be;
  5. “The lines might show relationships.”

Written record

First Nations, Inuit and Métis are distinct Indigenous peoples. In art analysis, I distinguish observation from interpretation. Morrisseau’s connecting lines may suggest relationships between living beings. A painting alone does not prove a community’s location or living conditions.

Homework

Write 60 to 80 words comparing the paintings. Include two observations, two cautious interpretations and one question.

LVB support:

However…
The lines might…
