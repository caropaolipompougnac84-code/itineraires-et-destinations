Session 3. Magna Carta's long life: from England to the world

Key question: Why does a medieval charter still matter today?

Marked recap · 3 points

What do you remember from session 2? Answer alone in five minutes, then check.

1. Where and when was Magna Carta sealed?

2. Did King John sign it?

3. Rewrite in today's English: “We will sell to no man [...] either Justice or Right.”

Recap answers

  1. At Runnymede, on 15 June 1215.
  2. No, he sealed it.
  3. For example: Justice cannot be bought; nobody can be refused a fair trial or made to wait for it.

Aims

  • Follow the legacy of Magna Carta across eight centuries
  • Read short quotations from the US Bill of Rights and from Eleanor Roosevelt
  • Explain an idea to a French classmate in English (mediation)

Today's language goal

To explain how an old idea travelled through time, when the idea matters more than the people, we need the passive voice: be + past participle (was reissued, is still quoted).

Documents

Document A. What is left of Magna Carta in English law

“There are 63 clauses in Magna Carta. [...] Only four of the 63 clauses in Magna Carta are still valid today: 1 (part), thirteen, 39 and 40.”

“Magna Carta was not intended to be a great charter of rights for all people, but designed by the barons to ensure that their rights were protected against the king's power.”

Clause 1 protects the liberties of the English Church, clause thirteen those of the City of London, and clauses 39 and 40 the right to a lawful judgment and to justice without delay.

Source: UK Parliament, The contents of Magna Carta

Document B. Across the Atlantic: the Fifth Amendment (1791)

“No person shall [...] be deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law.”

The idea of the law of the land in Magna Carta became due process of law in the United States Constitution.

Source: National Archives (US), The Bill of Rights: a transcription

Document C. Eleanor Roosevelt, United Nations, 9 December 1948

Presenting the Universal Declaration of Human Rights to the UN General Assembly, Eleanor Roosevelt said that it “may well become the international Magna Carta for all men everywhere”.

Source: Roosevelt House, Eleanor Roosevelt and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights

Words in action

To say it precisely, we need the right words

EntryComplete with: legacy, clause, due process, deprived of, worldwide.

  1. Clause 39 is one ______ of Magna Carta.
  2. Nobody can be ______ liberty without a trial.
  3. The US speaks of ______ of law.
  4. The ______ of Magna Carta is still felt today.
  5. Its ideas became ______.

Answer key

1. clause. 2. deprived of. 3. due process. 4. legacy. 5. worldwide.

CoreMatch to make collocations: a lasting / a legal / due / to deprive someone + process, of liberty, legacy, clause.

Answer key

a lasting legacy, a legal clause, due process, to deprive someone of liberty.

StretchWrite one sentence on Magna Carta with legacy and worldwide.

Answer key

For example: The legacy of Magna Carta became worldwide when its ideas were taken up in the United States and at the United Nations.

To say it: The passive voice

To explain how an old idea travelled through time, when the idea matters more than the people

Passive = be (in the right tense) + past participle.

Past: The charter was reissued in 1225. Present: Clause 39 is still quoted today.

We use the passive when the action or the document matters more than who did it. Add by + agent only if it is useful: The charter was declared void by the Pope.

EntryChoose.

  1. Magna Carta (was reissued / reissued) in 1225.
  2. The name Magna Carta (was first used / first used) in 1217.
  3. Clauses 39 and 40 (are still part / still part) of the law.

Answer key

1. was reissued. 2. was first used. 3. are still part.

CoreRewrite in the passive.

  1. King John sealed the charter in 1215.
  2. The Pope declared the charter void.
  3. The US Constitution guaranteed due process in 1791.
  4. People still quote clause 39 today.

Answer key

1. The charter was sealed by King John in 1215. 2. The charter was declared void by the Pope. 3. Due process was guaranteed by the US Constitution in 1791. 4. Clause 39 is still quoted today (no agent: people adds nothing).

StretchIn which of the four sentences above is by + agent useless? Explain why.

Answer key

In sentence 4: people is vague, it gives no information. In sentences 1 to 3 the agent is important (the King, the Pope, the Constitution).

Figures of speech in the documents

A name used as a metaphor (antonomasia)

Eleanor Roosevelt: the Declaration “may well become the international Magna Carta for all men everywhere” (Document C)

Magna Carta, a proper name, becomes a common name for any founding text of rights. This figure is called antonomasia: one famous example stands for a whole category. The words all men everywhere add an effect of universality.

EntryWhat does Eleanor Roosevelt compare to Magna Carta?

Answer key

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights.

CoreWhy choose Magna Carta, and not another text, for this comparison in 1948?

Answer key

Because Magna Carta is old, famous and seen as the first limit put on power: the comparison gives the new declaration the same prestige.

StretchIs “for all men everywhere” really universal? Link with Document A.

Answer key

In 1215 the charter protected the barons, not everybody, and “men” leaves women out of the words: the formula announces a universal ambition that the history of Magna Carta did not have at first.

Model answer

Magna Carta was sealed in 1215 by King John. It was written to protect the barons, not everyone, but it was reissued several times and four of its clauses are still valid in English law. Its legacy is worldwide: the idea of a fair trial was taken up in the US Constitution in 1791, and in 1948 Eleanor Roosevelt compared the Universal Declaration of Human Rights to Magna Carta.

C1 extension

Eleanor Roosevelt said “for all men everywhere”. Is this formula as universal as it sounds? Compare it with what Document A says about 1215, in a paragraph of 120 words.

Model answer: for example the UK Parliament page The contents of Magna Carta. It explains that the charter has 63 clauses, that only four are still valid, and that clauses 39 and 40 inspired principles of English law that were exported to America and other English-speaking countries.
