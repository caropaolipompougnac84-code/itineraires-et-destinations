Whose Land, Whose Water?

When a government makes promises to Indigenous peoples, who decides what happens to the land and the water, and how can citizens check whether those promises are kept?

Road map

Eight lessons of 55 minutes, each timed for 52 minutes. The remaining three minutes allow for arrival, equipment and departure. The stopping point in every lesson is minute 44. Lesson 8 is the final task: a citizens’ hearing and an open letter.

  1. Lesson 1. Look Before You Judge 52 min
  2. Lesson 2. 1969: The White Paper and the Red Paper 52 min
  3. Lesson 3. Rivers into Reservoirs 52 min
  4. Lesson 4. Dams Today: Muskrat Falls and Site C 52 min
  5. Lesson 5. Pipelines on Unceded Land 52 min
  6. Lesson 6. Promises Kept, Promises Broken 52 min
  7. Lesson 7. The Grizzlies: Youth, Land and Hope 52 min
  8. Lesson 8. A Citizens’ Hearing and an Open Letter 52 min
  • Written record. Every lesson ends with a short written record that students copy and keep.
  • Participation. Everyone speaks at least once: protected individual turns in pairs or small groups, not only volunteers.
  • Homework for next lesson. A short written task and two student-made flashcards, set at the end of each lesson.

Stopping point. Minute 44 in every lesson.

Emergency exit · 8 min. If time runs short, an eight-minute emergency exit replaces everything remaining from minute 44, inside the 52 minutes; it is not added to them. Each lesson page gives its own exit, which still leaves every student with the written record, one spoken turn and the homework.

The film at home: The Grizzlies

The whole film The Grizzlies (Canada, 2018, directed by Miranda de Pencier, 1 h 42) is compulsory home viewing before lesson 7, set as homework in lesson 6. It opens up the voices students use in the debate and in the lesson 8 hearing. In class, lesson 7 shows only short extracts: the official trailer and three interview extracts. See the videos and the film, lesson 7 and the method The Sentence I Cannot Finish Alone.

Your mission

Central question. When a government makes promises to Indigenous peoples, who decides what happens to the land and the water, and how can citizens check whether those promises are kept?

Dams, pipelines and reservoirs bring electricity, jobs and revenue. Many of them stand on land and rivers where First Nations, Inuit and Métis peoples have lived for thousands of years. You will look at two paintings, listen to news reports, read official figures with their dates, and always ask: who says this, and what does the evidence show?

Final task, lesson 8. In groups of four, hold a citizens’ hearing on one case: the White Paper and the Red Paper, James Bay or a later dam, a pipeline, or drinking water. Every student presents sourced evidence, asks and answers a question, and makes a proportionate recommendation. Then each student writes an open letter to a Member of Parliament.

Cultural axes

Première générale. Axis 6, “English-speaking areas of the Americas”, associated with Axis 2, “Diversity and inclusion”. The enquiry into Indigenous political representation, collective rights, consultation and participation gives substance to that pairing. Canada is not only a setting: students examine relationships between Indigenous nations, Canadian institutions, English-language media and public debate.

The paintings also connect with Axis 3, “Art and power”, and the energy cases with Axes 4 and 5, “Scientific innovation and responsibility” and “Human beings and nature”. These are useful connections, not a claim that one sequence covers five axes.

Bac pro and CAP. Use the vocational programme’s social, civic, cultural and professional communication contexts: exhibition labels, briefings, meeting notes, quality-monitoring notes and a briefing email. The general-pathway axes do not govern the vocational programme.

How we work

  • Say what you see before you interpret. A painting does not prove where a village is or how people live there.
  • Attribute every viewpoint. First Nations, Inuit and Métis are distinct peoples, and people within one community can disagree. We report who says what; no student speaks in place of an Indigenous person or invents testimony.
  • Explain arguments, do not adopt them. When we study the 1969 White Paper, we explain the government’s argument and the answer of First Nations leaders; nobody is asked to defend assimilation as a personal position.
  • Listen first, read afterwards. Every video is heard once for the main message, then again for details, before the support text. Notes are labelled H (heard), R (read afterwards), I (inferred) or ? (unsure).
  • Everyone speaks. Each student has a protected individual turn in a pair or small group, then asks or answers a question.
  • Every figure has a source and a date. We check what a number counts before we use it.

Students are assessed on accurate attribution and qualification, not on whether they agree with one political conclusion.

Three central structures

Three production tools come back in every lesson:

  1. “The government promised to…”
  2. “The project was approved / the agreement was signed…”
  3. “However… We recommend…”

The past perfect, habitual would, complex causatives, should have, wish followed by the past perfect and several passives in one sentence are supported extensions, not minimum requirements. See the language page.

Figures with their dates. Drinking water: the Indigenous Services Canada snapshot dated 10 September 2026 records 158 long-term advisories lifted since November 2015, and 38 active advisories in 36 communities. These figures concern long-term advisories on public systems on reserves, not every water problem; new advisories can be added. Check the official page before class.

Trans Mountain: the November 2026 federal budget records an offer of a collective 15% ownership share to 128 Indigenous groups. An offer is not a completed transfer of ownership.

The 1969 White Paper was a policy proposal, withdrawn in 1970 after strong opposition, according to the Canadian Encyclopedia. The 1997 Delgamuukw decision explains Aboriginal title and the importance of oral histories; it is not a simple final award of the whole disputed territory. A figure in cubic metres per second measures a river’s discharge, not its speed.

Two paintings, reproduced with permission

Lesson 1 studies two paintings: Jim Logan, No One to Play with but his Dog, and Norval Morrisseau, Loon and Fish. They are reproduced with the permission of the artist and of the Norval Morrisseau Estate. Look first, describe, then interpret with care.
