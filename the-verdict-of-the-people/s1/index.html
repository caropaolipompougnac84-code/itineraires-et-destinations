Lesson 1. Reading the Verdict

How does Bingham represent popular power and exclusion?

Road map

Lesson of 55 minutes, timed for 52 minutes. Lesson 1 of 7. Minutes 52 to 55 stay free for collecting work, moving and the change of room.

  1. Paired opening: “An election can show people’s power because…” 4 min (minutes 0 to 4)
  2. Title, artist and final project 4 min (minutes 4 to 8)
  3. Silent observation, then Exercise 1A 7 min (minutes 8 to 15)
  4. Read Source A and complete Exercise 1B 8 min (minutes 15 to 23)
  5. Correction: visible detail or contextual knowledge? 7 min (minutes 23 to 30)
  6. Grammar: describe and qualify, Exercise 1C 6 min (minutes 30 to 36)
  7. Main production: evidence table and museum guide, Exercises 1D and 1E 8 min (minutes 36 to 44)
  8. Written record 4 min (minutes 44 to 48)
  9. Exit ticket: one observation and one interpretation 2 min (minutes 48 to 50)
  10. Explain and record homework 2 min (minutes 50 to 52)
  • Written record. Bingham represents an election result as a public event; we separate what we see, what a source tells us and what we interpret.
  • Participation. Everyone speaks at least once: Partner A speaks for about 20 seconds, Partner B answers for about 20 seconds, then they swap during the museum-guide challenge. A quiet exchange with a partner counts; a student who needs support may read a prepared sentence.
  • Homework for next lesson. Finish the 90 to 110-word museum label, make three flashcards, prepare one question about how election results affect government.

Stopping point. Minute 44, after the paired museum-guide description and the evidence table. Next lesson starts from the label.

Emergency exit · 8 min. If only eight working minutes remain, this replaces the unfinished plan; it is not added to it.
  1. Minutes 0 to 2: both partners state one observation.
  2. Minutes 2 to 5: write the written record, adding one spatial expression.
  3. Minutes 5 to 7: record and restate the homework.
  4. Minute 7 to 8: hand in one cautious interpretation on a slip of paper.

Objectives

Describe an image, separate observation from interpretation and support a cautious deduction.

Language: spatial expressions (in the foreground, in the background, on the left), present continuous, may, might, could and must.

Source A

The painting and its context

Reference. George Caleb Bingham, The Verdict of the People, 1854 to 1855, oil on canvas, Saint Louis Art Museum, accession 45:2001. Museum record.

Student text, newly written from the museum record.

Bingham was both a painter and a politician. In this painting, a crowd gathers around a courthouse to hear election results. The people react differently: some appear delighted, while others seem disappointed, confused or argumentative.

The painting belongs to a three-part Election Series. In narrative order, the works show campaigning, voting and the announcement of results. However, their production dates do not follow that order: the museum dates The County Election to 1852, before Stump Speaking (1853 to 1854) and The Verdict of the People (1854 to 1855).

The museum connects the women on the balcony and the enslaved African American figure in the left foreground with groups who had no voice in government at that time. It also describes the women as temperance (anti-alcohol) reformers. Their presence raises a question: who belongs to “the people” when some people cannot take part on equal terms?

Words beside the text

ExpressionAccessible explanation
narrative orderthe order of events in the story
political participationtaking part in political decisions
excludednot allowed to take part
interpretationan explanation of what something may mean

Teacher’s clarification: students need to understand that the three dates differ, but they do not need to memorise all three for the language assessment.

Exercise 1A

Observation, interpretation or context?

Label each statement. O: directly observable. I: an interpretation. C: contextual information that needs a source.

  1. A crowd occupies much of the painting.
  2. Bingham was also a politician.
  3. The variety of reactions suggests that elections divide opinion.
  4. Some figures stand above others.
  5. The painting was completed in the nineteenth century.
  6. The image celebrates democracy without any criticism.

Answer key

  1. O.
  2. C.
  3. I.
  4. O.
  5. C.
  6. I, and too absolute without further support.
Exercise 1B

Understanding the source

  1. What event brings the crowd together?
  2. Put the three stages in narrative order: results, campaign, vote.
  3. Why must we separate narrative order from painting dates?
  4. Which excluded groups does the museum identify?
  5. Explain the question raised by the word “people” in the title.

Answer key

  1. The announcement of election results.
  2. Campaign, vote, results.
  3. The scenes form a logical story, but the paintings were not produced in that order.
  4. Women and enslaved African Americans, in the historical setting discussed by the museum.
  5. The title suggests collective power, but the image and its context make us ask who was excluded from that collective.
Exercise 1C

Grammar: describe and qualify

Complete the sentences.

  1. In the foreground, several people together. (stand, present continuous)
  2. The disappointed-looking figure support the losing side. (express possibility)
  3. A man is lying on the ground. This mean that he is tired, but the posture alone does not prove it. (express possibility)
  4. The painting historical information, but it is not a photograph. (provide, present simple)
  5. Rewrite more cautiously: “The painter definitely wants us to admire every aspect of the election.”

Answer key

  1. are standing.
  2. may, might or could.
  3. may, might or could. Note: the museum record describes this man as “inebriated” (drunk). That is the museum’s interpretation, a piece of context (C), not something the posture alone proves.
  4. provides.
  5. Possible answer: “The painter may be celebrating democratic participation, although the image also invites questions about exclusion.”
Exercise 1D

Museum-guide challenge (pairs)

Partner A: describe two visible details, add one spatial expression, suggest one interpretation.

Partner B: say which statement is an interpretation and ask: “What detail supports that?”

Swap roles. Everyone speaks.

Answer key

Success criteria: two details that can really be seen; one spatial expression used correctly; the interpretation is introduced with may, might, could or suggests; Partner B’s question is answered with a detail.

Exercise 1E · main production

Build the evidence, then write the label

Complete the table with a detail of your choice.

Visible detailPossible interpretationLimit
People react differently.The result may divide the crowd.We cannot identify every person’s political preference.

Then write three sentences: one describing the detail, one interpreting it, one saying what the image alone cannot establish.

Common B1+ route: a 90 to 110-word museum label with two visual details, one contextual fact, one supported interpretation and one qualification.

Towards B2: explain how the title may create a tension between popular power and exclusion.

Answer key

Accept different details if the interpretation really follows from them. Possible answer: “Several figures appear to react differently to the announcement. This may suggest that the election creates both excitement and disappointment. However, the image alone cannot tell us exactly what every person thinks.”

Possible label: “The Verdict of the People was painted by George Caleb Bingham in 1854 to 1855. A large crowd gathers in front of a courthouse to hear election results. In the foreground, one man waves his hat while others look dejected or argue, so the event appears lively and divided. The painting can be read as a celebration of popular power. However, according to the museum, the women on the balcony and the enslaved man on the left had no voice in government at the time. The title therefore raises a question: who counts as “the people”?” (104 words)

Answer key

depict, onlooker, elated.

Written record

Bingham represents an election result as a public event with varied reactions. We must separate what we see, what a source tells us and what we interpret. The painting raises a central question: who is included in “the people”?

Exit ticket

Write one observation and one interpretation of the painting. Begin the second sentence with “This may suggest…”.

Homework

  1. Finish the 90 to 110-word museum label (Exercise 1E).
  2. Make three flashcards with the method on the flashcards page.
  3. Prepare one question about how election results affect government.

Estimated time: 15 minutes.
