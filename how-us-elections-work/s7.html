What we need to say

In this lesson, you need to say what can really happen on 3 November, and imagine different rules.

To talk about what is at stake, we need these words: midterms, seat, majority, governor, special election, redraw a map.

To say what can really happen, we need the first conditional; to imagine other rules, we need the second conditional: If the Democrats win 218 seats, they will control the House. If there were no midterms, the House would stay the same for four years.

Documents

Key document

Midterm elections take place halfway through a president's four-year term. On Tuesday 3 November 2026, Americans elect all 435 members of the House and 35 senators: 33 in regular elections, plus two special elections in Florida and Ohio to replace Marco Rubio, now Secretary of State, and JD Vance, now Vice President. Thirty-six states also elect a governor, and voters decide on ballot measures, local questions put to the voters. Before the vote, the Republicans control both chambers: in the House, 218 Republicans, 214 Democrats, one independent and two empty seats (official list of the Clerk, 1 October 2026); in the Senate, 53 Republicans and 47 Democrats and independents who vote with them. A party needs 218 seats for a majority in the House and 51 in the Senate; with 50 seats each, the Vice President breaks the tie. The new Congress meets on 3 January 2027. If the opposition wins a chamber, it can block bills and open investigations. In 2025 and 2026, several states redrew their district maps before the vote, for example Texas in 2025 and California, where voters approved Proposition 50 in November 2025.

Grammar to say it: the conditional (3), real or imaginary?

Why we need it

To say what can really happen, we need the first conditional; to imagine other rules, we need the second conditional: If the Democrats win 218 seats, they will control the House. If there were no midterms, the House would stay the same for four years.

Grammar point: board explanation

Teacher script
  1. Show the seat bars. Ask two questions: What will happen if the Democrats win 218 seats? What would happen if there were no midterms?
  2. Write both answers side by side and compare the verbs.
  3. Explain how to choose: can it really happen on 3 November? first conditional. Is it a dream or another rule? second conditional.
  4. Prepare the results lesson: past simple with a finished date (The House was won on 3 November) and present perfect for news linked to now (The results have just been announced).
  5. Write the time markers: yesterday, on 3 November (past simple); just, already, yet, since, for (present perfect).
  6. Students copy, then do the quick check.
Board: students copy

Real or imaginary? Both conditionals; present perfect and past simple

Rule
Real: If + present, will. Imaginary: If + past, would.
Form
If the Democrats win 218 seats, they will control the House.
If there were no midterms, the House would stay the same for four years.
Use
Real: what can happen on 3 November. Imaginary: other rules, dreams, debates.
Examples from the lesson
  • If the Senate is split 50 to 50, the Vice President will break the tie.
  • If every state used the same map rules, gerrymandering would be harder.
  • The results have just been announced. The House was won on 3 November.
Common mistakes
  • If the Democrats would win → If the Democrats win.
  • The results have been announced on 3 November. → The results were announced on 3 November (finished date).
  • Since two years → for two years; since 2024.
French and English
French depuis → for + duration (for two years) or since + starting point (since 2024), with the present perfect: Republicans have controlled the House since 2023.
Also on the board: Tenses: present perfect or past simple?
Past simple + finished time: yesterday, on 3 November, in 2022. Present perfect + link to now: just, already, yet, since, for: The results have not been announced yet.

Figures of speech: the blend word and political metaphors

Learn

Gerrymander is a blend word: Governor Gerry + salamander, because a district looked like a salamander and Elkanah Tisdale added a head, wings and claws. Commentators also use metaphors: the midterms are a referendum on the president; a close race is a toss-up (like a coin); a president who has lost power is a lame duck.

  1. Which two words make gerrymander?
  2. Why did the artist draw a monster?
  3. Why are the midterms called a referendum on the president?
  4. Where does toss-up come from?
  5. What is a lame duck?
  6. Invent a blend word for a school situation and explain it.
Answer key
  1. Gerry and salamander.
  2. To show that the district was unnatural and dangerous for democracy.
  3. Many voters use them to judge the president's first two years.
  4. From tossing a coin: heads or tails, nobody knows.
  5. An official who stays in office until a successor takes over, with less power.
  6. Example: brunch = breakfast + lunch; homeworkload = homework + workload.

Text analysis: a pattern in midterm historyGoing further

Midterm elections have often been difficult for the party in the White House. In 2010, two years after Barack Obama's victory, Republicans won control of the House. In 2018, under Donald Trump's first presidency, Democrats took it back. In 2022, during Joe Biden's term, Republicans regained the House. Political scientists suggest several reasons: voters who are unhappy are more likely to turn out, and the president's supporters may feel less urgency once their candidate is in office.

  1. Find the three examples and say who won the House each time.
  2. Which tense is used in the first sentence, and why?
  3. Find a word meaning took back.
  4. Give the two reasons suggested.
  5. Is this pattern a rule? Justify with a word from the text.
Answer key
  1. 2010: Republicans (under Obama); 2018: Democrats (under Trump); 2022: Republicans (under Biden).
  2. Present perfect: a general pattern up to now.
  3. regained.
  4. Unhappy voters vote more; the president's supporters feel less urgency.
  5. No: often and suggest show a tendency, not a rule.
