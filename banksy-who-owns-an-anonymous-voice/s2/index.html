Lesson 2. Should the public know who Banksy is?

Question of the day: When does reporting an identity serve the public, and when does it intrude on a private life?

For the teacher: timing (52 minutes)
TimeActivity
00 to 05Flashcard retrieval from Lesson 1
05 to 12Read the source cards, silently and then in pairs
12 to 20Fact, claim, rumour and interpretation: sorting
20 to 28Attribution grammar and exercises
28 to 37Newsroom ethics role-play, A2 and B1 pathways
37 to 42Paired oral verdict
42 to 44Two new flashcards
44 to 49Written record: a corrected headline
49 to 52Exit question and homework

Checkpoint at minute 44. Every student can say who made the identity claim, and can avoid presenting it as Banksy's own confirmation.

Eight-minute essential closing route (replaces the last eight minutes if the class needs more time; it does not add time): 44 to 46: correct an overconfident headline. 46 to 49: each student gives a 20-second ethical judgement. 49 to 51: copy the accurate reporting sentence. 51 to 52: note homework.

A2 outcome

Identify the source of a statement and distinguish a press report from a personal confirmation.

B1 outcome

Evaluate evidence, keep attribution accurate, and defend a position on the ethics of publication.

Step 1. Retrieval

In pairs, use yesterday's cards. One student says a word or a structure; the other gives the example sentence. Then swap.

Step 2. Source cards

These cards are educational paraphrases, not copied articles. They exist so that you can practise a distinction. Read them carefully. Then, for each card, write: is this a documented fact, an attributed claim, a rumour, or an interpretation?

Card A. Reuters, 13 March 2026

Reuters published an investigation by Simon Gardner, James Pearson and Blake Morrison. The investigation reports that Banksy was born Robin Gunningham and later used the name David Jones. Reuters presents this as the conclusion of its own enquiry, and says it used police and court documents, interviews and travel information. Reuters is a named source, making a strong claim. It is not the same thing as a confirmation by the artist.

Source: reuters.com/investigates/special-report/global-art-banksy/, filed 13 March 2026, 10 a.m. GMT.

Card B. What Banksy and those around the artist said, as reported by Reuters

According to Reuters, Pest Control, the artist's official contact, said the artist had decided not to comment. A lawyer, Mark Stephens, challenged the accuracy of many details. He did not, in the reporting consulted for this sequence, confirm or deny the proposed identity. An absence of confirmation is not a denial. A challenge to details is not a confirmation either.

Card C. NPR, 22 March 2026

NPR interviewed Simon Gardner about the investigation. The presenter stated clearly that NPR had not independently confirmed Reuters' findings. This is useful: a second organisation can report that a first organisation has made a claim, without adopting the claim as its own established fact.

Source: NPR, 22 March 2026.

Card D. The Mail on Sunday, July 2008

Eighteen years earlier, the Mail on Sunday had already put forward the name Robin Gunningham, after what it described as a long investigation. The paper itself hedged: it presented a photograph of a man "believed to be Banksy". Banksy did not confirm the identification. In class, this is an earlier attributed claim, not a proof that Reuters must be right, and not a proof that Reuters must be wrong.

Card E. A genuine case of misidentification, 19 March 2026

After the Reuters investigation, some outlets and social-media posts circulated photographs of a London resident, George Georgiou, as if he were Banksy. Reuters published a fact-check: those photographs were not of the person Reuters had written about, and Georgiou is not Robin Gunningham. This shows how a story about identity can create a new error. We do not reproduce that person's photograph in this sequence.

Source: Reuters Fact Check, 19 March 2026.

Step 3. Four useful categories

CategoryWorking meaningExample from this lesson
Documented factAn event or a publication supported by the source under examinationReuters published its investigation on 13 March 2026.
Attributed claimA conclusion clearly assigned to a named sourceAccording to Reuters, Banksy was born Robin Gunningham.
RumourAn allegation circulating without adequate supporting evidence in the material availableAn unnamed account on social media says a local stranger is Banksy.
Interpretation or opinionA judgement about meaning, value, or what should happenBanksy's anonymity makes the work more powerful.

A claim can be well supported. Calling something a claim does not mean it is false. Reuters presents documentary evidence; students may discuss how strong that evidence seems, without turning the name into a classroom fact, and without flattening a documented investigation into "just a rumour".

Exercise 1. Sort these statements. Write F (documented fact), C (attributed claim), R (rumour) or I (interpretation).

  1. Reuters published an investigation on 13 March 2026.
  2. Banksy's real name is Robin Gunningham.
  3. According to Reuters, Banksy later used the name David Jones.
  4. NPR said it had not independently confirmed the findings.
  5. Anonymity is the only reason Banksy is famous.
  6. A classmate heard that a teacher in Bristol is Banksy, but has no source.
  7. The Mail on Sunday put the name Gunningham forward in 2008.
  8. Pest Control confirmed the Reuters identification. (Careful.)

Corrigé

1 F (the publication and its date are documented). 2 This sentence is not acceptable in this sequence: it turns a claim into a fact, with no attribution. The accurate form is a C: According to Reuters, Banksy was born Robin Gunningham. 3 C. 4 F (what NPR stated, on a dated broadcast). 5 I (a judgement, and a risky one: fame has more than one cause). 6 R (no adequate source). 7 F (the paper did publish that claim in 2008; the identity itself remains a claim). 8 This is false, and it is not a fair claim either. According to Reuters, Pest Control said the artist had decided not to comment. An absence of comment is not a confirmation.

Step 4. Expanding your vocabulary

Word or phraseMeaningExample
according toused to show the source of a piece of informationAccording to Reuters, the artist later used another name.
to report thatto give information as news, as a journalist doesReuters reported that it had examined court documents.
to confirmto say officially that something is trueBanksy did not confirm the identification.
to denyto say that something is not trueA refusal to comment is not a denial.
to allegeto say that something is true, without proving itThe paper alleged that it had identified the artist.
investigationa careful enquiry that looks for evidenceThe investigation was published in March 2026.
privacythe right to keep some parts of life out of public viewSome people argue that a private name should stay private.
public interesta reason for publishing something because society may need to know itThe newsroom must decide whether publication is in the public interest.
to attributeto assign a statement or a work to a source or a personWe attribute the claim to Reuters, not to Banksy.
to qualify (a claim)to limit a statement so that it stays accurateYou can qualify a headline by adding according to.

Grammar of the lesson

  • according to + source: According to Reuters, the artist was born Robin Gunningham.
  • X reported that + clause: Reuters reported that it had used court documents.
  • may / might for a possibility we do not treat as certain: The documents may support the claim. They might still be incomplete.
  • A useful contrast: did not confirm is not the same as denied. The artist did not confirm the name. The artist did not deny it either, in the sources we have.

Exercise 2. Correct the headlines. Each headline is too sure of itself. Rewrite it so that it is accurate.

  1. BANKSY'S REAL NAME REVEALED: ROBIN GUNNINGHAM.
  2. PEST CONTROL CONFIRMS THE STORY.
  3. NPR PROVES REUTERS RIGHT.
  4. BANKSY DENIES EVERYTHING.

Corrigé

Example rewrites. Other accurate versions are possible. 1. Reuters reports that Banksy was born Robin Gunningham. / According to a Reuters investigation, Banksy was born Robin Gunningham. 2. Pest Control said the artist had decided not to comment. 3. NPR said it had not independently confirmed Reuters' findings. 4. In the sources examined for this sequence, Banksy did not confirm the identification, and did not issue a personal denial either. A lawyer challenged many details without confirming or denying the name.

Exercise 3. Complete with according to, reported that, may, did not confirm, or denied.

  1. _______ Reuters, the artist later used the name David Jones.
  2. The Mail on Sunday _______ a man in its photographs was "believed to be Banksy".
  3. NPR _______ it had not independently confirmed the findings.
  4. Banksy _______ the identification in the sources we have.
  5. A well-supported claim _______ still be debated. It is not automatically a classroom fact.

Corrigé

1 According to. 2 reported that (or alleged that). 3 reported that / stated that. 4 did not confirm. 5 may.

Step 5. Newsroom role-play

You work on the news desk of a school magazine. A major agency has just published an investigation into Banksy's civil identity. You must decide what to print, and how to print it. You are not asked to find a private address or to contact a private person.

A2 pathway

In pairs: one student is a reporter, the other is the editor. The reporter has 40 seconds to say what the agency reported, using according to. The editor has 40 seconds to decide: print the name with a cautious headline, print the story without the civil name, or wait. Use I think we should... because...

B1 pathway

In groups of three: reporter, editor, and a lawyer for the magazine. In three minutes, reach a decision. You must: attribute the claim; mention that Banksy did not confirm it; mention NPR's caution; and answer this objection: 'The public has a right to know everything about a famous person.' Use although, may and public interest.

Step 6. Oral verdict

A2 pathway

Give a 20-second answer to the question of the day. Frame: I think the public should / should not know, because... According to... Banksy did not confirm...

B1 pathway

Give a 45-second answer. Include one argument for publication, one argument for privacy, and your own qualified decision. Do not present the civil name as a fact.

Step 7. Written record and flashcards

Write the accurate classroom sentence in your exercise book:

Reuters reported in March 2026 that Banksy was born Robin Gunningham and later took the name David Jones. Banksy did not confirm that identification in the sources examined for this sequence.

Then write two flashcards: according to and did not confirm. Homework will reuse them.

A2 pathway

Copy the classroom sentence. Then rewrite headline 1 from exercise 2 in your own words.

B1 pathway

Copy the classroom sentence. Then write a six-sentence news brief for the school magazine, with accurate attribution and a final ethical judgement.

Step 8. Homework

A2: record yourself (on a school-approved device, or read aloud at home) reading your corrected headline and one reason, in about 20 seconds. B1: write a short comment (80 to 100 words) on this statement: "If you choose to be famous, you lose the right to privacy." Use according to, although and may. Do not try to discover a private address.
