Expanding your vocabulary: student-made flashcards

There is no ready-made deck. You make your own cards; the vocabulary tables in each lesson are selection banks, not finished packs to copy.

The method

Step 1. Choose a useful difficulty

Choose a word, chunk or distinction that you misunderstood, could recognise but not use, or need for the final discussion. Examples: override a veto, whereas, called versus certified.

Step 2. Write a retrieval prompt on the front

The front must make you find something. Weak front: “Congress = Congress.” Better front: “Complete: Congress can a presidential veto.”

Step 3. Build the back

  1. the target word or chunk;
  2. a short definition in English;
  3. your own accurate example;
  4. a pronunciation or stress cue if helpful;
  5. the source or lesson;
  6. a warning against one common error.

Step 4. Test before turning

Look at the front. Say the answer. Write or spell the difficult part. Use it in a sentence. Only then turn the card.

Step 5. Sort and review

Three piles: not yet secure, correct with effort, easy today. Suggested review: the next day, three days later, one week later. Move cards according to your results; do not keep reviewing only the easy ones.

Step 6. Improve the card

If you fail a card again and again, shorten the prompt, add a useful contrast or replace an unclear example.

One distinction card per lesson

At least one card per lesson should test a relationship, not just a definition:

  • Lesson 1: description versus interpretation;
  • Lesson 2: bill versus law;
  • Lesson 3: citizen versus resident;
  • Lesson 4: promise versus outcome;
  • Lesson 5: media call versus certification;
  • Lesson 6: fact versus possible consequence;
  • Lesson 7: outgoing versus incoming Congress.

Complete: Congress can a presidential veto.override. Meaning: overcome the veto through the votes required by the Constitution. Example: A veto can be overridden by Congress. Lesson 2. Warning: a simple seat majority is not the veto-override threshold (two thirds in each chamber).
Link the contrast: Washington, D.C. has electors, Puerto Rico does not.whereas. Example: Washington, D.C. has presidential electors, whereas Puerto Rico does not. Lesson 3.
Does “a news organisation called the race” mean “the result has been legally certified”?No. A media call and legal certification are different acts. Example: The race has been called, but we have not established certification. Lesson 5.
Make the claim cautious: “The election will determine every trade outcome.”The election may affect trade debates, but it will not by itself determine every outcome. Lesson 6.

Flashcard quality check

  • Does the front test retrieval?
  • Is the answer accurate?
  • Does the example make sense?
  • Have I avoided putting the full answer on the front?
  • Can I use the item in a new sentence?
