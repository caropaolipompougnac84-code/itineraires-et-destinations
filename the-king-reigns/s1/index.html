Session 1. Who's in charge? The King, the Prime Minister and Parliament

Key question: Who has the real power in the United Kingdom: the King, the Prime Minister or Parliament?

Marked recap · 3 points

This first recap checks what you already know. It is marked, but every answer counts as a starting point.

1. Who is the King of the United Kingdom today?

2. In which city does the UK Parliament sit?

3. Name one thing you associate with British politics.

Recap answers

  1. Charles III, King since 8 September 2022.
  2. In London, at the Palace of Westminster, on the River Thames.
  3. Any accurate answer: Brexit, the Prime Minister, 10 Downing Street, the House of Commons, elections, the King's Speech.

Aims

  • Name the three main actors of British politics and say what each one does
  • Describe a painting of Westminster and link it to power
  • Use can / cannot and has to to talk about powers

Today's language goal

To explain who can do what in British politics, we need modals of power (can, cannot, has to, must) and the present simple for facts.

Documents

Document A. The King and the Prime Minister

“The UK has a constitutional monarchy, meaning that whilst The King is the Head of State, he remains politically impartial. The ability to make and pass legislation resides with an elected Parliament.”

“As Sovereign, The King holds a weekly Audience with the Prime Minister to discuss Government matters. The Monarch remains politically neutral on all matters, but he is able to ‘advise and warn’ his ministers, including his Prime Minister, when necessary. These Audiences are entirely private.”

Source: The Royal Family, The Sovereign and the Prime Minister

Document B. A week in the House of Commons

“The House will sit from 11.30am, starting with Wales questions. Prime Minister’s Questions will take place at 12 midday.” (Wednesday 4 November 2026)

Every Wednesday, the Prime Minister stands in the House of Commons for about half an hour and answers questions from MPs, starting with the Leader of the Opposition.

Source: UK Parliament, Coming up in the Commons, 2 to 4 November 2026

Then and now. In 1903 Monet painted the new Palace of Westminster as a dark silhouette against the sunset. More than a century later the same towers still stand for Parliament, and journalists still say "Westminster" when they mean the MPs inside.

Look: The painting is a small landmark, not the lesson: in two sentences, say which building you see and why a painter might choose it as a symbol of Britain.

Key words

WordMeaning
Head of Statethe person who represents the country (in the UK, the King)
constitutional monarchya system where the monarch's powers are limited by rules and by Parliament

Parliamentthe place where laws are made: the House of Commons, the House of Lords and the King

Activities

  1. Pair work: read Document A and underline what the King can do, then circle what he cannot do.
  2. Read Document B: who answers the questions, who asks them, and how often?
  3. Fill the class grid: King / Prime Minister / Parliament, with one power for each. Language: To fill the grid, use the tools below: The King can ..., the Prime Minister has to ..., MPs can ...
  4. Summary sentence, said aloud by three students: In the UK, the King reigns, but ...

Exercise: Who does what?

Match each action with the King, the Prime Minister or MPs.

  1. answers questions every Wednesday at noon
  2. meets the Prime Minister every week in private
  3. are elected by the people
  4. advises and warns, but stays neutral
  5. vote on new laws
  6. leads the Government

Answer key

1. The Prime Minister. 2. The King. 3. MPs. 4. The King. 5. MPs (in Parliament). 6. The Prime Minister.

Words in action

To say it precisely, we need the right words

EntryComplete with a word from the key words: Head of State, impartial, Parliament, MP, to appoint.

  1. The King is the ______ of the United Kingdom.
  2. An ______ is elected to sit in the House of Commons.
  3. The King stays ______: he does not take sides.
  4. ______ makes the laws.

Answer key

1. Head of State. 2. MP. 3. impartial. 4. Parliament.

CoreWord families. Give the noun: to appoint, to elect, to govern, impartial.

Answer key

appointment, election, government, impartiality.

StretchDocument A uses two words for the same idea: find them, then write one sentence about the King with each.

Answer key

impartial and neutral. For example: The King remains impartial in elections. He stays neutral on all political matters.

To say it: Modals of power

To explain who can do what in British politics

can + verb: a power or a permission. The King can advise and warn.

cannot (can't) + verb: something impossible or not allowed. The King cannot give orders.

has to / must + verb: an obligation. The Prime Minister has to answer MPs' questions. Must often shows a strong rule or the speaker's opinion.

Careful: no to after can and must (can to), and no -s: he can, he must, but he has to.

EntryChoose the right modal.

  1. The King (can / cannot) give orders to the Prime Minister.
  2. The Prime Minister (has to / cannot) answer questions on Wednesdays.
  3. MPs (can / cannot) vote on new laws.

Answer key

1. cannot. 2. has to. 3. can.

CoreRewrite each sentence with a modal.

  1. The King is able to advise and warn his ministers.
  2. It is impossible for the King to take sides.
  3. It is compulsory for the Prime Minister to keep the confidence of the House of Commons.

Answer key

1. The King can advise and warn his ministers. 2. The King cannot take sides. 3. The Prime Minister has to (must) keep the confidence of the House of Commons.

StretchWrite two sentences that contrast the powers of the King and of the Prime Minister. Use two different modals and whereas.

Answer key

For example: The King can warn the Prime Minister in private, whereas the Prime Minister has to defend his choices in public every Wednesday.

Figures of speech in the documents

Metonymy: a building for the people inside

“The House will sit from 11.30am” (Document B)

The House does not sit: the MPs do. Using a place for the people who work there is called metonymy. Journalists say Westminster for Parliament and Downing Street for the Prime Minister and his team.

EntryWho sits in “The House will sit”?

Answer key

The MPs, the members of the House of Commons.

CoreSay who is really speaking or acting: “Downing Street said no.” “Westminster voted the law.”

Answer key

Downing Street = the Prime Minister and his team. Westminster = Parliament, the MPs and the Lords.

StretchLook at Monet's painting: why can a building become a symbol of power, in a painting as in a newspaper?

Answer key

A building is visible, stable and easy to recognise: it stands for an institution that we cannot see. Monet paints Westminster as a landmark of Britain; journalists use its name for the people who decide there.

Expression task

Now say it

Oral. A younger student asks you: “Who's in charge in Britain?” Answer in 45 seconds.

EntrySay three sentences, one for each actor, with one modal in each.

CoreSpeak for 45 seconds. Use at least four modals and one metonymy (Westminster or Downing Street).

StretchSpeak for one minute and finish with a nuanced answer to the key question, using whereas.

Tools to use:

  • can / cannot / has to
  • Head of State, Parliament, MP, impartial
  • one metonymy

Model answer

The King is the Head of State, but he cannot give orders: he can only advise and warn in private. The Prime Minister leads the Government and has to answer MPs' questions every Wednesday. MPs are elected and they can vote on new laws. So Westminster makes the laws and Downing Street governs, whereas the King represents the country.

C1 extension

Find the two adjectives royal.uk uses for the King (impartial, neutral). Explain in five lines why neutrality might be the price a monarchy pays to survive in a democracy.

Homework

Learn the six key words. Then write three sentences beginning with: I think the real power belongs to ... because ...

Write it in your planner before the bell. It is also posted on the school platform.

Model answer

Model answer: I think the real power belongs to the Prime Minister because he leads the Government. I think Parliament also has power because it makes the laws and MPs are elected. The King is important as a symbol, but he does not take political decisions.
