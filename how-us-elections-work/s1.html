Question: Who can vote in the United States, and who does what in Washington?

Objectives

  • Name the three branches of the American government
  • Say who can vote today

What we need to say

In this lesson, you need to explain who does what in Washington, and who is given the vote.

To talk about voting, we need these words: citizen, vote, ballot, register, branch.

To say what is done, and by whom, we need the passive: The laws are made by Congress. The vote is given to citizens at eighteen.

Documents

Key document

The government of the United States has three branches. Congress makes the laws. The President carries out the laws and leads the country. The Supreme Court decides what the Constitution means. Today, you can vote in a federal election if you are an American citizen, if you are eighteen on Election Day and, in almost every state, if you are registered on the list of voters. This was not always the case: the 15th Amendment (1870) says the right to vote cannot be refused because of race, the 19th Amendment (1920) gives the vote to women and the 26th Amendment (1971) lowers the voting age to eighteen.

Grammar to say it: the passive voice (1), the form

Why we need it

To say what is done, and by whom, we need the passive: The laws are made by Congress. The vote is given to citizens at eighteen.

Grammar point: board explanation

Teacher script
  1. Show the diagram of the three branches. Ask: Who makes the laws? Students answer: Congress makes the laws.
  2. Write the active sentence on the board. Ask: What is more important here, Congress or the laws? Then write the passive sentence under it.
  3. Circle are + made. Explain: passive = be + past participle. Be shows the tense, the participle shows the action.
  4. Ask students to find the agent after by. Explain that we often leave it out when it is obvious.
  5. Explain the present simple: we use it for facts and rules that are always true. Underline the time markers: every four years, always, today.
  6. Students copy the board. Then do the quick check together, orally, before the exercises.
Board: students copy

The passive voice (1) and the present simple for facts

Rule
Passive = be + past participle. The object of the active sentence becomes the subject.
Form
Present simple passive: am / is / are + past participle (+ by + agent).
Regular: elect → elected. Irregular: make → made, choose → chosen, give → given, hold → held, put → put.
Use
When the action or the result matters more than the person who acts; in rules, institutions and news. The present simple is used for facts that are always true.
Examples from the lesson
  • Congress makes the laws. → The laws are made by Congress.
  • The president is elected every four years.
  • The right to vote is protected by the Constitution.
Common mistakes
  • The laws are make by Congress. → The laws are made by Congress.
  • The president elected every four years. → The president is elected every four years.
  • The laws is made. → The laws are made (plural subject).
French and English
French often uses on: On élit le président tous les quatre ans. English prefers the passive: The president is elected every four years. Par = by.
Also on the board: Tenses and time markers: the present simple
Facts and habits: Americans vote in November. Third person singular: -s: Congress makes, the president signs. Time markers: every, always, usually, today, in November.

Figures of speech: the symbol and the metaphor

Learn

A symbol is a picture that stands for an idea. A metaphor describes one thing as if it were another. In The Awakening (1915), the light of the torch stands for the vote. When we say women won the vote, the vote becomes a prize or a battle.

  1. In the 1915 cartoon, what does the light stand for?
  2. What do the dark states stand for?
  3. Find the metaphor: The vote was a long battle for women.
  4. Which verb turns the vote into a prize: won or lost?
  5. In The First Vote, the voters stand in a line: what does the long queue suggest?
  6. Invent your own metaphor: The ballot is a ______ because ______.
Answer key
  1. The vote for women.
  2. The states where women could not vote yet.
  3. a long battle: the struggle for the vote is described as a war.
  4. won.
  5. Many people are waiting for this new right; it is a long and important moment.
  6. Example: The ballot is a key because it opens the door to decisions.

Finer grammar: the passive with modals and perfect tensesGoing further

Rule

Modal + be + past participle: The vote cannot be refused because of race. Present perfect: has / have been + past participle: The voting age has been lowered to eighteen.

  1. The vote / cannot / refuse / because of race (15th Amendment).
  2. The voting age / have / lower / to eighteen (since 1971).
  3. Women / have / give / the vote in every state (since 1920).
  4. Voters / must / register / before Election Day (most states).
  5. The Constitution / can / amend / by Congress and the states.
Answer key
  1. The vote cannot be refused because of race.
  2. The voting age has been lowered to eighteen.
  3. Women have been given the vote in every state.
  4. Voters must be registered before Election Day.
  5. The Constitution can be amended by Congress and the states.

Text analysis: a right won step by stepGoing further

When the Constitution was written in 1787, it said very little about who could vote: each state decided. Over almost two centuries, the right was widened by amendments. In 1870, the 15th Amendment stated that the vote could not be denied because of race, yet many Black citizens were kept from the polls for decades by local rules. In 1920, the 19th Amendment extended the vote to women in every state. In 1971, the 26th Amendment lowered the voting age to eighteen, at a time when young Americans were being sent to fight in Vietnam.

  1. Find three passive forms and give their tense.
  2. What does widened mean here? Give a synonym.
  3. Which word shows a contrast in the fourth sentence?
  4. Why was the voting age lowered in 1971, according to the text?
  5. In your own words, explain the title.
Answer key
  1. was written (past), was widened (past), could not be denied (modal past), were kept (past), were being sent (past continuous): any three.
  2. made larger, extended.
  3. yet.
  4. Young people were sent to fight in Vietnam but could not vote: it seemed unfair.
  5. The right to vote did not arrive all at once: it was extended in several stages, by amendments.

Argued task (200 to 250 words, or a two-minute oral)Going further

Should the voting age be lowered to sixteen, in the United States or in France? Give arguments for and against, then your opinion.

Steps

  1. Introduction: the question and today's rule (eighteen).
  2. Arguments for, with examples.
  3. Arguments against.
  4. Your opinion, with a strong conclusion.
Sentence starters
  • It is often argued that ...
  • On the one hand, ...
  • On the other hand, ...
  • Some would say that ..., yet ...
  • All things considered, I believe that ...
Key points

Key points: today the voting age is eighteen in both countries; for: young people pay taxes, work, are affected by decisions on climate or education, and some countries already allow voting at sixteen; against: maturity, influence of parents and social media; a clear personal conclusion. Use at least two passives (the age was lowered, it is often argued) and linking words.
