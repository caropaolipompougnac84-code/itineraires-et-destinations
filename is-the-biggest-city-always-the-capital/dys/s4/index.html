Lesson 4. Capitals by compromise

Become an expert on one Commonwealth capital and explain it to others (mediation).

Road map

Lesson of 55 minutes, timed for 52 minutes. 7 lessons, plus a final-task assessment.

  1. Recap of lesson 3 4 min
  2. Jigsaw: how it works, then expert groups 20 min
  3. Home groups: each expert explains in one minute 18 min
  4. Whole class: what do the stories have in common? 10 min
  • Written record. In many Commonwealth countries, the capital is not the biggest city. It is often a compromise between rival cities or regions, or a central, sometimes new, city.
  • Participation. Everyone speaks at least once: each student, as an expert, explains one country in one minute, with five key words, without reading the card aloud.
  • Homework for next lesson. Finish the grid. Then record a one-minute audio message for a student who was absent: explain the story of your expert country, with three key dates and one comparison.

Stopping point. After the home groups, keep the ten-minute whole-class comparison even if it is reduced to three shared points on the board.

Emergency exit · 8 min. If time runs short at this stopping point, condense the end of the lesson inside the 52 minutes (this is not 52 plus 8). Dictate a short written record (two sentences). A quick round so that everyone speaks at least once (one sentence each). Write the homework on the board.

Recap · 4 min

  1. Why was the US capital built on the Potomac River?
  2. Put in the passive: They chose the site in 1791.
  3. How can you get to Juneau?

Answer key

  1. Because of the deal of 1790 between the North and the South.
  2. The site was chosen in 1791.
  3. By plane or by boat: there is no road from the rest of North America.

Jigsaw: how it works

  • Expert groups. Each group reads one country card below and fills its line of the grid. Keep only five key words on a card.
  • Home groups. New groups of six, one expert per country. Each expert explains the case in one minute, without reading, with the five key words. The others complete the grid.
  • Whole class. What do the six stories have in common?

Mediation starters. In my country, the capital is…, whereas the biggest city is… · It was chosen in… by… · The main reason was that… · In a nutshell, it was a compromise between… and…

Expert card: Canada: a Queen chooses a timber town

In the 1850s, the Province of Canada had no permanent capital. Parliament moved between Toronto, Kingston, Montreal and Quebec City, and every city wanted to keep it. In the end, the politicians asked Queen Victoria to decide.

On 31 December 1857, she chose Ottawa, a small timber town on the Ottawa River. The choice was a compromise: Ottawa stood on the border between Canada West, where most people spoke English, and Canada East, where most people spoke French. It was also further from the border with the United States, which made it easier to defend.

Today Toronto is by far the biggest city in Canada, but Parliament Hill, above the Ottawa River, is the heart of political life. The trap exists in the provinces too: the capital of British Columbia is Victoria, not Vancouver, and the capital of Quebec is Quebec City, not Montreal.

Expert card: Australia: a capital between two rivals

On 1 January 1901, six British colonies became one country, the Commonwealth of Australia. But which city would be the capital? Sydney and Melbourne, the two biggest cities, were rivals, and neither wanted the other one to win.

The Constitution found a compromise: the capital had to be in New South Wales, the state of Sydney, but at least 100 miles (about 160 km) away from Sydney. Until then, Parliament would sit in Melbourne. The first Parliament opened in Melbourne on 9 May 1901.

The site of Canberra was chosen in 1908, and the city was named on 12 March 1913. It was designed by Walter Burley Griffin, an American architect from Chicago, with his wife Marion Mahony Griffin. Parliament finally met in Canberra on 9 May 1927.

Expert card: New Zealand: chosen by three Australians

From 1841, the capital of New Zealand was Auckland, in the north of the North Island. For politicians from the South Island, the trip to Parliament meant a long and sometimes dangerous voyage by sea.

In 1863, Parliament decided to move the seat of government to a central place on the shores of Cook Strait, the stretch of sea between the two islands. To avoid local quarrels, the choice was left to three commissioners from Australia. In 1864, they unanimously chose Wellington, for its central position and its excellent harbour.

Parliament met in Wellington for the first time on 26 July 1865. Auckland protested, but it lost. Today Auckland is still the biggest city in the country, while Wellington is home to Parliament and to the Beehive, the round building where the government works.

Expert card: South Africa: three capitals for one country

In 1910, four British colonies joined together to form the Union of South Africa. Each one wanted to host the new government, so the leaders agreed on a compromise that still exists today: the country has three capitals.

Pretoria is the seat of the executive: the President and the government work in the Union Buildings, designed by the British architect Herbert Baker and completed in 1913. Cape Town is the seat of Parliament. Bloemfontein is home to the Supreme Court of Appeal.

Yet none of them is the biggest city. Johannesburg is, because it grew very fast after gold was discovered there in 1886. The Union Buildings are also a symbol of change: on 10 May 1994, Nelson Mandela was sworn in there as President, after the first elections in which all South Africans could vote.

Expert card: India: a new city for an old capital

For more than a century, Calcutta (today Kolkata), a huge port city in the east, was the capital of British India. On 12 December 1911, at a great ceremony called the Delhi Durbar, King George V announced that the capital would move to Delhi, a city that had been the seat of earlier empires, such as the Mughal Empire.

A new city, New Delhi, was planned next to the old one by two British architects, Edwin Lutyens and Herbert Baker. It was inaugurated in February 1931. The Viceroy’s House, completed in 1929, was the home of the British ruler of India.

When India became independent in 1947, it kept New Delhi as its capital, and the Viceroy’s House later became Rashtrapati Bhavan, the official home of the President of India. Today Mumbai, on the west coast, is known as the country’s financial capital.

Expert card: Nigeria: a capital built from scratch

When Nigeria became independent on 1 October 1960, Lagos was its capital. Lagos is a port on the Atlantic coast, in the south-west. It was growing very fast and it was crowded; it was also the capital of Lagos State.

In 1975, the government asked a panel led by Justice Akinola Aguda to find a new site. The panel recommended a place in the centre of the country, sparsely populated and easy to reach from every region. It was also seen as neutral ground, not linked to one of the country’s largest ethnic groups.

In February 1976, a decree created the Federal Capital Territory, and a brand-new city, Abuja, was built from scratch. The government officially moved there on 12 December 1991. Today Lagos is still the biggest city in Nigeria and one of the biggest in Africa.

Whole class: what do the stories have in common? · 10 min

Find at least three points that several stories share.

Possible answers

  • The capital is not the biggest city.
  • It is often a compromise between rival cities or regions (Canada, Australia, South Africa).
  • A central position matters (New Zealand, Nigeria).
  • Several capitals were chosen or built during the British Empire and kept after independence (India).
  • Some cities were built from scratch (Canberra, New Delhi, Abuja), like Washington, D.C.

Written record. In many Commonwealth countries, the capital is not the biggest city. It is often a compromise between rival cities or regions, or a central, sometimes new, city.
