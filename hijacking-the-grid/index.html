Our guiding question

How does the New York City Marathon temporarily hijack the city’s inequalities and turn its streets into a theater of psychological resilience?

Adapted version: the same lessons, with shorter texts, one step at a time and a reading aid. Open the adapted version

The city for a day

Every first Sunday of November, New York closes its streets, opens its bridges to runners and lines its avenues with more than a million spectators. For a few hours, the city of business and speed becomes a public stage where bankers and bus drivers cheer the same strangers. Is this a moment of genuine unity, or a beautiful illusion that disappears the next morning?

You will travel from the four loops of Central Park in 1970 to the record race of 2026, follow the course bridge by bridge, watch the mayor of New York hand out water to runners, and finally write your own argued article.

Session 1: Fifty Years, Five Boroughs

Discover how a small race in a park became the biggest marathon in the world.

Recap

Before we start: what do you already know? Answer quickly, in English.

  1. What are the five boroughs of New York City?
  2. How long is a marathon, in kilometers or in miles?
  3. Name one famous park in New York City.

Answer key

  1. Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens, the Bronx and Staten Island.
  2. 42.195 kilometers, or 26.2 miles.
  3. Central Park, for example (also Prospect Park in Brooklyn, Flushing Meadows Corona Park in Queens...).

Look at the painting 10 min

Look at the painting at the top of the page and read its reference. Where are we? What fills the street, and what fills the sky? What kind of event do you think the crowd is waiting for?

Sentence starters
  • The painting depicts… It was painted in…, during…
  • In the foreground / In the background, we can see…
  • The crowd seems to…, which suggests that…
  • The street is no longer a place for traffic: it has become…

Answer key

It is Fifth Avenue in Manhattan, in May 1917, a few weeks after the United States entered the First World War. Flags of the United States, Britain and France hang over the street; a dense crowd fills the avenue. The street has become a public stage for a shared event: this is exactly what the marathon does every November.

Read: from four loops to five boroughs 20 min

On a September morning in 1970, 127 runners, 126 men and one woman, lined up outside Tavern on the Green, in Central Park. The entry fee was one dollar. The course was simple: one trip around the park’s 1.7-mile lower loop, then four full loops of the park. Only 55 runners finished. The winner, Gary Muhrcke, was a New York City firefighter. The only woman, Nina Kuscsik, had to stop after 14 miles because she was ill.

In 1976, the year of the Bicentennial, the city was going through a severe financial crisis. A runner and political campaigner, George Spitz, had a bold idea: a marathon through all five boroughs. Manhattan Borough President Percy Sutton supported it, and the race director Fred Lebow turned it into a citywide celebration. That year, 2,090 entrants ran from Staten Island to Central Park.

Fifty years later, on 1 November 2026, the five-borough course celebrated its anniversary. According to New York Road Runners (NYRR), 61,529 runners started and 61,146 finished, from 153 countries: the largest marathon ever, for the third year in a row. More than a million spectators line the streets every year.

Check you understand

  1. Describe the first race in 1970: where, how many runners, what course?
  2. Why was 1976 a symbolic year, for the country and for the city?
  3. Who supported the idea of a five-borough race?
  4. Find three figures for the 2026 race and say what they refer to.
  5. In your own words: how did the race change its relationship with the city?

Answer key

  1. In Central Park, outside Tavern on the Green; 127 starters (126 men, one woman); one lower loop of 1.7 miles then four full loops of the park; 55 finishers.
  2. It was the Bicentennial of the United States (1776, 1976), and New York was in a severe financial crisis: the race was a way to celebrate and unite the city.
  3. George Spitz had the idea; Percy Sutton, Manhattan Borough President, supported it; Fred Lebow organized it.
  4. 61,529 starters, 61,146 finishers, 153 countries (and more than a million spectators).
  5. It left the closed space of the park and took over the streets of the whole city: it went from a race in the city to a race of the city.

Sources: NYRR, “NYRR in the 1970s: A Decade of Firsts”; NYC Parks, “New York City Marathon”; Central Park Conservancy; The New Yorker, “The First Five-Borough New York City Marathon”; NYRR press release, 2 November 2026; World Marathon Majors (spectators).

Read the data 15 min

Comment on the chart orally, for one minute. Compare, give proportions, interpret.

Sentence starters

  • That is more than … times as many as…

Answer key

From 55 finishers in 1970 to 61,146 in 2026: more than 1,100 times as many. Between 2024 and 2026, the race gained about 5,500 finishers. The 2026 figure is provisional (as of race day, according to NYRR).

Session 2: Bridges, Silence and the Wall of Sound

Follow the course mile by mile and dramatize it with emphatic inversion.

Recap

Last session: from four loops to five boroughs.

  1. How many runners started the first race in 1970, and where?
  2. What happened to the course in 1976?
  3. How many runners finished in 2026?

Answer key

  1. 127 runners, in Central Park.
  2. It went through all five boroughs, for the Bicentennial.
  3. 61,146 (provisional figure, NYRR).

The course, mile by mile 15 min

Read the course and find the places where the crowd disappears and where it explodes.

  1. Mile 1Verrazzano-Narrows Bridge, Staten Island: the start, and the highest point of the course.
  2. Miles 2 to 12Brooklyn: the longest part of the race, through many different neighborhoods.
  3. HalfwayThe Pulaski Bridge takes runners into Queens, to Long Island City.
  4. Miles 15 to 16The lower level of the Queensboro Bridge, a long climb into Manhattan. Spectators are not allowed on the bridges of the course.
  5. Miles 16 to 19First Avenue, Manhattan: huge crowds wait for the runners as they come off the bridge.
  6. Mile 20The Willis Avenue Bridge takes runners into the Bronx, then the Madison Avenue Bridge brings them back to Manhattan.
  7. Miles 22 to 24Fifth Avenue, from Harlem along the east side of Central Park, a long gentle climb.
  8. FinishCentral Park, where the race was born in 1970.

Runners often speak of hitting the wall: the moment, late in a marathon, often around mile 20, when the body suddenly seems empty. In New York, this is roughly where the course enters the Bronx.

  1. Where is the course silent? Why?
  2. Where does the noise come back? How would you feel as a runner?
  3. Which borough do runners reach around mile 20, and what may happen to them there?

Answer key

  1. On the bridges, especially the Queensboro Bridge (miles 15 to 16): spectators are not allowed on the bridges.
  2. On First Avenue, as soon as runners come off the Queensboro Bridge: a wall of sound after the silence, a psychological boost.
  3. The Bronx: many runners hit the wall there.

Sources: NYRR, official 2026 course map; NYRR spectator guidelines, as reported by New York City Wave (October 2026).

Grammar: emphatic inversion (advanced emphasis) 25 min

The rule

To dramatize what you say, you can start a sentence with a negative adverb (Never, Seldom, Rarely) or a restrictive expression (Not only, No sooner, Only when, Under no circumstances, Hardly). Then the order of the subject and the auxiliary is inverted, as in a question.

Negative / restrictive adverb + auxiliary + subject + verb

Standard sentenceWith inversion (B2)
New York rarely sees such unity.Rarely does New York see such overwhelming unity among its diverse neighborhoods.
Runners face physical exhaustion and they must also conquer psychological walls.Not only do runners face physical exhaustion, but they must also conquer psychological walls.
I had never seen so many people in the street.Never had I seen so many people in the street.

Watch out. No auxiliary in the sentence? Add do, does or did, as in a question: Rarely does New York see…. With Only when and No sooner… than, the inversion is in the main clause: Only when they reach mile 15 do they realize…

Grammar in context: rewrite each sentence with an emphatic inversion

  1. The runners realize how brutal the Queensboro Bridge is only when they reach mile 15.
  2. A sports event has seldom modified the socio-economic boundaries of a city so deeply.
  3. Spectators are not allowed on the bridges under any circumstances.
  4. The runners had just left the bridge when the roar of First Avenue hit them.
  5. The race crosses five bridges and it also visits all five boroughs.
  6. I have never felt such energy in a crowd.

Answer key

  1. Only when they reach mile 15 do the runners realize how brutal the Queensboro Bridge is.
  2. Seldom has a sports event modified the socio-economic boundaries of a city so deeply.
  3. Under no circumstances are spectators allowed on the bridges.
  4. No sooner had the runners left the bridge than the roar of First Avenue hit them. / Hardly had the runners left the bridge when the roar of First Avenue hit them.
  5. Not only does the race cross five bridges, but it also visits all five boroughs.
  6. Never have I felt such energy in a crowd.

Before you watch 5 min

Zohran Mamdani has been the mayor of New York City since 1 January 2026. Before he was elected, he ran the marathon himself, in 2022 and in 2024, according to the press. On Sunday 1 November 2026, he did not run. Look at the first image of the video: what is he wearing? What do you think he is doing there?

Sentence starters

Watch the video 20 min

First viewing (0:00 to 0:22)

  1. What is the mayor wearing, and why is this surprising?
  2. What does he give the runners? What else does he do?
  3. How do the runners react? Give two examples.
  4. Read the caption on the screen: who is the message for?

Second viewing (0:23 to 0:40)

  1. The mayor says the name of a runner. What does he wish her?
  2. What news does the runner give him? Where will it take place?
  3. Why does the mayor say “I’m sorry”? How does she answer?

Answer key

First viewing.

  1. A bright green volunteer vest over a dark suit and tie, and a cap: he looks like one of the race volunteers, not like a politician on a stage.
  2. Cups of water. He also cheers the runners on and gives them high-fives.
  3. Some runners slow down or turn around, smile, high-five him; some stop to talk to him or film him.
  4. For every New Yorker running the marathon: “Let’s go!”

Second viewing.

  1. He wishes her a great race (“Have a great race, Maddie”).
  2. She is getting married, in Central Park; she says her letter (her invitation) to him got lost in the mail.
  3. Because he did not answer her letter, which never reached him; she answers “I forgive you”, and he wishes her an amazing wedding in advance.

Public space, private moment 15 min

The video carries the words “Mayor Zohran Kwame Mamdani”: it was first posted by the mayor himself, then shared by news websites.

  1. Is the street a public or a private space on Marathon Sunday? Justify.
  2. Why might a mayor choose to stand at a water station rather than in an official stand?
  3. A video like this one spreads very fast on social networks. Is it a sincere gesture, a communication strategy, or both? Give arguments for each side.
Sentence starters
  • On the one hand…; on the other hand…
  • Not only does the gesture…, but it also…
  • Unless we know…, we cannot say for sure that…

Answer key

Open answers. Expected ideas: the street is closed to traffic and becomes a shared public space where private stories (a wedding, a personal challenge) are told aloud; standing among volunteers makes the mayor equal to ordinary New Yorkers; the video is posted by his own team, so it is also political communication, but the gesture is real and useful to runners. Both can be true.

Mediation 5 min

Your French-speaking friend saw the video with the sound off. Explain to him or her, in French, in about one minute: who the man is, where and when the scene takes place, what he does, and why people found the gesture so likeable.

Homework

Watch the video again at home. Write a short news report of about 100 words for your school website: “The Mayor at the Water Station”. Answer the questions who, what, where, when and why, and use one emphatic inversion.

Speaking drill: the audio-guide hack 30 min

In pairs. Student A is an undercover journalist or an urban sociologist posted at a strategic point of the course: where runners come back into Manhattan from the Bronx and start the long climb of Fifth Avenue towards Central Park. Student B is a runner close to exhaustion.

Goal: in 1 minute 30 seconds, the journalist interviews the runner about the contrast between the neighborhoods crossed and the effect of the crowd on his or her mind. You must use at least one emphatic inversion and three words from the vocabulary lists. Then swap roles.

Sentence starters
  • Journalist: Tell me, what went through your mind on the Queensboro Bridge?
  • Journalist: Did you notice any difference between the neighborhoods?
  • Runner: Never have I heard such deafening cheers…

Debate in groups of five 30 min

Does the marathon really bring New Yorkers together, or is it a beautiful illusion that lasts one day? Two students defend each side; the fifth student is the reporter: he or she notes the arguments and sums up the debate for the class.

Genuine unityA 24-hour illusion
The race crosses all five boroughs and every kind of neighborhood.The next day, the barricades are gone and the inequalities are back.

Part 1: reading comprehension (10 points) 30 min

A text written for this class

This text was written for this unit and is inspired by the facts we have studied. It is not a real essay and its author is not a real psychologist. “Collective emotional refueling” is an image invented for the text, not a scientific term.

For 364 days a year, New York City is an uncompromising grid governed by business and speed. Wealth shapes geography; invisible walls separate the busy towers of Manhattan from many streets of the outer boroughs. Yet, on Marathon Sunday, this layout is spectacularly hijacked. More than a million spectators line the asphalt, offering what we might call “collective emotional refueling”. Suddenly, the banker from Wall Street and the bus driver from the Bronx scream the same encouragement at the same strangers. Never before have these social worlds stood so close, shoulder to shoulder. However, this unity is ephemeral. No sooner has the last runner crossed the finish line in Central Park than the barricades are removed, the streets swept, and the ruthless economic architecture of the metropolis instantly reinstalled.

  1. Socio-spatial analysis. How does the author contrast the daily reality of New York with the specific atmosphere of Marathon Sunday? (3 pts)
  2. The concept of hijacking. In what way can the marathon be considered a political or social disruption rather than just a race? (3 pts)
  3. Linguistic analysis. Identify the two instances of emphatic inversion in the text. Translate them into French and explain how they reinforce the author’s argument about the intensity of the event. (4 pts)

Expected answers and marking

  1. Daily reality: a rigid grid ruled by business and speed, where wealth shapes geography and invisible walls separate Manhattan from the outer boroughs. Marathon Sunday: the layout is hijacked, a million spectators line the streets, people from opposite social worlds cheer side by side.
  2. The race takes over the streets, which normally serve traffic and business; it crosses the boundaries between rich and poor areas and puts different social groups in the same place, with the same emotion. It is a temporary disruption of the social order, and the text stresses that it does not last.
  3. “Never before have these social worlds stood so close” : « Jamais auparavant ces mondes sociaux ne s’étaient trouvés si proches ». “No sooner has the last runner crossed the finish line… than the barricades are removed” : « À peine le dernier coureur a-t-il franchi la ligne d’arrivée… que les barrières sont retirées ». The first inversion stresses how exceptional the unity is; the second stresses how fast it disappears. Together, they dramatize the contrast at the heart of the argument.

Part 2: written expression (10 points) 22 min

Subject A, critical article. You are a sports columnist for The New York Times. Write an article titled “The NYC Marathon: Genuine Social Unity or a 24-Hour Capitalist Illusion?”.
