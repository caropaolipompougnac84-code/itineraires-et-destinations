Introduction

Can one language hold many different stories? Through seven writers from India, New Zealand, Nigeria, Jamaica, Australia and Canada, and through the Commonwealth Charter, students discover how English carries very different voices, and they make a class anthology with a page and a two-minute presentation each.

Our question

Can one language hold many different stories?

A rule for the whole sequence. A text is one voice, not a whole country. We speak about the writers and the documents; no student is asked about their origins, their family, their languages at home or their beliefs. The colonial past is named factually, when a text needs it, without judging any country.

The skill tags. Every activity shows its skill: CO listening (compréhension de l'oral), CE reading (compréhension de l'écrit), PO speaking (expression orale), PE writing (expression écrite). Every lesson works the four skills.

The Gorse spiral of the sequence

Six questions, one per stage of the sequence, each one narrower than the last, until the point: the question the students answer in their anthology page and presentation.

  1. 1Whose English words?Tagore wrote in Bengali and translated himself: a voice can cross languages.
  2. 2Can an object carry a life?Naidu's bangles follow the stages of a woman's life, in images of light.
  3. 3Who arranges the perfect day?Mansfield shows two worlds in one garden through Laura's eyes.
  4. 4Must English sound the same?Adichie found her own stories; English has borrowed and travelled.
  5. 5Can a shop window contain another place?McKay's fruit brings Jamaica into New York: a list becomes longing.
  6. 6Can shared values protect different voices?The Charter speaks of a shared language and of « multiple identities »: a promise, not a proof.

At the point. Can one language hold many different stories? Yes, if each voice keeps its own words: our anthology gives each writer a country, a date, a short quotation and a personal response, and never makes one text speak for a whole country. This is the question each student answers in the final page and presentation.

The six directives of the programme, and where they are worked

« Le Commonwealth of Nations rassemble des pays qui partagent un patrimoine linguistique et un héritage politique complexe. Quelle place occupe-t-il dans l'aire anglophone ? Comment les pays membres parviennent-ils à y évoluer tout en affirmant leur singularité culturelle ? »

Axis 6, compulsory in Seconde. Object of study « La littérature anglophone, témoignage de l'histoire et d'histoires » (lessons 1 to 6 and 8), with « Représenter le Commonwealth de sa conception à aujourd'hui » for the Charter and the official video (lesson 7). Optional link with axis 5, « Créer et recréer », in the rewriting of lesson 3.

2. Grammar in context

« s'effectue en situation, à la fois dans les activités de compréhension et d'expression, ainsi que dans des temps de réflexion sur la langue, d'explicitation et de conceptualisation »

Each Language focus starts from the text of the day (a poem, a story, a talk, the Charter), states the rule, practises it and grades it.

3. Sounds and spellings

« Elle met en évidence les liens ou les écarts entre graphie et phonie. »

A pronunciation point in every lesson: the -s ending, /aɪ/, the -ed ending, sentence stress, /iː/ and /ɪ/, polite intonation.

4. Lexis in a cultural setting

« L'acquisition du lexique consiste en la mémorisation de mots et d'expressions lexicalisées et idiomatiques toujours en lien avec un environnement culturel et une situation de communication. »

A voice, to translate, a quotation, a bride, to blush, a borrowing, longing, diversity: flashcards in every lesson and « My anthology file ».

5. Authentic documents

« Les documents authentiques s'adressent à des locuteurs natifs dans des contextes réels, non pédagogiques. »

Seven writers in their own words (texts in the public domain), a TED talk, the Commonwealth Charter and an official video of the Commonwealth Secretariat.

6. The written record

« la trace des activités effectuées et corrigées en classe [...] occupe une place fondamentale ainsi que les bilans lexicaux et grammaticaux. »

A « Written record » box in every lesson, a self-check grid after every graded check, and the « Taking a correction » routine in lessons 5, 6 and 8.

Quotations: programme d'anglais du lycée général et technologique, 2025 (in French, as published; [...] shows a cut).

Objectives

  • Read and hear authentic literary voices in English from six countries, in their own words.
  • Distinguish a voice (a poem, a story) from a fact (a source) and from a promise (a Charter).
  • Follow an image, a viewpoint and a turning point, and support a reading with a short quotation.
  • Describe, narrate, compare, concede and recommend in English.
  • Explain a text to a French reader (mediation).
  • Present a text in two minutes, clearly and with key words only.

The seven literary texts are in the public domain (their authors died more than seventy years ago) and are quoted from the editions named under each text, checked against Project Gutenberg, Wikisource, the University of Pennsylvania or the Academy of American Poets; the few typographical changes are declared. The two videos are on their official channels (TED, The Commonwealth). The three teacher's scripts were written for this sequence and say so.

CO · Meet the first writer (diagnostic) CO

For the teacher. Read the script twice, slowly, with a pause after each sentence the second time. Students only see the questions. Dys: questions with choices (dys version).

Today we start a journey through stories written in English. Our first writer is Rabindranath Tagore. He was born in Calcutta, in India, in 1861. His first language was Bengali. He wrote poems and songs in Bengali, and then he translated some of them into English himself. In 1913, he won the Nobel Prize in Literature. He was the first writer from outside Europe to win it. In this sequence, we will read writers from India, New Zealand, Nigeria, Jamaica, Australia and Canada. They all use English, but each one has a different voice. At the end, our class will make an anthology: a small book of our favourite texts, with a short quotation and our own response. Listen to the writers. Then speak for yourself.

For students.

  1. Where and when was the writer born?
  2. What was his first language?
  3. What did he do with some of his poems?
  4. What did he win, and when?
  5. Name three countries of the writers in this sequence.
  6. What will the class make at the end?

Answer key (teacher)

1 Calcutta, India, 1861. 2 Bengali. 3 He translated them into English himself. 4 The Nobel Prize in Literature, 1913. 5 three of India, New Zealand, Nigeria, Jamaica, Australia, Canada. 6 an anthology (a small book of texts, each with a quotation and a personal response). Language focus · Present simple for facts about a writer; voice and translation; the -s ending PEPO

For students. Language focus: the short lesson.

Grammar

Facts that are true: the present simple. « Tagore writes in Bengali and in English. » With he, she, it: verb + -s. Negative: does not + base verb: « A poem does not tell the whole story. » Question: « Does the poem speak about a country? » For a finished life, use the past simple: « Tagore won the Nobel Prize in 1913. »

Lexis

A writer, a poet, a poem, a line, a voice, a reader; to translate (« traduire »), a translation; a mother tongue, a first language; to quote, a quotation. Many voices = many different ways of writing and speaking.

Pronunciation

Three sounds for -s: /s/ after p, t, k, f: writes /raɪts/, speaks; /z/ after a vowel or a voiced sound: reads /riːdz/, lives; /ɪz/ after s, z, sh, ch: teaches /ˈtiːtʃɪz/, uses /ˈjuːzɪz/. Never a silent -s in English.

Practice.

  1. Complete: Tagore ___ (write) poems. A poem ___ (not / tell) everything.
  2. Make a question: the poem / speak / about freedom?
  3. Sort aloud: writes, reads, teaches, speaks, uses, lives. Which have /ɪz/?

Answer key (teacher)

writes; does not tell. Does the poem speak about freedom? /ɪz/: teaches, uses. /s/: writes, speaks. /z/: reads, lives.

Graded language check (5 minutes, /6).

  1. Complete: The poet ___ (use) the word « where » many times. (1)
  2. Correct: A poem do not tell the whole story. (1)
  3. Make a question: Tagore / write / in English? (1)
  4. Complete with the past: Tagore ___ (win) the Nobel Prize in 1913. (1)
  5. Translate « traduire » and « une citation ». (1)
  6. Which ending is /ɪz/: writes or teaches? (1)

Answer key and marking (teacher)

1 uses. 2 A poem does not tell the whole story. 3 Does Tagore write in English? 4 won. 5 to translate; a quotation (both needed). 6 teaches. Each item: 1 point. For a whole sentence, 0.5 if the target structure is right but another error remains. Total /6. The sound item tests sound awareness; actual pronunciation is observed in the paired speaking.

Language focus · Comparatives and used to; words that travel; sentence stress PEPO

Grammar

Comparing: short adjective + -er than: « Nigeria is hotter than England. » Long adjective: more + adjective + than: « Her stories became more personal. » Used to + base verb for a past habit that has changed: « She used to write about snow. » Negative: « She didn't use to write about mangoes. »

Lexis

A variety of English, an accent, a dialect, a borrowing (a word taken from another language). Words that travelled into English: bungalow, shampoo, pyjamas, jungle (from languages of India), kangaroo (from Guugu Yimithirr, Australia), kiwi (from Māori, New Zealand). Taking a correction

A corrected paper is not a verdict: it is a map that shows where to go next. This step is done after every assessment, in class or at home, and it counts in the sequence mark.

Six steps

For students.

  1. I read all the comments before I look at my mark again.
  2. I compare my work with the criteria, line by line: what I have, what is missing.
  3. I find my errors with the code in the margin (table below).
  4. I rewrite three sentences, correctly, in another colour.
  5. I write what I keep: one success I want to repeat.
  6. I set my goal for the next task: one rule to revise, one flashcard to make.

Emotional storm and emergency exits

Speaking a foreign language in front of others, talking about yourself or your future, hearing your own voice on a recording: all this can bring strong emotions (anger, shame, nervous laughter, tears, blocking). This tab gives simple moves for the student and for the teacher.

Exercise 1. My inner weather (2 minutes)

For students.

I choose my weather: sunny, cloudy, rainy, stormy. I write one sentence: « Today I feel ... because ... » I keep it, or I give it to my teacher.

Exercise 2. Square breathing (1 minute)

For students.

I breathe in for 4. I hold for 4. I breathe out for 4. I hold for 4. Three times, feet flat on the floor. This exercise is optional.

Exercise 3. Give the feeling to a character (5 minutes)

For students.

I choose a person from the sequence (Laura in « The Garden-party », the drover's wife, the nervous young man of « My Financial Career », the speaker of « The Tropics in New York ») or « Sam », an imaginary student who presents a poem to the class. I write three lines: how could this person feel at that moment? What could help them? Three intensities: a little nervous, quite nervous, very nervous.

Exercise 4. The bridge sentence (in an assessment)

For students.

If I am stuck: I breathe, I read the question again, I underline the key word, I go on to the next question and I come back later. I write at least one short answer for each question, even if it is not complete.

In this sequence, watch out for

  • Identity (all lessons): never ask a student about their origins, a migration, their languages at home or their beliefs, and never ask a student to speak for a country or a people. Students speak about the texts.
  • Missing a place (lesson 6): McKay's longing can touch students who have left a country, a home or a family. Keep to the poem, never ask for personal stories, and use the emotional storm exercise of the lesson. The infirmière, the CPE and the PsyEN are the people to see.
  • Accents (lesson 4) and social class (lesson 3): stop any mockery calmly and recall the rule: every accent is a voice; we speak about characters, never about classmates.
  • Religion and politics (lessons 1, 2, 7): Tagore's prayer, the temple fair and the Charter are explained as words and texts; no belief is discussed, no country is judged, no current political event is debated.
  • Being recorded (lessons 5, 8): recordings are made in pairs, kept on school devices, deleted after marking; a very anxious student can record alone with the teacher, at an appointment.

> Many Voices, One Commonwealth? The question looks simple. English travels; institutions travel; a flag and a charter travel with them. But a language does not arrive empty. It carries the history of who had the power to name things, and who had to borrow the words. > > Rabindranath Tagore wrote in Bengali and in English. Sarojini Naidu's bangles are only jewellery if you refuse to see the life around them: work, ritual, colour, and a woman's voice arranging the scene. Katherine Mansfield, born in New Zealand and published in London, can make a garden party look perfect, then let a death on the other side of the hedge interrupt the arrangement. Claude McKay, in Harlem, looks at a window and recognises home: the Commonwealth, in his lines, is not a club of comfort, it is a memory that follows you. > > In 2013, the Commonwealth Charter set down values: democracy, human rights, tolerance, diversity. Shared values can protect difference, or they can become a slogan that hides it. A class anthology is a small test of that idea. If every text sounds like the same English, readers will leave with one voice. If the anthology helps them hear Tagore, Naidu, Mansfield and McKay side by side, without reducing any of them, then « one Commonwealth » does not mean « one story ».

Pour chaque tour : la question du professeur, la réponse attendue, et ce qui se resserre.

TourQuestion du professeurRéponse attendueCe qui se resserre
1. Je remarque (I notice)Qu'est-ce qui saute aux yeux ?Le titre pose une question : Many Voices, One Commonwealth? Elle a l'air simple.On voit d'abord une unité possible (une langue, une institution), déjà mise en doute par le point d'interrogation.
2. Je repère (I spot)Quels éléments le texte nomme-t-il ?L'anglais voyage ; les institutions voyagent ; un drapeau et une charte voyagent avec eux. Mais une langue n'arrive pas vide.L'unité affichée se heurte à une réserve : la langue porte autre chose qu'elle-même.
3. Je comprends (I understand)Que permettent de comprendre ces éléments ?Le Commonwealth n'efface pas les voix distinctes (Tagore, Naidu, Mansfield, McKay, parmi d'autres) : il les réunit sous une langue qui a une histoire.La question n'est plus « une ou plusieurs langues ? », c'est « que portent ces voix dans la même langue ? ».
4. J'anticipe (I anticipate)Que vise le texte ?Il prépare une lecture des voix de l'anthologie, pas un cours d'institutions du Commonwealth.On anticipe le hors-sujet : parler du drapeau sans les textes.
5. Je trouve le sens (I find the meaning)Quel est l'essentiel, et sa limite ?Une langue commune ne fait pas une seule voix. Limite : on ne peut ni nier ce qui circule (langue, charte), ni faire dire la même chose à toutes les voix.Une phrase défendable, avec sa limite.

L'élève ne peut pas venir en classe ou est hospitalisé

L'épreuve blanche peut se faire à la maison ou à l'hôpital, sans aide, en respectant la durée. L'élève note l'heure de début et de fin. Le document audio est déposé sur l'ENT ; l'élève l'écoute trois fois, avec une minute de pause entre les écoutes, sans revenir en arrière. L'oral se fait par enregistrement (présentation), puis par un échange au téléphone ou en visioconférence avec l'enseignante, ou à défaut par trois questions écrites envoyées après l'enregistrement.
