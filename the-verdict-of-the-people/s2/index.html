Lesson 2. Why Midterms Matter

How can Congress limit presidential power?

Road map

Objectives

Explain the role of Congress and separate several institutional powers.

Language: passive voice, can, must, both, whereas.

Source B · common reading

Congress and the president

Student text, newly written from USAGov and the Constitution.

Congress has two chambers: the House of Representatives and the Senate. House members are elected every two years. Senators serve six-year terms, so only part of the Senate (about one third) is regularly elected every two years.

Midterm elections take place between presidential elections. They can change the balance of power in Congress without directly electing a new president.

For ordinary federal legislation, both chambers generally need to approve the same bill before it is presented to the president. The president may veto it. Congress can override a veto through the constitutionally required votes.

A president’s party may therefore win the presidency but still face opposition in Congress. This can make negotiation necessary.

Sources: USAGov, Congressional elections and midterm elections · National Archives, the Constitution, transcript.

Extension and teacher reference

More powers, more thresholds

All 435 House seats are contested every two years. To override a veto, Congress needs a two-thirds vote in each chamber. The Senate considers many presidential nominations and gives advice and consent to treaties, which requires two thirds of the senators present. The House has the power to impeach; the Senate holds the trial.

With all 435 House seats filled, 218 is a majority of seats. In a full 100-seat Senate, 51 seats form a numerical majority. However, not every decision uses the same threshold, and the Vice President may break a tied Senate vote. Chamber control is not a guarantee that every proposal will pass.

These points stay available for interested students; they are not all compulsory recall items.

Exercise 2B

Who can do what?

Match each power to House, Senate, both chambers or president. Items 3 to 5 use the extension box.

  1. Veto a bill.
  2. Approve ordinary legislation before it is presented to the president.
  3. Impeach.
  4. Hold an impeachment trial.
  5. Consider many presidential nominations.
  6. Override a presidential veto through the required votes.

Then explain why this statement is misleading: “Any party with 51 senators can automatically make every proposal become law.”

Answer key

  1. President.
  2. Both chambers.
  3. House.
  4. Senate.
  5. Senate.
  6. Both chambers.

The statement is misleading because lawmaking normally also involves the House and the president, and some Senate decisions need other procedural or constitutional thresholds.

Exercise 2C

Passive voice, with a reason

Transform the sentences.

  1. Voters elect House members every two years.
  2. Both chambers must approve the bill.
  3. The Senate considers the nomination.
  4. The president vetoed the bill.
  5. Congress can override a veto.

Why the passive? In a news report, why might a journalist put the bill rather than both chambers at the start of sentence 2?

Answer key

  1. House members are elected every two years.
  2. The bill must be approved by both chambers.
  3. The nomination is considered by the Senate.
  4. The bill was vetoed by the president.
  5. A veto can be overridden by Congress.

The report may be focusing on the bill and the stage it has reached, rather than on the people or institutions doing the action.

Exercise 2D

Mediation: answer an exchange student

An exchange student says: “The president won an election, so Congress has to accept the president’s plans.”

Give a 45-second reply with one institutional fact, one example of a limit and one qualification. Your partner then summarises your reply without copying its exact words.

Common B1+ route: explain why a president cannot simply turn every proposal into law; use one institutional example and one passive construction.

Towards B2: explain why congressional support may help a president without guaranteeing every policy outcome.

Answer key

Possible response: “Winning a presidential election does not give the president unlimited power. Congress has separate constitutional responsibilities. For example, a proposed law normally has to be approved by both chambers. The Senate also considers many presidential nominations. A president may find cooperation easier with the support of Congress, but support does not guarantee that every proposal will pass.”
