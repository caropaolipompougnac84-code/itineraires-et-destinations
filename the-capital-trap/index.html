The Capital Trap

Is the biggest city always the capital?

Seven lessons of 55 minutes, each timed for 52 minutes, plus a written assessment and an oral assessment. Students learn to read the map of the United States as a story of rivers, roads and political choices, avoid the capital trap, compare places, and present a road trip in the past, with a postcard.

Road map

Seven lessons of 55 minutes, each timed for 52 minutes, plus a written assessment and an oral assessment.

  1. Lesson 1. Why geography matters 52 min
  2. Lesson 2. Discovering the map 52 min
  3. Lesson 3. Big cities vs capitals 52 min
  4. Lesson 4. Map detective 52 min
  5. Lesson 5. Guess the city 52 min
  6. Lesson 6. The Capital Trap game 52 min
  7. Lesson 7. Planning a road trip 52 min
  • Written record. Each lesson ends with a short written record, copied in the notebook.
  • Participation. Everyone speaks at least once in every lesson.
  • Homework for next lesson. Each lesson page states the homework; lessons 6 and 7 prepare the postcard and the oral.

Stopping point. Each lesson page marks a stopping point, so that the end of the lesson can be condensed if need be.

Emergency exit · 8 min. If time runs short, a condensed eight-minute ending (inside the 52 minutes, not 52 plus 8) still leaves every student with the written record, one spoken sentence, and the homework.

The assessment page has the written test, the oral grid and the visible answer keys. The written test is also timed for 52 minutes; the oral is organised separately (10 minutes of preparation, then 5 minutes per student).

Why geography matters

A land of contrasts

The United States is a huge country. It stretches from the Atlantic Ocean to the Pacific Ocean, over 4,500 kilometres. You can find almost every landscape there: snowy mountains in Alaska, deserts in Arizona, tropical beaches in Florida, endless plains in the Midwest and giant lakes on the border with Canada.

But geography is not only about landscapes. It also explains where people decided to live, and why.

Water first, cities next

Two hundred years ago, there were very few roads. People and goods travelled by water. So the first big cities grew where boats could stop: on the coast, on a river or on a lake.

New York has a huge natural harbour. New Orleans stands near the mouth of the Mississippi. St. Louis grew where the Missouri meets the Mississippi. Chicago grew on Lake Michigan, and later became the heart of the railways.

In 1825, the Erie Canal linked New York to the Great Lakes. Goods from the Midwest could now travel by boat to New York City, and it became the biggest city in the country. Then the railways crossed the continent in the 19th century, and the highways followed in the 20th century, like the famous Route 66.

Why the capital is often a small city

When Americans chose the capital of each state, they often did not choose the biggest city. They wanted a place near the centre of the state, easy to reach for everybody. Sometimes they also wanted to keep political power away from the big business cities.

That is why Sacramento, not Los Angeles, is the capital of California, and Albany, not New York City, is the capital of New York State.

Why it matters

Knowing the map helps you understand American culture. Jazz was born in New Orleans and travelled up the Mississippi to Chicago. “Go West!” was the dream of the pioneers. Route 66 is the road of novels, films and rock ’n’ roll songs.

When an American film talks about “upstate New York”, “the Deep South” or “the Midwest”, you will know exactly where it is, and what it means.

Story: Inès and the map

When Inès arrived in Austin for her exchange year, she was proud of her English and proud of her geography. On the first evening, at dinner, she said: “I’m so happy to be in Texas! Next weekend, I want to visit the capital, Houston.”

Her host family smiled. Her host brother laughed. “Houston? But you’re in the capital right now!”

Inès turned red. How could the biggest city in Texas not be the capital?

After dinner, Mr Walker, her host father, opened an old map on the kitchen table. “Look,” he said. “Houston is near the Gulf of Mexico. It has a port, ships, oil and business. It’s big because of the water. Austin is in the heart of Texas, on the Colorado River. In 1839, Texan leaders chose it because it was in the middle of the land they wanted to develop.”

He moved his finger across the map. “Every American city tells a story. New Orleans speaks French because of the Mississippi. Chicago is huge because of the lake and the trains. Las Vegas started as a train stop in the desert.”

That night, Inès wrote in her notebook:

“A map is not a list of names. It is a story of rivers, roads and choices. To understand Americans, I must first understand their land.”

The next weekend, she did visit Houston. But this time, she knew why it was there.

At the end of this journey, you will be able to

  • locate the main cities, rivers and lakes of the United States;
  • avoid the capital trap: the biggest city is not always the capital;
  • compare places with comparatives and superlatives;
  • tell a trip in the past, and pronounce regular -ed endings;
  • explain why a city is where it is, and present a road trip with a postcard.

The seven lessons

Lesson 1 Why geography matters: Inès and the map. Lesson 2 Discovering the map: oceans, rivers and lakes. Lesson 3 Big cities vs capitals: the trap, and how to compare. Lesson 4 Map detective: the Juneau mystery and the origin of names. Lesson 5 Guess the city: describe and identify famous cities. Lesson 6 The Capital Trap game. Lesson 7 Planning a road trip on Route 66, in the preterite.

Final task: present your road trip and write a postcard, like Inès. The grids are on the assessment page. Language tables and exercises are also collected on the language page. Cut-out cards are on the cards page. The student sheet is in the downloads.

To revise, make your own bristol flashcards (Carole’s method): the word or the state on the front, the meaning or the capital on the back, studied in both directions, in short daily sessions.
