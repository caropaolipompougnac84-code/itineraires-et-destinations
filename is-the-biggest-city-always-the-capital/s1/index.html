Lesson 1. Water first, cities next

Understand how transport shaped American cities, then locate capitals with precision.

Road map

Lesson of 55 minutes, timed for 52 minutes. 7 lessons, plus a final-task assessment.

  1. Recap: three quick questions 4 min
  2. Look at the engraving of the Capitol grounds 10 min
  3. Read: water first, cities next, then check you understand 18 min
  4. Map search: locate capitals precisely 20 min
  • Written record. Transport shaped the map, but politics shaped the capitals. The first big cities grew near water; capitals were often chosen for a central position, away from business power.
  • Participation. Everyone speaks at least once: each student locates one place on the map in a full sentence, with a precise expression (not far from, on the banks of, at the far end of…).
  • Homework for next lesson. Learn the words of part 1 on the language page. Then write five sentences to locate five US cities precisely, with five different expressions (not far from, at the far end of, on the banks of, halfway between, north of).

Stopping point. After the reading check, before a long map search if time is short. Keep at least two locating sentences.

Emergency exit · 8 min. If time runs short at this stopping point, condense the end of the lesson inside the 52 minutes (this is not 52 plus 8). Dictate a short written record (two sentences). A quick round so that everyone speaks at least once (one sentence each). Write the homework on the board.

You already know, if you have studied The Capital Trap: the idea that cities grew near water, and that a capital is often not the biggest city, is treated at length there (lessons 1 and 2), with the full story of Inès. This lesson does not repeat that story. It opens on a document of its own, the 1870 engraving of the Capitol grounds, and it trains B1+ locating phrases on the map.

Recap · 4 min

What do you already know? Answer quickly, in English.

  1. What is the capital of the USA?
  2. What is the biggest city in the USA?
  3. Is Sydney the capital of Australia?

Answer key

  1. Washington, D.C.
  2. New York City.
  3. No: the capital of Australia is Canberra. Sydney is a much bigger city.

Look at the engraving · 10 min

Sentence starters. The engraving shows… It was published in 1870. In the foreground, I can see… In the background, I can see…

Answer key

The engraving shows people attending a concert on the Capitol grounds in Washington, D.C. It was published in Harper’s Weekly on 23 July 1870. The Capitol, where the United States Congress meets, represents political power. Washington, D.C., not New York City, is the capital of the USA.

Read: water first, cities next · 18 min

Two hundred years ago, there were very few roads in the United States. People and goods used to travel by water, so the first big cities grew where boats could stop: on the coast, on a river or on a lake. New York has a huge natural harbour; New Orleans stands near the mouth of the Mississippi; Chicago grew on Lake Michigan.

In 1825, the Erie Canal linked New York to the Great Lakes. Goods from the Midwest could now be carried by boat to the harbour of New York City, which grew faster than any other city in the country. Then railways were built across the continent in the 19th century, and highways followed in the 20th century, such as Route 66.

Transport shaped the map, but politics shaped the capitals. When Americans chose the capital of each state, they often avoided the biggest city. They wanted a place near the centre of the state, easy to reach for everybody, and sometimes far from the power of business. That is why Sacramento, not Los Angeles, is the capital of California, and Albany, not New York City, is the capital of New York State.

Check you understand

  1. Why did the first big cities grow near water?
  2. What changed in 1825? What was the consequence?
  3. Which two kinds of transport came after the canals?
  4. Explain the sentence “Transport shaped the map, but politics shaped the capitals.”
  5. Find two verbs in the passive in the text.

Answer key

  1. Because people and goods used to travel by water: there were very few roads.
  2. The Erie Canal linked New York to the Great Lakes; as a result, New York City grew faster than any other city.
  3. Railways, then highways.
  4. Big cities grew where transport was easy, but capitals were chosen for political reasons: a central position, easy access, distance from business power.
  5. “could be carried”, “were built”.

Map search · 20 min

In pairs, find on the map: two state capitals that are much smaller than the biggest city of their state; a capital on a river; a capital on the coast; a state capital you cannot reach by road. Then locate each place precisely for your partner.

Sentence starters. It is located not far from… · It lies at the far end of…, close to the border with… · It stands on the banks of…, about … km from…

Answer key

Possible answers: Sacramento (California), Albany (New York State), Springfield (Illinois), Tallahassee (Florida); Albany is on the Hudson River, Sacramento on the Sacramento River; Juneau, Boston and Honolulu are on the coast; Juneau cannot be reached by road.

Written record. Transport shaped the map, but politics shaped the capitals. The first big cities grew near water; capitals were often chosen for a central position, away from business power.
