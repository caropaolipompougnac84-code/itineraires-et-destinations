Lesson 5. Annigoni 1955, Freud 2001: idealised or unflinching?

Does a royal portrait owe the public the truth, or flattery?

Road map

Lesson of 55 minutes, timed for 52 minutes. 8 lessons.

  1. Recap (marked /3) 5 min
  2. Compare Annigoni and Freud from the official pages, with the method 18 min
  3. Reception: two short press reactions 8 min
  4. Debate: truth or flattery? 14 min
  5. A brief official comparison (Sutherland), exit ticket and homework 7 min
  • Written record. A written contrast of format and gaze, and one conceded then rebutted sentence from the debate.
  • Participation. Everyone speaks at least once: one contrast between the two portraits, or one argument in the debate.
  • Homework for next lesson. Write a 200-word review of one of the two portraits for an art magazine. Give the official link; do not copy the picture.

Stopping point. End of this lesson.

Emergency exit · 8 min. If time runs short at this stopping point, condense the end of the lesson: dictate a short written record (two sentences); a quick round so that everyone speaks at least once (one sentence each); write the homework on the board.

Recap · marked /3
  1. What is the mask of youth?
  2. Why was the anointing not filmed in 1953?
  3. Name two adjectives from the method that you could use about a royal portrait.

Answer key

  1. An official, ageless, approved face that hides real ageing.
  2. To keep a sacred moment out of the public gaze.
  3. Accept any from the method: idealised, unflinching, iconic, timeless, divine, human, maternal…

These two paintings are still in copyright and are not reproduced here. Show them in class, enlarged, from the official pages, and compare them with the five-step method (format, gaze, light, attributes).

  • Pietro Annigoni, portrait of Elizabeth II, 1954 to 1955, Fishmongers’ Hall, London: a young Queen in Garter robes against a landscape. Official page: The Fishmongers’ Company, Court Drawing Room.
  • Lucian Freud, portrait of Elizabeth II, 2000 to 2001, Royal Collection (RCIN 407895): a tiny, head-only painting with the Diamond Diadem. Official page: Royal Collection Trust, RCIN 407895.

  • Reception. Freud’s portrait divided the British press in December 2001. Students read two short contrasting reactions selected by the teacher (fair-use quotations, not reproduced here in full).
  • Debate. Motion: “A royal portrait owes the public the truth, not flattery.” Use a concession and answer one objection.

A further official case, also not reproduced. Graham Sutherland’s portrait of Winston Churchill (1954) was so unflinching that Churchill hated it, and it was later destroyed. It is useful as a comparison (a sitter who did not accept the truth of the paint), but it is not the main document of this lesson, and it is still in copyright. If you show it, use an official museum or archive page, not a copy on this site.

Answer key · model comparison

Annigoni builds a timeless icon, close to Elizabeth I’s mask of youth: the Queen is distant and serene. Freud refuses the icon: tiny format, heavy paint, an ageing face. Paradoxically, the Queen accepted to sit for him, which can be read as a sign of confidence in the institution.

Exit ticket
  1. Give one contrast of format or gaze between Annigoni and Freud.
  2. Write one sentence that concedes something to the other side of the debate, then rebuts it.

Homework

Write a 200-word review of one of the two portraits for an art magazine. Give the official link, do not copy the picture, and use five precise art or method words.
