Session 8. Citizens decide: four nations, a referendum and votes at sixteen

Key question: Who has a voice in British democracy, and is the United Kingdom still united?

Marked recap · 3 points

What do you remember from session 7? Answer alone in five minutes, then check.

1. Who was the first Prime Minister?

2. What is a by-election?

3. What must a Prime Minister have to be appointed?

Recap answers

  1. Sir Robert Walpole (1721 to 1742).
  2. An election in one constituency between two general elections.
  3. The confidence of the House of Commons.

Key words first

  • referendum: a vote of all citizens on one question
  • turnout: the percentage of voters who actually vote
  • to leave / to remain: to go out of / to stay in
  • devolution: giving some powers to Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland
  • independence: the state of being a separate country
  • to register: to put your name on the list of voters

Aims

  • Read and present election figures
  • See the differences between the four nations of the UK
  • Debate the voting age

Today's language goal

To present election figures accurately and argue for or against votes at sixteen, we need quantifiers and comparatives (a majority of, more than, a narrow margin) and should, with a review of the passive and the second conditional.

Documents

Document A. The EU referendum, 23 June 2016

In 2016, the UK voted to leave the EU: 52% Leave, 48% Remain.

England and Wales voted Leave.

Scotland and Northern Ireland voted Remain.

The original text

UK result: Leave 51.9% (17,410,742 votes), Remain 48.1% (16,141,241 votes). Turnout: 72.2%.

NationLeaveRemain
England53.4%46.6%
Wales52.5%47.5%
Scotland38.0%62.0%
Northern Ireland44.2%55.8%

David Cameron, who had called the referendum, announced his resignation the next day. The UK left the European Union on 31 January 2020.

Source: Electoral Commission, Results and turnout at the EU referendum · House of Commons Library, Analysis of the EU Referendum results 2016

Document B. Four nations (written for this sequence)

The UK has four nations: England, Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland.

In 2014, Scotland voted to stay in the UK: 55% No.

The original text

Since 1999, Scotland and Wales have had their own parliaments, and Northern Ireland its Assembly, created after the Good Friday Agreement of 1998. On 18 September 2014, Scotland voted on independence: No 55.3%, Yes 44.7%, with a turnout of 84.6%. Sixteen and seventeen year olds could vote in that referendum, and they can vote in elections to the Scottish and Welsh parliaments.

Source: Electoral Management Board for Scotland, Scottish Independence Referendum 2014

Document C. Votes at sixteen?

A new bill wants 16 and 17 year olds to vote in all UK elections.

The Lords are studying it now. It is not law yet.

The original text

“The Representation of the People Bill will extend the voting age to 16 for all UK elections. 16- and 17-year-olds will have the same rights, once registered, as any other voter.”

The bill has passed the House of Commons. Its committee stage in the House of Lords began on 2 November 2026. It is not law yet.

Source: GOV.UK, Representation of the People Bill: Votes at 16 · UK Parliament, Representation of the People Bill enters Lords committee stage

Activities

  1. Document A: present the figures orally in pairs (the majority, a minority, a narrow margin). Language: Present each figure with a quantifier: A narrow majority voted Leave; more than six voters in ten in Scotland voted Remain.
  2. Map work: colour the four nations Leave or Remain. What does the map show about the unity of the Kingdom?
  3. Document C: debate in two teams: Should sixteen-year-olds vote? Terminale students are directly concerned.
  4. Back to Magna Carta: who had a voice in 1215, in 1689, in 2016, and tomorrow? Then prepare the plan of the final task.

Sentence starters

  • A majority of ... voted ...
  • Only ... % ...
  • Scotland and Northern Ireland, whereas ...
  • I am for / against votes at sixteen because ...

Exercise: Reading figures

Complete with the right word or number.

  1. In 2016, ______ % of voters chose Leave.
  2. The turnout was ______ %.
  3. ______ voted Remain with 62%.
  4. In 2014, Scotland voted ______ to independence.
  5. The voting age bill is now in the House of ______.

Answer key

1. 51.9. 2. 72.2. 3. Scotland. 4. No (55.3%). 5. Lords.

Stopping point · end of lesson 15. Lesson 16 starts here.

Words in action

To say it precisely, we need the right words

EntryComplete with: referendum, turnout, devolution, register.

  1. A vote of all citizens on one question is a ______.
  2. The percentage of people who vote is the ______.
  3. Giving powers to Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland is ______.
  4. Before voting, you must ______.

Answer key

1. referendum. 2. turnout. 3. devolution. 4. register.

To say it: Figures, comparatives and should

To present election figures accurately and argue for or against votes at sixteen

more than, higher than = plus que.

should = devrait.

EntryChoose.

  1. The turnout was (higher / lower) in 2014 than in 2016.
  2. (A majority / A minority) of Scottish voters chose Remain.
  3. Sixteen-year-olds (should / should to) vote.

Answer key

1. higher. 2. A majority. 3. should.

Figures of speech in the documents

Rhetorical question and paradox

Study topic: “A Kingdom still united?”; the United Kingdom, where four nations voted in different ways (Document A)

A question that makes you think, without waiting for an answer: a rhetorical question.

EntryWhich word in the question is challenged by the map of 2016?

Answer key

united.

Expression task

Now say it

Debate (activity 3): Should sixteen-year-olds vote in all UK elections?

EntryOne opinion with should and one figure.

Tools to use:

  • should, more than, higher than
  • if + past, would
  • turnout, register, referendum

Sentence starters:

  • I think sixteen-year-olds should ...
  • In 2014, the turnout was higher than ...
  • If they voted, ...
  • Although ..., ...

Model answer

If you can work and pay taxes at sixteen, why can't you vote? I think sixteen-year-olds should vote in all UK elections. In 2014, they voted in the Scottish referendum, and the turnout was higher than in 2016. If young people voted, politicians would talk more about education and climate. Although some say they are too young, they are already trusted in Scotland and Wales.

Going further (optional)

The éduscol question for this axis is “Un Royaume toujours uni ?”. Using Documents A and B, write 180 words: can a referendum divide a country as much as it decides?

Help: use the key words and the sentence starters above. Write one idea per line.

Write it in your planner before the bell. It is also posted on the school platform.

Model answer

Model plan: idea 1, 1215: the king accepts limits (Magna Carta). Idea 2, 1689: Parliament controls laws, taxes and the army (Bill of Rights). Idea 3, today: the Prime Minister depends on the Commons, and citizens decide in elections and referendums (2016, votes at sixteen).

Going further in Terminale

Who Rules Britain? Sovereignty, Crown and People is the advanced Terminale version of this sequence (tronc commun B2, with C1 tasks; a good bridge towards the spécialité LLCER, towards C1). It does not repeat these lessons: it starts from what students learnt here (Magna Carta, 1689, Bagehot, Royal Assent, the 2016 referendum, votes at sixteen) and takes them further, with unadapted primary sources, Dicey, the 2019 prorogation case, the 2022 Scottish referendum ruling, a mid-sequence test and a written exam.

A further sequence in Terminale, The Queen Who Had to Be Seen: Elizabeth II, Image and Power, starts from Bagehot’s dignified power (already studied here, especially in lesson 9) and asks how Elizabeth II used her image as its instrument. It does not repeat the two-column sort, the reign-or-rule exercise, or the extract read here.
