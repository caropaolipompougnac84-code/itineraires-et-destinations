Look at the painting

Describe, then interpretCore

  1. What do you see in the foreground, in the middle and in the background?
  2. Look at the ship at the quay. Why is it painted with strange shapes?
  3. What mood does the painter create?
Answer key
  1. Green hills in the foreground, a ship, a quay and factories in the middle, the water and distant hills behind.
  2. It is “dazzle” camouflage, used in the First World War to confuse enemy submarines.
  3. Calm, glowing, almost peaceful, although the harbour had just been destroyed by an explosion.

In December 1917, a ship carrying explosives blew up in Halifax Harbour, killing nearly 2,000 people. In 1918, the Canadian War Memorials Fund asked the British painter Harold Gilman to paint the harbour in wartime. It was his largest painting, and one of his last: he died in 1919.

Document 1 · Pier 21

Pier 21, an immigration shed on the Halifax waterfront, opened on 8 March 1928 and closed in 1971. Nearly one million immigrants, refugees, war brides and evacuee children came through its halls before travelling across Canada by train. During the Second World War, about 500,000 Canadian soldiers left for Europe from Pier 21.

After the war, some 48,000 war brides, mostly young British women who had married Canadian soldiers, and 22,000 of their children came to Canada, most of them through Pier 21. Many “displaced persons” followed, among them survivors of the Holocaust. In 1951 alone, almost 94,000 people arrived there. Today, Pier 21 is the Canadian Museum of Immigration.

Sources: The Canadian Encyclopedia, “Pier 21”; Government of Canada, opening of the Canadian Museum of Immigration at Pier 21, 2011; Canadian Museum of Immigration at Pier 21.

UnderstandCore

  1. When did Pier 21 open and close?
  2. Name four groups who arrived there.
  3. Who were the war brides?
  4. Why did Pier 21 close? Make a hypothesis.
Answer key
  1. It opened on 8 March 1928 and closed in 1971.
  2. Immigrants, refugees, war brides and evacuee children (also displaced persons).
  3. Mostly young British women who had married Canadian servicemen during the war.
  4. Air travel replaced ships: by 1970, fewer than 1,200 immigrants arrived in Halifax.

Note pour l'enseignante
