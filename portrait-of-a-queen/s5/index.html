Lesson 5. Aberfan, 21 October 1966: the face she did not show

The Crown, Season 3, Episode 3, "Aberfan", and the real disaster.

Key question: Could the Queen have reacted differently, and what does duty allow a sovereign to feel?
Lesson of 55 minutes, timed for 52 minutes: Recap 5 min · Teacher’s briefing 8 min · Document 8 min · Vocabulary 7 min · Grammar 11 min · Figures and idioms 6 min · Expression task 7 min.

1The teacher’s briefing8 min

For the teacher

Judging a reaction in the past calls for forms students often find difficult: the past perfect simple and continuous (what had happened before), past modals (reproach, deduction: should have gone, might have felt, can't have known), the third and mixed conditionals, and I wish / If only + past perfect for regret. Start from the question: Should she have gone earlier?

Starting point

The tip had been growing for years. She should have gone earlier. If she had gone at once, people would have felt comforted.

Past perfect simple and continuous

  • Simple: an earlier completed event. When she arrived, the rescuers had already recovered the bodies.
  • Continuous: a long process before a past moment. It had been raining for days before the tip slid.

Modals in the past

  • Reproach: should have / ought to have + past participle; needn't have (it was unnecessary but done).
  • Deduction: must have (certain), might / may / could have (possible), can't / couldn't have (impossible).

Third and mixed conditionals

  • Third: If she had gone earlier, she would have been praised.
  • Mixed (past cause, present result): If the Coal Board had listened, those children would be grandparents today.

Regret

I wish / If only + past perfect: If only the tip had been removed. It's (high) time + past: It's high time the truth was told.

Help: should have + past participle = it was a mistake not to do it.

2The document8 min

Official video: What Happened At Aberfan? This Is The Full Story | The Crown (Still Watching Netflix, Netflix's official channel, YouTube)
An official Netflix video (2019, about 12 minutes) telling the real story behind Season 3, Episode 3. No official clip of the episode itself is published.

The facts. On Friday 21 October 1966, shortly after 9 a.m., a colliery spoil tip above the Welsh mining village of Aberfan slid down the mountainside after days of heavy rain and buried Pantglas Junior School and nearby houses. 144 people died, 116 of them children. The official tribunal of inquiry blamed the National Coal Board.

Prince Philip and Lord Snowdon went to the village quickly; the Queen went on 29 October, eight days after the disaster. Her delay was criticised. Years later her former private secretary, Lord Charteris, admitted that the advice had been his: "We told her to stay away until the preliminary shock had worn off", and he called it the biggest regret of her reign (BBC News). She kept a promise to return, opened the new school in 2012, and in 2016 recalled the posy a girl had given her in 1966, inscribed "from the remaining children of Aberfan".

Fiction and history. The episode imagines a Queen who cannot cry in public and asks herself whether something is wrong with her. This inner struggle is the screenwriters' interpretation: nobody knows what she felt. The disaster, the dates and the delay are historical.

Understanding the document

  1. What caused the disaster?
  2. Why were most victims children?
  3. Who was found responsible?
  4. Why was the Queen criticised?
  5. Which part of the episode is fact and which is interpretation?
Answers
  1. A spoil tip of mining waste, saturated by days of rain, slid onto the village and the school.
  2. The tip buried Pantglas Junior School during morning lessons.
  3. The National Coal Board, according to the tribunal of inquiry.
  4. She waited eight days before visiting, while others went at once.
  5. Facts: the disaster, dates, the delay. Interpretation: her private feelings and her inability to cry.

4Grammar in practice

From recognition to open production.

RecognitionExercise 3. Meaning of the modal

Reproach, certainty, possibility or impossibility?

  1. She should have gone at once.
  2. The parents must have felt abandoned.
  3. She might have wanted to avoid distracting the rescuers.
  4. She can't have known the scale of the disaster immediately.
  5. They ought to have removed the tip.
Answers
  1. reproach
  2. certainty (deduction)
  3. possibility
  4. impossibility (logical deduction)
  5. reproach

Controlled practiceExercise 4. Past perfect simple or continuous?

Put the verbs in the right form.

  1. It (rain) ______ for days when the tip slid.
  2. Residents (warn) ______ the Coal Board before 1966.
  3. By the time she arrived, the rescuers (work) ______ for a week.
  4. The children (just / start) ______ lessons.
  5. Nobody (imagine) ______ such a tragedy.
Answers
  1. had been raining
  2. had warned
  3. had been working
  4. had just started
  5. had imagined

TransformationExercise 5. Third and mixed conditionals

Rewrite.

  1. She didn't go at once. She was criticised.
  2. The tip wasn't removed. The children died.
  3. The Board didn't listen. Aberfan still mourns today.
  4. She is a reserved person. She didn't show her grief.
Answers
  1. If she had gone at once, she wouldn't have been criticised.
  2. If the tip had been removed, the children wouldn't have died.
  3. If the Board had listened, Aberfan wouldn't still be mourning today. (mixed)
  4. If she weren't so reserved, she would have shown her grief. (mixed)

TransformationExercise 6. Regret

Use I wish / If only.

  1. I regret that she didn't come earlier.
  2. The village regrets that nobody listened.
  3. I'm sorry I didn't watch the whole episode.
  4. We regret the tip was there.
Answers
  1. I wish she had come earlier.
  2. If only somebody had listened.
  3. I wish I had watched the whole episode.
  4. If only the tip hadn't been there.

5Figures of speech

  • Euphemism: "we lost them", "those who are no longer with us".
  • Litotes: "It was not a small thing for her to go there."
  • Pathos: appealing to emotion through detail (a school bag, a clock stopped at 9.15).

Exercise 8. Transform

Rewrite each sentence with the figure given.

  1. She was very upset. (litotes)
  2. The children died. (euphemism)
  3. Many people died. (pathos)
Answers
  1. She was not unmoved. / She was far from indifferent.
  2. The children were lost. / The children passed away.
  3. A row of small coats still hung on the pegs of the classroom.

6Idioms

a stiff upper lip
the habit of hiding one's emotions in difficult situations.
First recorded in American English in the early 19th century, later associated with British reserve.
She kept a stiff upper lip throughout.
to put on a brave face
to look calm or happy when one is not.
Idiomatic use of face as appearance.
The mothers put on a brave face for their other children.
to be at a loss for words
to be unable to speak because of strong emotion.
Neutral, slightly formal.
Even the Prime Minister was at a loss for words.
to bite one's tongue
to stop oneself from saying something.
Literal image of keeping the tongue between the teeth.
She bit her tongue and said nothing.

Exercise 9. Which idiom?

Complete.

  1. He wanted to protest but he ______.
  2. Faced with such grief, I was ______.
  3. British reserve is often summed up as ______.
  4. Despite her pain, she ______.
Answers
  1. bit his tongue
  2. at a loss for words
  3. a stiff upper lip
  4. put on a brave face

7Expression task7 min

Hot seat. One student plays the Queen in 1967, the others are journalists. They ask why she waited; she answers with past modals and conditionals. Then the class votes: was the criticism fair?

Exit ticket · formative check
  1. Reproach: She (go) ______ earlier.
  2. Third conditional: If it (not rain) ______, the tip (not slide) ______.
  3. How many children died at Aberfan?
Answers
  1. She should have gone earlier.
  2. If it hadn't rained, the tip wouldn't have slid.
  3. 116 (out of 144 victims).
