Introduction

Can a country remember? Through a World Heritage site, songlines, three artworks, a public apology, languages, sport, music and a sports event, students meet Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander cultures as they live today, and record a museum audio guide in English.

Our question

Can a country remember?

Cultural note. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander readers are advised that this sequence contains names and voices of people who have died. In this sequence we write Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples (plural: many peoples and languages) and Country with a capital C for the land, waters and sky a people belongs to.

The skill tags. Every activity shows its skill: CO listening (compréhension de l'oral), CE reading (compréhension de l'écrit), PO speaking (expression orale), PE writing (expression écrite). Every lesson works the four skills.

The Gorse spiral of the sequence

Six questions, one per stage of the sequence, each one narrower than the last, until the point: the question the students answer in their final audio guide and text.

  1. 1What is heritage?What we receive and choose to pass on: a channel, a story, a skill (Budj Bim).
  2. 2How can land remember?Songlines connect places, peoples, earth and sky: knowledge held by its custodians.
  3. 3How can art remember?A painting can hold a Country, a family, a language: we see, we source, we interpret with care.
  4. 4What can words do for the past?In 2008, an apology named the harm in public. Words recognise; they do not repair everything.
  5. 5Is a culture only in the past?No: languages, sport and music are alive today.
  6. 6Can a shared event help?A Games can invite, promise and leave a legacy, if people follow up.

At the point. Can a country remember? A country remembers when its people choose to listen to those whose story it is, to keep it and to pass it on: the question each student answers in the final audio guide and the final text.

The six directives of the programme, and where they are worked

1. Le passé dans le présent

« Comment le patrimoine culturel, matériel et immatériel, témoigne-t-il du passé ? »

Axis 3 of the 2025 programme, with its suggested topic « La culture aborigène dans l'Australie contemporaine ». Tangible and intangible heritage in lesson 1, a country's lessons from a dark page in lesson 4, living cultures in lesson 6; link with axis 6 (the Commonwealth) in lesson 7.

2. Grammar in context

« s'effectue en situation, à la fois dans les activités de compréhension et d'expression, ainsi que dans des temps de réflexion sur la langue, d'explicitation et de conceptualisation »

Each Language focus starts from the document of the day (UNESCO, the Apology, a museum label, the Census), states the rule, practises it and grades it.

3. Sounds and spellings

« Elle met en évidence les liens ou les écarts entre graphie et phonie. »

A pronunciation point in every lesson: /h/ and silent h, /eɪ/ and /aɪ/, the -ate ending, sentence stress, /θ/ and /ð/, list intonation.

4. Lexis in a cultural setting

« L'acquisition du lexique consiste en la mémorisation de mots et d'expressions lexicalisées et idiomatiques toujours en lien avec un environnement culturel et une situation de communication. »

Heritage, Country, custodian, elder, to pass on, to acknowledge, legacy: flashcards in every lesson and « My memory file ».

5. Authentic documents

« Les documents authentiques s'adressent à des locuteurs natifs dans des contextes réels, non pédagogiques. »

A Gunditjmara elder, the Sydney Opera House, the Parliament of Australia, a government LiDAR project, Baker Boy, the 2018 Games, a curator of the National Museum of Australia, UNESCO and the Australian Bureau of Statistics.

6. The written record

« la trace des activités effectuées et corrigées en classe [...] occupe une place fondamentale ainsi que les bilans lexicaux et grammaticaux. »

A « Written record » box in every lesson, a self-check grid after every graded check, and the « Taking a correction » routine.

Objectives

  • Explain what heritage is, tangible and intangible, with a real World Heritage site.
  • Understand authentic Australian voices: an elder, a guide, a Prime Minister, a rapper, a curator.
  • Describe a work of art, separate what we see, what a source says and what we interpret.
  • React to an official text with a reason and a limit, without a ready-made opinion.
  • Read numbers and facts carefully: a number can rise while a share falls; a promise is not a result.
  • Record a clear museum audio guide: rhythm, stress, intonation.

CE · A channel and a story CE

For students.

Budj Bim Cultural Landscape (UNESCO World Heritage List, 2019)

1 The Budj Bim Cultural Landscape, located in the traditional Country of the Gunditjmara people in south-eastern Australia, consists of three serial components containing one of the world's most extensive and oldest aquaculture systems.

2 The Budj Bim lava flows provide the basis for the complex system of channels, weirs and dams developed by the Gunditjmara in order to trap, store and harvest kooyang [...]. The highly productive aquaculture system provided an economic and social base for Gunditjmara society for six millennia.

3 The Budj Bim Cultural Landscape is the result of a creational process narrated by the Gunditjmara as a deep time story, referring to the idea that they have always lived there. From an archaeological perspective, deep time represents a period of at least 32,000 years.

4 The ongoing dynamic relationship of Gunditjmara and their land is nowadays carried by knowledge systems retained through oral transmission and continuity of cultural practice.

PO · Explain to a visitor PO

For students.

  1. Choose a channel or a story. Explain in four sentences: What is it? Who passes it on? How is it heritage? What do you still want to know?
  2. Listener: ask « How do you know? » and repeat one detail. Swap after two minutes.

Answer key (teacher)

Possible answer: A channel is tangible heritage. The Gunditjmara built it to trap eels. Knowledge has passed through generations. UNESCO shows that heritage is a living relationship, not just an old object. Observe clarity, use of evidence and a question; do not mark personal opinions.

PE · My first museum note (diagnostic) PE

For students.

Write 50 to 70 words for a visitor who thinks heritage is only old buildings. Use Budj Bim, name the people and the source, give one example of knowledge and finish with a question. Make two flashcards: heritage and Country.

Answer key (teacher)

Model: Heritage is not only a building. Budj Bim is Gunditjmara Country in south-eastern Australia. According to UNESCO, people developed channels to trap eels. Language focus · Present perfect with for and since; heritage; /h/ PEPO

For students. Language focus: the short lesson.

Grammar

From then until now: subject + have/has + past participle. « People have lived here for thousands of years. » For + duration (for six millennia); since + starting point (since 2019). « Budj Bim has been on the World Heritage List since 2019. » The action or state links the past to now. Compare French: « Ils vivent ici depuis... » does not use the same tense.

Lexis

Heritage = what we receive from the past and pass on. Tangible: a stone channel. Intangible: a song, knowledge. A legacy, an ancestor, a descendant; to preserve, to pass on. Use peoples for different communities and Country for an Aboriginal relationship to place. There is not one single Aboriginal culture.

Pronunciation

/h/ is breath, not a French silent h: heritage /ˈherɪtɪdʒ/, history /ˈhɪstəri/. Hold a hand before your mouth and feel air. But honour /ˈɒnə/ and hour /ˈaʊə/ have a silent h. Contrast: a history / an honour.

Practice.

  1. Choose for or since: Budj Bim has been listed ___ 2019. People have passed on knowledge ___ many generations.
  2. Complete: The Gunditjmara ___ (live) here for thousands of years.
  3. Listen and repeat: heritage, history, honour, hour. Which two begin with a vowel sound?

Answer key (teacher)

since; for; have lived. Honour and hour begin with a vowel sound. Repeat without adding /h/ to them.

Graded language check (5 minutes, /6).

  1. Complete: Budj Bim has been listed ___ 2019. (1)
  2. Complete: People have passed on knowledge ___ six millennia. (1)
  3. Complete: The site ___ (be) on the List since 2019. (1)
  4. Correct: People has lived here for generations. (1)
  5. Give one example of intangible heritage from today. (1)
  6. Which has a silent h: history or honour? (1)

Answer key and marking (teacher)

1 since. 2 for. 3 has been. 4 People have lived here for generations. 5 a song / oral knowledge / a story (one). 6 honour. Each item: 1 point. For a whole sentence, 0.5 if the target structure is right but another error remains. Total /6. The sound item tests sound awareness; actual pronunciation is observed in the paired speaking.

Written record

Copy into your notebook.

Heritage can be tangible (a channel) or intangible (knowledge). Budj Bim is Gunditjmara Country. People have passed on knowledge for generations. Homework

Work at home.

Emotional storm

No student has to represent a community or disclose their origins. Use the documents, not classmates, as evidence. Explain the warning about names of people who have died. A student may choose a neutral landscape example if the subject is difficult.

Emergency exits (plan B)

  • Early finish: explain why six millennia is not the same date as 32,000 years (different evidence and claims).

Language focus · Contrast connectors; promises and action; list intonation PEPO

For students. Language focus: the short lesson.

Grammar

But joins two clauses: « The Games ended, but the plan promised a legacy. » Although introduces a concessive clause: « Although the Games ended, the commitments remained. » Do not add but after although. However links separate sentences: « The plan is a promise. However, we need evidence of results. » B1+: whereas contrasts two facts within a sentence.

Lexis

A member, a host, a commitment, a legacy, a workforce, procurement (buying goods/services); to recognise, to respect, to celebrate, to implement, to follow up. Distinguish a promise in a 2018 plan from evidence of what happened later.

Pronunciation

List intonation: « recognise ↗, respect ↗ and celebrate ↘ ». The first items rise, the final item falls. Pause between chunks, not after every word. A contrast can start a fresh chunk: « However, / we need evidence. »

Practice.

  1. Join with although: The Games ended. The commitments remained.
  2. Correct: Although the plan is ambitious, but we need evidence.
  3. Say « jobs, training and culture » with rises then a final fall.

Answer key (teacher)

Although the Games ended, the commitments remained. Although the plan is ambitious, we need evidence. jobs ↗, training ↗ and culture ↘.

Taking a correction

A corrected paper is not a verdict: it is a map that shows where to go next. This step is done after every assessment, in class or at home, and it counts in the sequence mark.

Six steps

For students.

  1. I read all the comments before I look at my mark again.
  2. I compare my work with the criteria, line by line: what I have, what is missing.
  3. I find my errors with the code in the margin (table below).
  4. I rewrite three sentences, correctly, in another colour.
  5. I write what I keep: one success I want to repeat.

Emotional storm and emergency exits

Speaking a foreign language in front of others, talking about yourself or your future, hearing your own voice on a recording: all this can bring strong emotions (anger, shame, nervous laughter, tears, blocking). This tab gives simple moves for the student and for the teacher.

Exercise 1. My inner weather (2 minutes)

For students.

I choose my weather: sunny, cloudy, rainy, stormy. I write one sentence: « Today I feel ... because ... » I keep it, or I give it to my teacher.

Exercise 2. Square breathing (1 minute)

For students.

I breathe in for 4. I hold for 4. I breathe out for 4. I hold for 4. Three times, feet flat on the floor. This exercise is optional.

Exercise 3. Give the feeling to a character (5 minutes)

For students.

I choose a person from the sequence (Uncle Ossie Lovett, Rhoda Roberts, Baker Boy, Margo Neale) or « Kim », an imaginary student guide at a museum. I write three lines: how could this person feel just before speaking in public, in front of a big audience? What could help them? Three intensities: a little nervous, quite nervous, very nervous.

Exercise 4. The bridge sentence (in an assessment)

For students.

If I am stuck: I breathe, I read the question again, I underline the key word, I go on to the next question and I come back later. I write at least one short answer for each question, even if it is not complete.

In this sequence, watch out for

  • Identity (all lessons): never ask a student about their origins or ask an Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander student, or any student, to speak for a people. Students speak about the documents.
  • Family separation (lesson 4): the Stolen Generations can touch students who have lived a placement, an adoption or a separation. Announce the topic, keep to the short extract, never ask for personal stories, and offer the Budj Bim alternative task. The infirmière, the CPE and the PsyEN are the people to see.
  • Laughter at names, songs or beliefs (lessons 2, 3): stop calmly and recall the rule: we describe with respect; we do not have to share a belief to understand why it matters.
  • Debates on current politics (lessons 4, 7): keep to the documents and to the language of reasons and limits; no current referendum, party or vote is discussed in class.
  • Being recorded (lessons 5, 8): recordings are made in pairs, kept on school devices, deleted after marking; a very anxious student can record alone with the teacher, at an appointment.

The lessons in one line

  1. Lesson 1. What is heritage? A living relationship. Diagnose listening, reading, speaking and writing without counting a global mark; distinguish tangible and intangible heritage; meet the Gunditjmara and define Country without treating Australia as one culture.
  2. Lesson 2. Land and memory: a network of songlines. Understand Rhoda Roberts explaining a public account of songlines; read about a community-led museum exhibition; use relatives to explain a custodian and a place.
  3. Lesson 3. Art as memory: what can we see, what can we infer?. Distinguish observation, sourced fact and interpretation; meet Sally Gabori, Tony Albert and Judy Watson through museum pages; draft a cautious audio-guide paragraph.
  4. Lesson 4. What can « sorry » do? Recognising the past at B1. Identify recognition, harm and repair in a short 2008 extract; distinguish what an apology says from what it can achieve; react with one reason and a question, without the Terminale timeline.
  5. Lesson 5. Mid-sequence assessment: explain a heritage place. Show your progress in the four skills (separate marks for CO, CE, PO and PE) and in language (grammar, lexis, pronunciation); begin to correct your work with clear criteria.
  6. Lesson 6. Living cultures today: languages, sport, music. Take your correction of the mid-sequence assessment; understand Baker Boy's message to young Australians; read facts about languages, sport and music today; contrast a past belief with a present trend.
  7. Lesson 7. Australia in the Commonwealth: can a Games make a promise?. Understand a Yugambeh elder's invitation and a reconciliation plan for the 2018 Commonwealth Games; distinguish a promise from evidence of results; link Axis 3 with Axis 6 (the Commonwealth).

> Hello. My name is Aunty Rose, and I work at a cultural centre in Victoria. Visitors often ask me: « What is Aboriginal heritage? Is it only in museums? » I always say no. Compréhension de l'écrit (/10), CE

Texte d'entraînement (environ 2 500 signes), écrit pour la classe à partir des documents de la séquence (Songlines, excuses de 2008, cultures vivantes, Jeux du Commonwealth). Le jour d'une évaluation officielle, prendre un document authentique inconnu de 2 300 à 4 000 signes, en libre accès.

> Can a country remember? In Australia, that question is not only about history books. It is about land, language and who is allowed to speak. For many Aboriginal communities, memory is carried by songlines: paths across the country that are also stories, laws and maps. A song can tell you where water is, and what your responsibilities are when you walk there. In museums, visitors often look at objects in silence. In a living culture, the same object may still have a job to do. > > In February 2008, Prime Minister Kevin Rudd said « sorry » in Parliament to the Stolen Generations. The speech was historic, and it was televised across the country. Some Australians hoped that one word would close a dark page. Many Aboriginal leaders answered that an apology is a door, not a destination. Recognition matters; so do languages still spoken, sports clubs, music, and the right to decide how heritage is shown. > > The Commonwealth Games have sometimes promised respect for First Nations. A ceremony, a flag, a welcome to country can be genuine, or they can be a show for visitors. The real test comes later: are languages funded, are sites protected, are young people heard? A museum audio guide, if it is written with the community, can help a visitor look properly. If it is written without them, it may repeat the old habit of speaking about people instead of with them.

Source : texte d'entraînement écrit pour la classe.

  • Présentez le document (nature, thème, question posée).
  • Qu'est-ce qu'une songline, et que transmet-elle ?
  • Que se passe-t-il en 2008, et comment ce geste est-il lu ?
  • Quel rôle les Jeux du Commonwealth peuvent-ils jouer, et quelle est la limite de ce rôle ?
  • Selon le texte, à quelle condition un audioguide de musée est-il utile ?

Corrigé (version prof). Le texte d'entraînement pose la question « Can a country remember? » et relie mémoire, terre, langue et droit à la parole. Les songlines sont des chemins qui sont aussi des récits, des lois et des cartes ; un chant peut indiquer l'eau et les responsabilités. En février 2008, Kevin Rudd présente des excuses aux Stolen Generations ; le texte souligne que, pour beaucoup de responsables autochtones, ces excuses sont une porte, non une fin. Les Jeux du Commonwealth peuvent témoigner du respect, ou n'être qu'un spectacle ; le vrai test est le financement des langues, la protection des sites, la parole des jeunes. Un audioguide n'aide le visiteur que s'il est écrit avec la communauté, non à sa place.
