Introduction card

Expanding your vocabulary: the eight axes

General track, tronc commun: B1+ towards B2 in Première, B2 in Terminale · spécialité LLCER: towards C1 by the end of Terminale. The lighter version for the technological track is here.

AudiencePremière and Terminale, general pathway, English as first or second foreign language.
LengthTwo lessons of 55 minutes, timed for 52 minutes, each opened by a marked recap, two homework banks with answer keys and a short marked assessment.
ObjectivesBuild a word bank for each of the eight axes of the programme. Use the right collocation and the right register. Build new words with prefixes and suffixes. Link ideas in an essay. Understand and use metaphorical language. Remember words for good with a notebook and spaced review. Grammar tied to each step: verb patterns, word classes, the passive and concession.
Link with the programmeThe programme for Première and Terminale (official bulletin of 22 January 2019) is built on one theme divided into eight axes, and at least six axes are studied each year. It asks for vocabulary learnt in context, organised in semantic fields linked to the axes, and reused from one sequence to the next. This workshop follows that order: the axes first, the methods in their service. From the start of the 2026 school year, Terminale follows the new modern-languages syllabus (arrêté of 5 May 2025, BO no. 22 of 29 May 2025). The banks remain a useful map; teachers can also attach the words to the corresponding themes of the 2025 syllabus.
Link with the bacThe bac assesses four activities: understanding a recording, understanding a written text, writing, and speaking. In each one, precise words make the difference: you understand the document faster, you summarise it in your own words, you argue with nuance, and you speak without searching for words.
Before this workshopBig Brother Is Watching, Artificial Intelligence and Fahrenheit 451, Learning in the Age of AI and Through the Lens. The recaps draw on these sequences.
MaterialsThe gap-fill worksheet of the session (paper or ENT). A dictionary, paper or online. A vocabulary notebook.

Why learn words by axis?

A word learnt alone is forgotten in a week. A word learnt in a family of ideas comes back when you need it. When the bac subject is about social networks, you open your “Citizenship and virtual worlds” bank, and the words are there, ready, with their collocations.

“Social media is bad because there are a lot of lies on it.”

“Social media can fuel misinformation: a false claim may go viral long before anyone has time to fact-check it.”

The second sentence says the same thing, but it is precise, it sounds natural, and it shows the examiner that you master the topic.

Method 1 · Collocations: words that go together

A collocation is a group of words that native speakers naturally use together. You can understand “make a research”, but it sounds wrong: we say carry out research. Learn verbs and nouns as pairs, never alone.

Verbgoes withExample in an axis
raiseawareness of, concerns aboutProtest art raises awareness of injustice.
posea threat to, a challenge toDeepfakes pose a threat to democracy.
takeresponsibility for, measuresPlatforms must take responsibility for harmful content.
strikea balance betweenLaws must strike a balance between safety and privacy.
playa key role inMemorials play a key role in collective memory.
drawattention toThe photograph draws attention to rural poverty.
meeta need, a demand, a deadlineRamps meet the needs of wheelchair users.
reachan agreement, a consensusScientists have not reached a consensus yet.

Grammar point. Many collocations are followed by a preposition, and after a preposition, a verb takes the -ing form: responsibility for protecting users, a key role in shaping opinion, concerns about losing jobs.

Method 2 · Register: formal or informal?

An essay is written in a formal register. A conversation with a friend uses an informal register. The same idea can be expressed in both.

InformalFormal, for the essay
a lot ofa great deal of (uncountable), numerous (countable)
kidsyoung people, children
getobtain, receive, become
find outdiscover
really baddeeply harmful
think aboutconsider
I thinkIt could be argued that
But, at the start of a sentenceHowever,
don’t, can’tdo not, cannot

Grammar point. Formal English often uses the passive or an impersonal structure to sound objective: It is often argued that…, Personal data is collected without our knowledge. Form: be + past participle.

Grammar point: word classes. The place in the sentence tells you which form to use: after an article or an adjective, a noun (a growing inequality); after be or before a noun, an adjective (the law is unfair); to describe a verb or an adjective, an adverb (deeply unfair).

Method 5 · Metaphorical language

English often talks about abstract ideas with images. Using one well-chosen image per paragraph makes your writing vivid. Learn them by families of images:

ImageExpressionsAxis
information is watera flood of information, to be drowning in notifications, to filter the news4, Citizenship and virtual worlds
society is a buildingthe pillars of democracy, to build bridges, to break down walls1 and 7
the past is a woundthe scars of history, to heal old wounds, a painful chapter8, Territory and memory
ideas are plantsto sow the seeds of doubt, an idea takes root, a movement blossoms3, Art and power
technology is a tool with two sidesa double-edged sword, a blessing and a curse6, Scientific innovation and responsibility

Trap. Too many images kill the image. One per paragraph is enough, and never mix two families in the same sentence.

Remembering words for good

  • A personal example: write each new word in a sentence about your own life. A sentence you invented is remembered far better than a sentence you copied.
  • A notebook in four columns: word, family, collocation, my sentence. Add the axis number in the margin.
  • Spaced review: read your list again on day 1, day 3, day 7 and day 21. Each time, hide the word and find it from your sentence.
  • Recycling: reuse five words from the previous axis in each new sequence, as the programme asks.
  • Your voice: record yourself saying the words and your sentences on your phone, and listen to them on the way to school.

Answer key

Game · Word Family Relay (6 minutes)

Teams of four in front of the board. The teacher writes a root. Each player in turn runs to the board and adds one word of the family (noun, adjective, verb, adverb, opposite), then hands the pen on. One point per correct word, one bonus point if the team uses one of its words in a sentence about an axis.

Roots: power, equal, create, connect, remember, sustain.

Game answer key

  • power: powerful, powerless, empower, empowerment, powerfully.
  • equal: equality, inequality, unequal, equally, equalise.
  • create: creation, creative, creativity, creator, recreate.
  • connect: connection, connected, disconnect, disconnection, reconnect.
  • remember: memory, memorial, memorable, commemorate, commemoration.
  • sustain: sustainable, sustainability, unsustainable, sustainably.

Bonus sentence example: “Social media can empower minorities, but it can also disconnect us from real life.”

Reuse · write, then speak

An essay paragraph (15 lines, at home or in class)

Choose one subject. A. Should social networks be held responsible for what their users post? (axis 4) B. Why do societies build memorials? (axis 8)

Success criteria: five words from the axis card, two collocations, two linking words, one image, one passive, a formal register throughout.

Model answer

Model paragraph, subject A. Social networks are often described as neutral platforms. However, it could be argued that they cannot escape responsibility for what is published on them. Their algorithms are designed to make content go viral, and misinformation, which is more shocking than the truth, spreads faster than any correction. Moreover, victims of cyberbullying are left powerless when hateful messages are not removed. Although moderation has a cost, these companies earn billions from our personal data. Therefore, they should be required to fact-check and remove harmful posts, so that the pillars of democracy are not undermined.

Checklist: axis words (go viral, misinformation, cyberbullying, personal data, fact-check); collocations (escape responsibility for, remove harmful posts); linking words (However, Moreover, Although, Therefore); image (the pillars of democracy); passives (are designed, are not removed, should be required, are not undermined).

The one-minute oral

Present one axis in one minute, as if you were starting your oral at the bac: the key question, three words from the card, one example from a sequence studied in class, your opinion. Use two linking words and one image. A classmate times you and ticks the words used.

Model answer

Model, axis 5, Fictions and realities. “The axis Fictions and realities asks how stories shed light on the real world. A good example is Fahrenheit 451: in 1953, Ray Bradbury depicted a society where books were burnt and people were glued to giant screens. At the time, it seemed far-fetched. However, today we spend hours on our phones, so the novel reads like a cautionary tale. In my opinion, dystopias are not predictions, they are warnings: they sow the seeds of doubt in our minds, and that is exactly why we need them.”

About 90 words: one minute at a calm pace. Words from the card: depict, far-fetched, a cautionary tale, a dystopia. Linking words: However, so. Image: sow the seeds of doubt.

Short marked assessment · 10 minutes · out of 10

Check your progress

In class, without notes. Vocabulary and grammar from both sessions.

  1. Vocabulary (2 marks). Give the axis of each word: a memorial, propaganda, gender equality, a carbon footprint.
  2. Collocations (2 marks). Complete: to ______ a threat to; to ______ attention to; to ______ research; to ______ tribute to.
  3. Word formation (2 marks). Give the noun: include, aware, responsible, equal.
  4. Grammar: the passive (2 marks). Rewrite: “The regime censored the film.” “People share millions of photos every day.”
  5. Grammar: concession (2 marks). Join with although, then with despite: “Robots are useful. They destroy jobs.”

Assessment answer key

  1. a memorial: 8, Territory and memory; propaganda: 3, Art and power; gender equality: 2, Private and public space; a carbon footprint: 6, Scientific innovation and responsibility. Half a mark each.
  2. pose; draw; carry out; pay. Half a mark each.
  3. inclusion; awareness; responsibility; equality. Half a mark each.
  4. The film was censored (by the regime). Millions of photos are shared every day. One mark each.
  5. Although robots are useful, they destroy jobs. Despite being useful, robots destroy jobs (or: Despite their usefulness, robots destroy jobs). One mark each.

My bristol flashcards · how to use them

Principle: each student makes their own cards by hand, on bristol cards. Writing the word is already a way of learning it. The pack stays in their bag: they can revise anywhere, with no screen. The notebook in four columns and the spaced review on this page go together with the bristols.

Materials

A7 bristol cards (74 × 105 mm), or A6 bristols cut in half. Coloured felt-tip pens. An elastic band or an envelope for the pack. A small cardboard box with three compartments for practice (a shoe box with two dividers is enough). You can also print the blank template and cut it out.

A shows the front, B gives the meaning and a sentence. If the card is right, it moves on one box. If it is wrong, it goes back to box 1. Target before the assessment: 12 cards in box 3.

Items to put on the cards (not a ready-made deck)

Choose the words that are still fragile. The general track offers six words or phrases per axis; the technological track offers four. Suggested fronts, among others:

  • Axis 1 · Identities and exchanges: a sense of belonging; to settle (in); a culture shock (general); to move abroad; a newcomer; a border (technological).
  • Axis 2 · Private and public space: gender equality; household chores; remote work.

  • Axis 4 · Citizenship and virtual worlds: misinformation; personal data; cyberbullying; fake news; to go viral; to check a source.
  • Axis 5 · Fictions and realities: a dystopia; a cautionary tale; a role model; a warning.
  • Axis 6 · Scientific innovation and responsibility: a breakthrough; to carry out research; ethical concerns; renewable energy; pollution.
