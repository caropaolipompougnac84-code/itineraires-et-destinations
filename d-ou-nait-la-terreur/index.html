2. Problématique

D’où naît la terreur ? Where does terror come from? Parfois du groupe, quand une communauté entière accuse (The Crucible) ; parfois de l’intérieur, de l’ambition puis de la culpabilité (Macbeth) ; parfois du doute, quand on ne sait plus si l’on rêve (Le Horla, La Vénus d’Ille).

Lesson 1 (lancement de la saison) : Where does terror come from: from the others, from inside us, or from doubt? Puis chaque séquence, dans l’ordre choisi par la classe.

3. Objectifs linguistiques

  • Exprimer la cause : comes from, is caused by, because of (lesson 1).
  • Faire des déductions : it may be, it must be, it can’t be (lesson 1).
  • Décrire un tableau : in the foreground … in the background …, on the left … on the right …
  • Lexique de la peur : terror, a crowd, a rumour, guilt, to haunt, doubt, to beware, a shadow.
  • Language activities : compréhension de l’écrit (quatre citations courtes), compréhension de l’image (quatre tableaux), expression orale en continu (présenter un tableau en une minute), interaction orale (débat : d’où naît la terreur ?).

4. Ancrage culturel

La terreur collective dans l’Amérique puritaine, de Salem (1692) au maccarthysme (The Crucible, Arthur Miller, 1953) ; l’Angleterre de Jacques Ier et l’Écosse des sorcières (Macbeth, Shakespeare, vers 1606) ; le fantastique français et l’hésitation selon Todorov (Le Horla, Maupassant, 1887 ; La Vénus d’Ille, Mérimée, 1837). Œuvres : Rimmer, Ensor, Fuseli, Goya.

  • Français : Seconde et Première ; bac pro et CAP.

Chers collègues,

Pour cette saison d’Halloween, j’ai voulu que mes séquences se répondent. Quatre œuvres, deux langues, une seule question : d’où naît la terreur ?

Parfois elle naît du groupe, quand une communauté entière accuse. Parfois de l’ambition et de la culpabilité d’un seul homme. Parfois du doute, quand on ne sait plus si l’on rêve. Les tableaux ouvrent chaque séquence, parce que la peur naît souvent d’une image.

Chaque séquence existe en plusieurs versions, dont une Dys-friendly version pour les élèves dys et neuroatypiques. Promenez-vous, prenez votre temps, et faites dialoguer les textes avec vos classes.

Lesson de lancement

The full spirale de Gorse · Lesson 1: Where does terror come from?

The first turn always starts from what strikes you. On each turn, the idea is outside the spiral and the line from the text is inside. Four turns, then the pointe. Your oral keeps turns 1, 2 and 5.

Text studied: Four short lines and four paintings: Miller, The Crucible (1953); Shakespeare, Macbeth (about 1606); Maupassant, Le Horla (1887); Mérimée, La Vénus d’Ille (1837); with Ensor, Fuseli, Goya and Rimmer

  • ““the little crazy children are jangling the keys of the kingdom, and common vengeance writes the law!”” (Arthur Miller, The Crucible, Act II, John Proctor)
  • ““Out, damned spot! out, I say!”” (William Shakespeare, Macbeth, Act V, scene 1, Lady Macbeth)
  • “« Je suis perdu ! Quelqu’un possède mon âme et la gouverne ! » (I am lost! Someone owns my soul and rules it!)” (Guy de Maupassant, Le Horla, 1887)
  • “CAVE AMANTEM. « Je traduirais donc : Prends garde à toi si elle t’aime. » (So I would translate: Beware if she loves you.)” (Prosper Mérimée, La Vénus d’Ille, 1837, the narrator reads the statue’s inscription)

1 Je remarque · I noticeSalemWhat strikes you first?
“common vengeance writes the law!” (The Crucible, Act II); in Ensor’s The Intrigue, a crowd of masks presses forward.
In Salem, terror comes from the group: rumour and revenge become the law, and nobody can see the real faces behind the masks.
Start from what strikes you.
2 Je repère · I spot the cluesMacbethWhich clues? In which line?
“Out, damned spot! out, I say!” (Macbeth, V.1); in Fuseli’s painting, Lady Macbeth walks in her sleep with a candle.
The spot is invisible: the blood is in her mind. Terror comes from inside, from guilt that does not let her sleep.
Quote each clue with its reference.
3 Je comprends · I understandLe HorlaWhat effect? What contrast?
« Je suis perdu ! Quelqu’un possède mon âme et la gouverne ! » (Le Horla, 1887).
The narrator cannot see the Horla, so he doubts everything, even his own mind: terror comes from not knowing what is real.
Explain the effect of a contrast.
4 J’anticipe · I predictVénusWhat is my hypothesis? Is it confirmed?
Hypothesis: in these works, terror always comes from a real monster.
Not confirmed: in La Vénus d’Ille the narrator only reads a sign, CAVE AMANTEM, « Prends garde à toi si elle t’aime » ; the Horla is invisible, and in Salem the “witches” are neighbours. Often there is no monster we can see.
Check a hypothesis in the text.
5 Je trouve le sens · I find the meaningAllWhich interpretation do I defend?
“common vengeance writes the law!”, “Out, damned spot!”, « Quelqu’un possède mon âme » ; in Rimmer’s Flight and Pursuit, a man runs from a shadow.
Terror comes from the others (the group in Salem), from inside (Macbeth’s guilt) and from doubt (the Horla, the Venus). Rimmer shows all three: we never know who is chasing the man, maybe himself.
Link the analysis to the key question.

Lesson 1: Where does terror come from? · Dys-friendly version

Question: Where does terror come from: from the others, from inside us, or from doubt?

Four words:

  • terror: a very strong fear
  • a crowd: a large group of people
  • a rumour: a story that people repeat, true or not
  • guilt: the feeling that you did something wrong

One line to read: ““the little crazy children are jangling the keys of the kingdom, and common vengeance writes the law!”” (Arthur Miller, The Crucible, Act II, John Proctor)

Steps: 1. Read the line aloud. 2. Learn the four words. 3. Do the spiral below, one step at a time. 4. Say your answer with the support sentence.

If a student cannot attend class or is in hospital

Lesson 1: Where does terror come from?. Your lesson at home or in hospital, about 40 minutes, at your own pace. Stop when you are tired: you can do it in two parts.

  1. Read the key lines aloud, slowly, twice:
    • ““the little crazy children are jangling the keys of the kingdom, and common vengeance writes the law!”” (Arthur Miller, The Crucible, Act II, John Proctor)
    • ““Out, damned spot! out, I say!”” (William Shakespeare, Macbeth, Act V, scene 1, Lady Macbeth)
    • “« Je suis perdu ! Quelqu’un possède mon âme et la gouverne ! » (I am lost! Someone owns my soul and rules it!)” (Guy de Maupassant, Le Horla, 1887)
    • “CAVE AMANTEM. « Je traduirais donc : Prends garde à toi si elle t’aime. » (So I would translate: Beware if she loves you.)” (Prosper Mérimée, La Vénus d’Ille, 1837, the narrator reads the statue’s inscription)
  2. Learn the eight words of the Vocabulary box of the lesson: terror, a crowd, a rumour, guilt, to haunt, doubt, to beware, a shadow.
  3. Do the spirale de Gorse: copy the five steps on your worksheet (or use the worksheet of this lesson). For each step, write one idea and one line from the text. Then check below.
  4. Quick check: Complete with: terror, crowd, rumour, guilt, haunts, doubt, beware, shadow.
    • In Salem, a ______ spreads from house to house.
    • In Ensor’s painting, a ______ of masks comes towards us.
    • Lady Macbeth cannot sleep because of her ______.
    • The memory of the murder ______ her.
    • The narrator of Le Horla lives in ______: is it real?
    • The inscription on the statue says: ______ if she loves you.
    • In Rimmer’s painting, a ______ follows the running man.
    • ______ is stronger than fear.
  5. Send to your teacher through the school’s online messaging: look at the four paintings of the season page (Ensor, Fuseli, Goya, Rimmer) and read the four lines. Write 100 words: where does terror come from in two of the works? Use comes from, because of, and one line from each work.
