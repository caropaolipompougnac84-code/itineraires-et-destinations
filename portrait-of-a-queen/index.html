The Queen Who Had to Be Seen: Elizabeth II, Image and Power

How does a Queen who reigns but does not rule exercise power through her image?

Level
Terminale, general pathway · tronc commun: B2 · spécialité LLCER anglais: towards C1 by the end of Terminale, with C1 tasks. Official wording from BO spécial no. 1 of 22 January 2019.
Enquiry
How does a monarch without executive power rule through images? Students analyse real speeches and real portraits, then study The Crown as fiction, compared with the historical record.
Curriculum
Aligned with the same curriculum reference as Who Rules Britain? (theme 6, the United Kingdom and its nations, and the study topic on political speech). The three sequences can be taught one after the other: The King Reigns, Parliament Rules, then Who Rules Britain?, then this one.
Lessons
Eight lessons of 55 minutes, timed for 52 minutes, plus a mid-sequence assessment after lesson 4, a summative written test and a final task. Each lesson has a stopping point, an 8-minute emergency exit, and three visible outcomes: a written record, participation (everyone speaks at least once), and homework for the next lesson.
Assessment
Mid-sequence test after lesson 4 (/20). Summative written test, 2 hours (/20, coefficient 2). Final task: gallery talk, op-ed or commission letter (/20, coefficient 2). Teacher marking grids and student self-correction grids are on the assessment pages.

You already know. If you have studied The King Reigns, Parliament Rules (Bagehot’s dignified and efficient parts, reign or rule, the three rights of the sovereign) and Who Rules Britain? (Bagehot’s own words, a harder sort, and the idea that royalty lives on mystery), do not start again from the beginning. This sequence does not repeat those documents or those exercises. It starts from what you know and asks a new question: how did Elizabeth II use her image as the instrument of dignified power?

Method: analysing a royal portrait

This five-step method is used in every lesson on images. Paintings still under copyright (Annigoni, Freud, Beaton, Sutherland) are shown in class from the official museum pages, enlarged on screen. They are not reproduced on this site.

StepQuestionsUseful language
1. IdentifyWho painted or photographed it, when, for whom (the commission), where is it now?commissioned by, sitter, sittings, on display at
2. DescribeFormat, framing, pose, gaze, light, colour, background.full-length / head-and-shoulders, three-quarter view, gazing into the distance, in the foreground
3. Read the symbolsAttributes of power: crown, diadem, robes, orb, sceptre, landscape, map.regalia, the Garter robes, emblem, to symbolise, to convey
4. InterpretWhat image of monarchy is constructed: divine, maternal, eternal, human, ageing?idealised, unflinching, iconic, timeless, a myth of
5. ReceptionHow was it received, and what does that reveal about the period?it divided critics, it was hailed as, it was dismissed as

Language for argument

Use these structures in your speaking and writing (not as isolated drills). Practise them in every production task.

  • Concession. Admittedly…; It is true that…; nevertheless…; even so…
  • Hedging. It could be argued that…; appears to; may; seems to.
  • Hypothesis. If the monarchy had remained invisible, …; If she had spoken sooner, …

My bristol flashcards · how to use them

Principle: each student makes their own cards by hand, on bristol cards. Writing the word is already a way of learning it. The pack stays in their bag: they can revise anywhere, with no screen. There is no ready-made deck.

What to put on the cards (choose at least 15)

  • Vocabulary. dignified, efficient, reverence, legitimacy, sitter, commission, regalia, idealised, unflinching, iconic, mask of youth, litotes, understatement, anaphora, performative, imperial, mediation, disclaimer.
  • Language for argument. Admittedly…; nevertheless…; It could be argued that…; If she had…; a concession and a rebuttal.
  • Devices. antithesis (“long or short”); litotes (“not … with undiluted pleasure”); anaphora (“we will… we will… we will…”); metaphor of the family.
  • Short official lines (check the wording on royal.uk before you copy them): the 1947 vow; the 1992 “Annus Horribilis”; “your Queen and as a grandmother”; “we will meet again”.

Front: the English item. Back: a short definition or a translation, and a precise example from this sequence, not only a translation. Practise with three boxes. Target: 12 cards in box 3.
