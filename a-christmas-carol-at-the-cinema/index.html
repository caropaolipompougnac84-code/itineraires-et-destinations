🎩 Victorian Times : Step into 1843

To understand Scrooge, you need to walk in his streets: smoky factories, crowded cities, workhouses for the poor and children at work.

🕰️ Dated landmarks

  • c. 1760 to 1840 : The Industrial Revolution: steam engines, factories and railways. Millions of people move from the countryside to towns like Manchester and London.
  • 7 February 1812 : Charles Dickens is born in Portsmouth.
  • 1824 : Aged 12, Dickens works in a blacking (shoe-polish) factory while his father is in a prison for debtors.
  • 1833 : Factory Act: no children under 9 in textile mills; children aged 9 to 13 may work up to 9 hours a day.
  • 1834 : Poor Law Amendment Act (the “New Poor Law”): poor people get help mainly by entering a union workhouse.
  • 1837 : Victoria becomes queen. Dickens publishes Oliver Twist in monthly parts (1837 to 1839).
  • 1842 : Mines and Collieries Act: women, girls and boys under 10 may no longer work underground.
  • February 1843 : A government report on children working in trades and factories shocks Dickens.
  • September 1843 : Dickens visits the Field Lane Ragged School, a charity school for very poor children in London.
  • 5 October 1843 : Dickens gives a speech in Manchester on education for working people. Soon after, he starts writing the story.
  • 1843 : The first commercial Christmas card is printed in London (Henry Cole and John Callcott Horsley).
  • 19 December 1843 : A Christmas Carol is published by Chapman & Hall, with colour illustrations by John Leech. The first 6,000 copies sell out by Christmas Eve.
  • 1847 : Factory Act (“Ten Hours Act”): women and young people aged 13 to 18 may work 10 hours a day at most.
  • 9 June 1870 : Dickens dies. The same year, the Elementary Education Act opens the way to schools for all children in England and Wales.

🏚️ The workhouse

After 1834, parishes joined together in “unions” and built workhouses. Poor people received food and a bed, but they had to work hard (breaking stones, picking old ropes), wear a uniform and follow strict rules. Families were separated: men, women and children slept in different parts of the building. Life inside was made deliberately harder than the poorest life outside, so that people would avoid it.

👦 Child labour

In the 1840s, many children worked long days in mills, mines, workshops and streets. In mines, young children opened and closed air doors in the dark for hours. In mills, they crawled under machines to tie broken threads. Dickens wanted readers to see these children: Ignorance and Want are the Spirit’s warning about poor children without school or food.

🏭 Work and money in the Carol

Bob Cratchit earns fifteen shillings a week and copies letters in a cold “dismal little cell”. Scrooge keeps the coal-box in his own room.

Picture Analysis · Image A (Ghost of Christmas Present)

Wide shot of a room full of food. The giant Spirit sits in the centre, very large, crowned with holly, holding a torch high: he is the light source and dominates the frame (like a low angle). Scrooge is small, at the edge, in his nightgown, hands joined: he looks humble. In the foreground, a feast : fruit, meat, puddings. Meaning: abundance and generosity; Christmas as sharing. Link to concept: the image shows the joyful, generous Christmas Dickens wanted, in contrast with Scrooge’s cold life.

Picture Analysis · Image B (Ignorance and Want)

Medium-wide shot in a dark, cold space. Foreground: two thin children in rags, barefoot, staring. Middle: Scrooge, bent, his hand open in surprise or protest. Background: the transparent Spirit and a city with a factory chimney. Light is pale and cold. Meaning: poverty is hidden behind the city’s wealth; the children are the result of industrial society. Link to concept: child poverty and lack of education in 1843 : “Beware them both”.

Picture Analysis · Image C (The Last of the Spirits)

The black, shapeless Spirit occupies the frame, taller than everything, without a face; only one hand points. Scrooge kneels, hiding his face, small and white against the dark. Foreground: the gravestone “EBENEZER SCROOGE”. Background: a closed wall and iron railings like a prison. Contrast black/white. Meaning: death, judgement, fear. Link to concept: the man who wanted the poor to die now faces his own lonely death.

Picture Analysis · Image D (Mr Fezziwig’s Ball)

Wide shot with an oval frame. In the centre, Mr and Mrs Fezziwig dance, smiling, in bright colours (yellow, red, white). Around them, a circle of guests of all ages; a fiddler plays on a small platform in the background; mistletoe hangs at the top centre. Warm light, full space. Meaning: community, joy, a generous employer. Link to concept: Dickens’s model of the Christmas of shared joy and of a good master : the opposite of Scrooge with Bob.

Oral · Final task : Ideas for “A place among others”

Gifts money cannot buy: time (Fred’s visits), joy (Fezziwig’s party), love and loyalty (the Cratchits), a second chance (the Spirits). Why receiving is hard: Scrooge would have to admit he needs others, lose his control and his pride; accepting Fred’s invitation on Christmas Day is his real change. His place: Scrooge’s place is first only his counting-house; Marley’s chain and the lonely grave show a life with no place among people; at the end he is “a second father” to Tiny Tim and welcome at Fred’s table. The heart of it: “Will you let me in, Fred?” is a request to belong; Fred’s welcome touches what Scrooge is, not what he has. Richer or poorer? Scrooge spends money but gains friends, a family and joy: he is richer in what he is. George: he has no money left but receives the help of the whole town; Harry calls him “the richest man in town” because he is loved. OUR THREAD : Finding one’s place in the world

Two Faces of Christmas: redemption and the worth of a life

Scrooge has lost his place among others: he has hurt people and must redeem himself to find it again. George Bailey has nothing to atone for: he must discover the place he already holds, and that his life matters.

🧭 Finding one’s place in the world · Young people today

Scrooge shuts himself away from everyone, “solitary as an oyster”; George Bailey comes to believe that his life is worth nothing. Today, many young people give their time to others : in clubs, associations, food drives : because they too are looking for a place among others. Why can giving help someone find their place?

🎁 Inside the thread: Giving and Receiving : what we ARE, not what we HAVE

We can only give and receive from a place.

Scrooge learns to GIVE
He has money but gives nothing. The Spirits show him who he has become. He learns to give time, joy and presence.
George learns to RECEIVE
He gives everything: his dreams, his travels, his savings. When he is lost, the town gives back to him. He learns that he is loved for who he is.

Both men measure their life by money at the darkest moment. Both discover that a life’s worth is not in a bank account.

🎩 Dickens & Victorian England (1843)

London, 1843. Since the Poor Law of 1834, the poor get help mainly in workhouses. Children work in mills and mines. Charles Dickens publishes A Christmas Carol on 19 December 1843 to make readers look at the poor.

Scrooge is a rich, lonely moneylender. Three Spirits show him his past, the present Christmas of others and his own lonely death. On Christmas morning he changes his life.

The story

Christmas Eve, just after the Second World War, in Bedford Falls, a small town in New York State. George Bailey dreamed of travelling the world and building big things, but he stayed home to run the Bailey Brothers Building and Loan, which lends money to working families so they can buy a house. When $8,000 of the company’s money is lost, George faces ruin and despair. An angel, Clarence, “angel second class”, shows him what Bedford Falls would be like if he had never been born: a hard, dark town called Pottersville, ruled by the greedy banker Mr Potter.

🕰️ Landmarks

  • October 1929 : the Wall Street Crash starts the Great Depression: banks fail and millions lose their jobs and savings. In the film, a run on the bank nearly destroys the Building and Loan, and George and Mary give their honeymoon money to save it.
  • 1933 : President Franklin D. Roosevelt launches the New Deal.
  • 1941 to 1945 : the United States fights in the Second World War. In the film, George’s brother Harry becomes a war hero.
  • 1944 : the GI Bill helps returning soldiers to study and to buy homes.
  • 1946 : the film comes out the year after the war, for an America that has known crisis and loss.

MODEL DIALOGUE

🌙 The Night of Places Found Again

Model dialogue by Madame Carole Paoli Pompougnac

The setting: A place outside time, on the border between their two eras. A rough wooden table, a flickering candle. Outside, thick snow falls in silence. It is Christmas night. George Bailey and Ebenezer Scrooge sit face to face, just after their spiritual visits (Clarence for one, the three Spirits for the other).

GEORGE BAILEY (passing a tired hand over his face)

I nearly gave up everything tonight, Mr Scrooge. I thought my place no longer existed. That the earth would be better off if I had never set foot on it. When you spend your life sacrificing yourself for a community, for the rights of the little people against giants like Potter, you end up forgetting yourself. You become a brick in the foundations of other people’s houses, but you no longer have a roof of your own.

EBENEZER SCROOGE (his voice trembling, his eyes softened by terror and grace)

And I, George, spent my life building a fortress to shut myself off from the world. I thought my place was above men, in my great account book. Society? The poor? Their struggles? I handed all that over to the institutions. When two gentlemen asked me to give for the poor, I answered: “Are there no prisons?”📖 Dickens, A Christmas Carol, Stave I · Project Gutenberg #46 and then: “And the Union workhouses?”📖 Dickens, A Christmas Carol, Stave I · Project Gutenberg #46 What madness… The spirit of my old partner Marley came to shatter my certainties. I was free, but I was a ghost among the living.

GEORGE BAILEY

So that is our disappointment? I was let down by the weight of duty and sacrifice, and you by the utter emptiness of gold. I wanted to die because I felt crushed by my place; you were dying slowly because you refused yours.

EBENEZER SCROOGE

Precisely. Those spiritual guides had to tear us out of our present to show us the reflection of the world without us. They forced us to look at the invisible. Did you see what became of your town without your battles, George?

GEORGE BAILEY (shivering)

A land of exploitation. Pottersville. A place without a soul, without solidarity, where people tear each other apart. That is when I understood. My place was not a prison: it was a lighthouse. And you, what did you see?

EBENEZER SCROOGE (a tear in his eye)

A deathbed that everyone had deserted, an abandoned grave where no one wept. And above all, the home of my clerk Cratchit, broken by the loss of his son, little Tim, because I had not known how to hold out my hand. My wealth was nothing but a crime against mankind. The Spirit of Christmas made me understand that our true business on this earth is not trade: it is charity, patience, the gift of oneself.

GEORGE BAILEY (smiling faintly)

The gift of oneself… It’s a strange balance. The more you give, the emptier you sometimes become. But tonight, when I thought I was going to prison, the whole town came and emptied their pockets to save me. My family, my friends, the workers… It was at that very moment, at Christmas, that the tree I had planted bore its fruit.

EBENEZER SCROOGE (rising, his eyes bright)

Because Christmas is the mirror of our humanity, George! My nephew Fred said it to my face, one Christmas Eve, and I did not want to hear it. Christmas, he said, is “the only time I know of, in the long calendar of the year, when men and women seem by one consent to open their shut-up hearts freely, and to think of people below them as if they really were fellow-passengers to the grave, and not another race of creatures bound on other journeys.”📖 Dickens, A Christmas Carol, Stave I (Fred) · Project Gutenberg #46 Finding one’s place is not about taking up physical space or a rank in society. It means agreeing to be part of the chain.

GEORGE BAILEY (standing in turn and holding out his hand)

Then what do you wish for now, Mr Scrooge?

EBENEZER SCROOGE (gripping George’s hand firmly)

“I will live in the Past, the Present, and the Future!”📖 Dickens, A Christmas Carol, Stave V (first promised in Stave IV) · Project Gutenberg #46 I want to mend what is broken, support those who struggle, and make my money serve life at last. And you, George?

GEORGE BAILEY (looking towards the light growing in the distance)

I just want to go home. Hold my children, kiss my wife, and accept my place. Not as a burden… but as the finest of privileges. The place of a man among his own people.

(They look at each other with mutual respect. Christmas bells begin to ring in the distance, abolishing space and time. Each walks away towards his own destiny, at last in his place.)

🔑 Answer Keys

Cross table : expected ideas

Scrooge is guilty and must atone: his change is a redemption (he learns to give). George has done good all his life: his change is a revelation of his own value (he learns to receive). Both have a supernatural guide and see a dark alternative world; in both, money is not the measure of a life.

Duel 1 · Judgement or care?

Leech: the Spirit is a huge black shape without a face, standing over Scrooge; one hand points to the grave; Scrooge kneels and hides his face: fear, judgement, guilt. Publicity photo: Clarence stands beside George under a lamp, his hand gently on George’s shoulder; George sits wrapped in a blanket, worried but protected: care, kindness, help. to Scrooge must face his faults; George must be comforted and shown his worth.

Duel 2 · Alone with money

Scrooge sits alone by a small fire, in a dark room, surprised by the ghost and his chain (the weight of a life spent on money). George sits in Mr Potter’s rich office with a cigar in his hand and a little skull on the desk: the temptation of money and comfort, and a sign of death. to Both images show that money can imprison a man.

Duel 3 · The joy of a community

Both images show a crowd in movement, smiling faces, music and dancing; the joy is shared, not bought. Fezziwig is a generous boss; George and Mary are young people in a community. to Happiness comes from what people are together.

Final task A · model answer

“Scrooge loses his place among people because of money, and finds it again at Fred’s table; George thinks he has no place, but the town shows him he has always been at its heart. George is rich at the end, not in money but in friends: Harry calls him ‘the richest man in town’ and Clarence writes ‘no man is a failure who has friends’. Scrooge discovers that he can give time and joy, like Fezziwig, whose happiness is ‘quite as great as if it cost a fortune’. To me, receiving is harder for George, because he has always been the one who helps; accepting the town’s money means accepting that he needs others.”

Understanding the model : answer key
  1. In a place outside time, between their two eras, on Christmas night, by candlelight, with snow falling. Clarence visited George; the three Spirits visited Scrooge.
  2. He has given everything to his community and forgotten himself. He is like a brick in the foundations of other people’s houses: he holds them up, but he has no roof of his own, no place of his own.
  3. “Are there no prisons?” and “And the Union workhouses?” (Stave I). They show that he put himself above the poor and handed them over to institutions instead of seeing them as people: he had a rank, not a place among others.
  4. George saw Pottersville, “a place without a soul, without solidarity”. Scrooge saw a deserted deathbed, a grave where no one wept, and the Cratchit home broken by the loss of Tiny Tim.
  5. Scrooge: finding one’s place means “agreeing to be part of the chain”; he will “live in the Past, the Present, and the Future”. George: his place is “a lighthouse”, “the finest of privileges”, “the place of a man among his own people”.
Final task B · model dialogue (extract)

Scrooge: Young man, I was once a man who said the poor should ‘decrease the surplus population’. George: I always gave to others, but I never knew how to accept help. Scrooge: A ghost showed me my own grave. George: An angel showed me a town without me, called Pottersville. Scrooge: Now I know that a man is worth what he gives. George: And what he can receive. I thought I had no place in the world. Scrooge: You had the biggest one, my friend.
