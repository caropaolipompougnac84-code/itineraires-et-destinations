What we need to say

In this lesson, you need to tell the story of past campaigns: what happened, and when.

To tell the steps of a campaign, we need these words: candidate, primary, caucus, convention, running mate, debate.

To tell what happened in the past without naming who did it, we need the past passive: Abraham Lincoln was chosen in Chicago in 1860. The banners were printed in 1876.

Documents

Key document

The campaign takes almost two years. Candidates announce that they want to run. To be president, you must be at least thirty-five years old, a citizen born American and a resident for at least fourteen years. From January to June of the election year, each party organises primaries (secret votes) or caucuses (party meetings) in every state to choose its candidate. The winners get delegates. In summer, from July to early September, each party holds a national convention: the delegates choose the candidate, who announces a running mate. In September and October, the candidates meet in debates. The next presidential election is on 7 November 2028.

Read the source: USAGov, Presidential election process · USAGov, Primaries and caucuses · National Archives, The Constitution, Article II

Grammar to say it: the passive voice (3), the past

Why we need it

To tell what happened in the past without naming who did it, we need the past passive: Abraham Lincoln was chosen in Chicago in 1860. The banners were printed in 1876.

Grammar point: board explanation

Teacher script
  1. Ask: When was Lincoln chosen? Write the answer: Lincoln was chosen in 1860.
  2. Compare on the board: is chosen (today, a rule) / was chosen (1860, finished). Explain: only be changes tense.
  3. Ask: was or were? Make two columns: one thing → was, several → were.
  4. Explain the past simple with finished dates and time markers: in 1860, last year, two years ago, then.
  5. Explain in, on, at with times: in May, in 1860; on 18 May; at nine o'clock.
  6. Students copy, then do the quick check on mini whiteboards.
Board: students copy

The passive voice (3): the past; past simple and time prepositions

Rule
Past simple passive = was / were + past participle.
Form
Lincoln was chosen in 1860. The banners were printed in 1876. Question: Was Lincoln chosen in Chicago? Negative: Seward was not chosen.
Use
To tell past events when the result matters: history, news reports.
Examples from the lesson
  • Abraham Lincoln was chosen in Chicago in May 1860.
  • The engraving was published by Harper's Weekly.
  • The delegates were elected in the primaries.
Common mistakes
  • Lincoln is chosen in 1860. → Lincoln was chosen in 1860 (finished date).
  • The banners was printed. → The banners were printed.
  • in 18 May → on 18 May; on 1860 → in 1860.
French and English
French uses the passé composé: Lincoln a été choisi en 1860. English uses the past simple with a finished date: Lincoln was chosen in 1860, not has been chosen.
Also on the board: Prepositions of time
in + month, year, season: in May, in 1860, in summer. on + day, date: on Tuesday, on 18 May. at + time: at nine o'clock, at night. ago after the period: two years ago.

Figures of speech: the sports metaphor

Learn

American politics often speaks about elections as a race: this is a sports metaphor. A candidate runs for president, the leader is the front-runner, a surprise candidate is a dark horse. In 1860, William Seward was the front-runner, but the convention chose Abraham Lincoln.

  1. What does the race for the White House mean?
  2. What does to run for president mean?
  3. Who is the front-runner?
  4. What is a dark horse?
  5. Neck and neck means very close. Why is it a horse-racing image?
  6. Write one sentence about a school election with a sports metaphor.
Answer key
  1. The competition to become president.
  2. To be a candidate.
  3. The candidate who is winning before the vote.
  4. A candidate nobody expected to win.
  5. Two horses run so close that their necks are at the same level.
  6. Example: Sara is the front-runner, but Tom is a dark horse.

Finer grammar: past perfect passive and the passive with getGoing further

Rule

Past perfect passive: had been + past participle, for an action before another past action: When the convention opened, the delegates had already been chosen. Informal passive: get + past participle: He got elected in 1860.

  1. When the convention opened, the delegates / already / choose.
  2. By November 1860, Lincoln / nominate / for six months.
  3. Seward / expect / to win, but he / beat.
  4. The banner / print / before the campaign started.
Answer key
  1. When the convention opened, the delegates had already been chosen.
  2. By November 1860, Lincoln had been nominated for six months.
  3. Seward had been expected to win, but he was beaten (got beaten).
  4. The banner had been printed before the campaign started.

Text analysis: the surprise of 1860Going further

In May 1860, the Republican Party met in Chicago in a temporary wooden hall nicknamed the Wigwam. Most observers expected Senator William Seward of New York to be nominated. Lincoln's supporters, however, worked hard to win the delegates who did not want Seward. On the third ballot, Lincoln was chosen. Six months later, he was elected president, and the country soon entered the Civil War.

  1. Find two past passives.
  2. Which word shows the contrast with what was expected?
  3. What does nicknamed mean?
  4. Why was the result a surprise?
  5. Find the time markers that organise the text.
Answer key
  1. to be nominated, was chosen, was elected: any two.
  2. however.
  3. given an informal name.
  4. Seward was the favourite; Lincoln was not expected to win.
  5. In May 1860, on the third ballot, six months later, soon.

Argued task (200 to 250 words, or a two-minute oral)Going further

Are primaries and long campaigns good for democracy? Compare with the French presidential campaign.

Steps

  1. Introduction: how long an American campaign lasts.
  2. Advantages: voters know the candidates, many debates.
  3. Drawbacks: cost, fatigue, money.
  4. Comparison with France.
  5. Conclusion.
Sentence starters
  • Whereas in France ..., in the United States ...
  • One advantage is that ...
  • A major drawback is ...
  • Had the campaign been shorter, ...
  • On balance, ...
Key points

Key points: campaigns last almost two years in the USA (USAGov); primaries let voters choose candidates; long and expensive; in France, parties organise primaries only sometimes and the official campaign is short; a nuanced conclusion with whereas and on balance.
