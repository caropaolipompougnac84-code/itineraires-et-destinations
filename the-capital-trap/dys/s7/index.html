Lesson 7. Planning a road trip

Go West: travel along Route 66, avoid the Springfield trap, and tell the trip in the preterite.

Road map

Lesson of 55 minutes, timed for 52 minutes. 7 lessons, plus a written and an oral assessment.

  1. What’s this? The Route 66 sign, in silence first 10 min
  2. The Route 66 trap: two Springfields, and Oklahoma City 10 min
  3. Grammar: the preterite, Lucas’s trip, and the sound of -ed 14 min
  4. Prepare the road trip with cards E, then rewrite it in the past 18 min
  • Written record. A short itinerary in the preterite, with at least three cities and one capital, for example: “First, we started in Chicago. Then we drove south along the Mississippi. Finally, we arrived in New Orleans.”
  • Participation. Everyone speaks at least once: each pair presents one stage of their trip in the past, in two or three sentences.
  • Homework for next lesson. Write the postcard (80 to 100 words) for the written assessment, and prepare the oral with the checklist on the student sheet: four cities, two capitals, four geography words, one comparison, careful pronunciation.

Stopping point. After the preterite exercise, keep at least a short start on the road-trip card so that homework is clear.

Emergency exit · 8 min. If time runs short at this stopping point, condense the end of the lesson inside the 52 minutes (this is not 52 plus 8). Dictate a short written record (two sentences). A quick round so that everyone speaks at least once (one sentence each). Write the homework on the board.

What’s this? · 10 min

Project the sign without saying anything at first. What’s this? Where is it? Why is it famous?

Route 66 links Chicago to Santa Monica, near Los Angeles. It is about 4,000 kilometres long and dates from 1926. It crosses eight states: Illinois, Missouri, Kansas, Oklahoma, Texas, New Mexico, Arizona and California.

The Route 66 trap · 10 min

Students trace the road on the National Atlas map (and on the rivers map if they follow the Mississippi first).

Route 66 goes through Springfield, Illinois, and Springfield, Missouri. Which one is a capital?

Answer key

Springfield, Illinois, is a capital. Springfield, Missouri, is not (the capital of Missouri is Jefferson City). Another capital on the road is Oklahoma City, which is both the biggest city and the capital. No trap this time.

The preterite · 14 min

Read Lucas’s trip. Students underline the verbs, then complete exercise 4. After that, sort the regular -ed endings.

Exercise 4. Lucas’s trip

Lucas ______ (fly) to Chicago. He ______ (visit) the city and ______ (take) photos of the lake. Then he ______ (drive) to Springfield, the capital of Illinois. In New Orleans, he ______ (eat) gumbo and ______ (listen) to jazz. At the end, he ______ (swim) in Key West.

Answer key

flew, visited, took, drove, ate, listened, swam

/t//d//ɪd/
walkedlistenedvisited
stoppedexplainedlanded
lookedstayedstarted

Rule: you hear /ɪd/ only after a t or a d sound.

Prepare the road trip · 18 min

Each pair draws a road-trip card (cards E), traces the itinerary, chooses three photographs from this sequence (or from a classroom atlas), and rewrites the trip in the past (exercise 5).

Model: First, we started in Chicago. Then we drove south along the Mississippi. Finally, we arrived in New Orleans.

The seven challenges.

  1. From coast to coast. Start in Los Angeles, finish in New York. Visit 2 capitals.
  2. Along the Mississippi. Start in Minneapolis, finish in New Orleans.
  3. The Sunshine Trip. Start in Atlanta, finish in Key West.
  4. The Wild West. Start in Denver, visit Utah and Nevada.
  5. The Northeast. Visit 4 capitals in New England.
  6. Island hopping. Start in Anchorage, finish in Honolulu (plane allowed!).
  7. Route 66. Start in Chicago, finish in Los Angeles. Find the 2 capitals on the way and avoid the Springfield trap.

Answer key, exercise 5

Open answers in the preterite. A complete itinerary names at least three cities and one capital, uses two or three connectors (first, then, finally), and, if possible, one comparison or one surprise fact (a name origin, Juneau, or a trap).

Written record. First, we started in Chicago. Then we drove south along the Mississippi. Finally, we arrived in New Orleans.

Homework is already the final task: the postcard, and the oral checklist.
