35. 27:14 to 27:43What is ironic about the name Concorde? What could Britain and France not even agree on?

Answer 35

Concorde comes from the Latin for harmony or agreement, yet there was « so much bickering and acrimony »; they could not agree on the spelling: Concord for the British, Concorde for the French.

36. 28:22 to 28:58The teams were « chalk and cheese or chalk and fromage ». Explain the expression and the joke, then describe the difference in lunch habits.

Answer 36

Chalk and cheese: completely different. « Fromage » is a bilingual joke. The British had a sandwich at their desk; the French had a main course with wine, and difficult subjects were kept until after lunch.

37. 29:07 to 29:28What technical problem did the two measurement systems cause?

Answer 37

The British used imperial measurements (feet, inches, yards), the French used the metric system, so the drawings were done in two sets.

39. 30:26 to 31:11Why couldn't Prime Minister Harold Wilson cancel Concorde? Explain « a suicide pact ».

Answer 39

De Gaulle was « an incredible enthusiast for the project »; the agreement said that a partner who pulled out could be sued and « had to pay all of the costs of the entire project ». A suicide pact: the two countries were tied together, leaving would be as ruinous as staying.

40. 31:42 to 32:37What went wrong with Boeing's swing wing? What did Boeing have to do?

Answer 40

The mechanism « became too heavy »: the engines were not powerful enough and there was not enough fuel. Boeing had to redesign the plane with a simpler double delta wing, « basically like a big Concorde ».

41. 33:30 to 34:02Prototype 001 is presented to the public with a promise. What is it, and what is the reality behind the scenes?

Answer 41

The promise: it will fly in 1968. The reality: many technological problems, the engines are not ready, and Concorde will not fly until 1969 at the earliest.

42. 34:06 to 34:19What were « the marching orders from Moscow »?

Answer 42

« You will fly in 1968 », as part of a huge propaganda effort presenting the Tu-144 as the world's first supersonic transport.

Grammar lesson: regret and hypothesis in the past

To express regret about the costs and quarrels of the partnership, and imagine another past, we need regret and hypothesis in the past
For the teacher: how to run it (8 to 10 minutes)
  1. Ask: what went wrong with the partnership? What should the governments have done?
  2. Board: should have, could / might have, must / can't have + past participle; If + past perfect, would have + past participle; I wish / If only + past perfect. Students copy.
  3. Stress that all these forms talk about a past that cannot be changed.
  4. Quick check, then exercises.
On the board, to copy

Rule

To criticise or regret the past: should have + past participle. For a possibility that did not happen: could / might have + past participle. For a deduction: must / can't have + past participle. For an unreal past, the third conditional: If + past perfect, would have + past participle. To wish: If only / I wish + past perfect.

Form

  • should have: The engineers should have solved the engine problems sooner.
  • could have: Concorde could have flown first if the engines had been ready.
  • must have: The Soviets must have known the dates of Concorde's first flight.
  • third conditional: If Concorde had flown in 1968, it would have been the first supersonic airliner in the air.
  • If only: If only we had started the engine tests earlier!

Use

  • To express regret about a past decision.
  • To imagine another history.
  • To write a letter of regret as a minister.

Examples from the lesson

  • “The engineers should have solved the engine problems sooner.”
  • “If Concorde had flown in 1968, it would have been the first supersonic airliner in the air.”
  • “If only we had started the engine tests earlier!”

Common mistakes

  • Not If Concorde would have flown first, ..., but If Concorde had flown first, ...
  • Not They should have solve it., but They should have solved it.
  • Not I wish we started earlier. (about the past), but I wish we had started earlier.

To say it: the inverted conditional

In formal English, If + had can be replaced by an inversion: If the engines had been ready = Had the engines been ready, Concorde would have flown in 1968. Same meaning: it did not happen.

Document en français pour la médiation

2 mars 1969 : « le grand oiseau vole »

Le dimanche 2 mars 1969, à Toulouse, de nombreux spectateurs et journalistes attendent le premier vol du prototype Concorde 001. Aux commandes, le pilote d'essai André Turcat. L'appareil décolle et vole pendant environ une demi-heure, train d'atterrissage sorti, sans dépasser la vitesse du son. À l'atterrissage, le pilote résume l'événement : « le grand oiseau vole ».

Le prototype britannique 002 décolle à son tour de Filton, près de Bristol, le 9 avril 1969, piloté par Brian Trubshaw. Concorde franchit le mur du son pour la première fois en octobre 1969. Sur le plan symbolique, la course est perdue : le Tupolev Tu-144 soviétique a volé le 31 décembre 1968. Mais pour les ingénieurs de Toulouse et de Bristol, c'est le début d'une aventure de plus de trente ans.

Texte rédigé pour l'activité, d'après Airbus, National Museums Scotland et Brooklands Museum.

Model mediation

On Sunday 2 March 1969, in Toulouse, the first Concorde prototype, 001, flew for the first time, with the test pilot André Turcat. The flight lasted about half an hour, with the landing gear down, and it stayed below the speed of sound. When he landed, the pilot simply said « the big bird flies ». The British prototype, 002, took off from Filton, near Bristol, on 9 April, with Brian Trubshaw. Concorde broke the sound barrier in October 1969. Symbolically, the race was lost, because the Soviet Tu-144 had flown on 31 December 1968, but for the engineers it was the start of an adventure that lasted more than thirty years.

Figures of speech in the documents

Metaphor and idiom

« It was like a suicide pact and they couldn't get out of it. »; « chalk and cheese or chalk and fromage »

The witness compares the treaty to a suicide pact: two partners tied together until the end, even if it destroys them. The idiom chalk and cheese, turned into chalk and fromage, is a bilingual pun about two very different nations.

EntryWhat is compared to a suicide pact?

Show the answers

The Concorde agreement between Britain and France.

CoreWhy is the image so strong, and what clause of the treaty does it refer to?

Show the answers

If either country withdrew, it could be taken to the international courts and had to pay all the costs: neither could leave, even when the costs exploded.

StretchFind a less dramatic way to describe the agreement, then compare the effect.

Show the answers

For example: The treaty made it very expensive to withdraw. This is more neutral, but it loses the feeling of being trapped.

Bac pro Aéronautique · technical task cards

Two measurement systems, one plane. Convert, then explain why it matters. 1 inch = 25.4 mm; 1 foot = 0.3048 m; 1 psi = 0.0689 bar; 1 pound = 0.4536 kg.

Avionics

Cables and connectors

A wiring drawing gives a cable run of 12 feet and a connector pitch of 0.1 inch. Convert both into metric units. Then explain in two sentences how a mix of units can cause a wiring error.

Expected answer

12 × 0.3048 = 3.66 m; 0.1 × 25.4 = 2.54 mm. If a technician reads inches as millimetres, a cable can be cut far too short or a connector chosen with the wrong pitch, so the circuit does not connect correctly.

Systems

Pressure

A hydraulic test sheet gives a pressure of 3,000 psi. Convert it into bar. Then write the safety sentence you would add to a bilingual test sheet.

Expected answer

3,000 × 0.0689 = about 207 bar. Safety sentence: « All pressures are given in psi and in bar; check the unit before you adjust the test bench. »

Structure

Fasteners

A rivet pitch is 0.75 inch and a panel weighs 22 pounds. Convert both. Then explain why the Concorde teams produced « Imperial set of drawings, metric set of drawings » (29:25).

Expected answer

0.75 × 25.4 = 19.05 mm; 22 × 0.4536 = about 9.98 kg, so about 10 kg. The British worked in feet and inches and the French in metres, so each team needed drawings in its own system to manufacture parts without conversion errors.
