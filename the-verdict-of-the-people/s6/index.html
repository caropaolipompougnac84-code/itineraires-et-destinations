Lesson 6. Beyond US Borders

Why might neighbouring countries care about who sits in Congress?

Road map

Lesson of 55 minutes, timed for 52 minutes. Lesson 6 of 7. Minutes 52 to 55 stay free for collecting work, moving and the change of room.

  1. Paired update from the results log 4 min (minutes 0 to 4)
  2. Locate Canada, Mexico and two Caribbean places 4 min (minutes 4 to 8)
  3. Read Source F; complete Exercise 6A 7 min (minutes 8 to 15)
  4. Two teacher readings of Source G; complete Exercise 6B 7 min (minutes 15 to 22)
  5. Compare and correct 7 min (minutes 22 to 29)
  6. Conditional workshop, Exercise 6C 7 min (minutes 29 to 36)
  7. Role-card preparation and paired challenge, Exercise 6D 8 min (minutes 36 to 44)
  8. Written record 4 min (minutes 44 to 48)
  9. Exit ticket: one fact and one hypothesis 2 min (minutes 48 to 50)
  10. Homework 2 min (minutes 50 to 52)
  • Written record. US elections matter beyond US borders, but consequences are not automatic: connect a verified change to a possible route to a decision and state a limit.
  • Participation. Everyone speaks at least once: each partner presents a role card and answers the challenge “Which part is established, and which part is your interpretation?”
  • Homework for next lesson. Prepare the radio discussion: role card, 45-second opening, two questions, a keyword card of 40 words maximum, draft of the briefing note.

Stopping point. Minute 44, after the role-card exchange.

Emergency exit · 8 min. If only eight working minutes remain, this replaces the unfinished plan; it is not added to it.
  1. Minutes 0 to 2: each partner gives one fact and one hypothesis.
  2. Minutes 2 to 5: complete the written record.
  3. Minutes 5 to 7: record the homework.
  4. Minute 7 to 8: hand in a role card with two sourced facts.

Objectives

Connect the powers of Congress with cross-border interests while separating established facts from predictions.

Language: if + present with may, might, could; second conditional as an extension.

Source F

Trade and political power

Student text, newly written from the Office of the US Trade Representative and the Constitution.

The United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement, or USMCA, entered into force on 1 July 2020. It concerns trade between the three countries and replaced NAFTA.

The US Constitution gives Congress powers over foreign commerce and over taxes and duties. However, the effects of an election are not automatic. The president, existing laws, negotiations and decisions by other countries also matter.

Canada and Mexico may therefore follow congressional elections, because changes in political support can affect the debate over trade. Other places in the Americas may focus on different issues. We need evidence before claiming that a whole country shares one concern.

Sources: USTR, United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement · National Archives, the Constitution.

Teacher’s note

The original sequence gave a specific account of the July 2026 USMCA joint review. The background sources above do not establish that outcome. If you add it, give the exact dated official statement and distinguish extension, continued operation and termination. A review is not an automatic expiry.

Exercise 6A

Read and distinguish

Label each statement: fact, hypothesis or unsupported certainty.

  1. USMCA entered into force on 1 July 2020.
  2. A new majority in Congress might influence trade debates.
  3. Every Canadian voter has the same view of US trade policy.
  4. Congress has constitutional powers over foreign commerce.
  5. An election result automatically fixes future prices.

Answer key

  1. Fact.
  2. Hypothesis.
  3. Unsupported certainty.
  4. Fact.
  5. Unsupported certainty.
Source G · listening

A reporter’s method

Original classroom script, read twice by the teacher. It is not an authentic broadcast. First listening: books closed, answer questions 1 and 2. Second listening: answer questions 3 to 5. The script is printed at the bottom of this page for the correction and for students working alone.

Exercise 6B

Listening

First listening

  1. Does the speaker say elections are unimportant?
  2. What distinction is the main message?

Second listening

  1. What three steps should the reporter follow?
  2. Name two other actors or factors.
  3. Which words signal uncertainty?

Answer key

  1. No.
  2. The difference between what has changed and what may happen next.
  3. Explain the change in Congress; explain how it could affect a decision; say what is still uncertain.
  4. The president, existing laws and the other countries.
  5. could, uncertain, may.
Exercise 6C

Hypotheses

Common B1+ route (if + present, may/might/could)

  1. If Congress the proposal, the debate change. (oppose, might)
  2. If a tariff increases import costs, prices rise. (possibility, not certainty)
  3. Rewrite cautiously: “If the opposition wins a chamber, all trade agreements will end.”

Towards B2 (second conditional)

  1. If I {G} a reporter, I {G} ask for the original evidence. (be, would)
  2. Write your own: “If I were reporting from Canada, I would…”

Answer key

  1. opposes; might.
  2. may, might or could.
  3. “If the opposition wins a chamber, it may affect support for some trade measures. That result would not by itself show that trade agreements will end.”
  4. were; would.
  5. Example: “If I were reporting from Canada, I would ask how the proposal could affect businesses.”

Counterfactual inversion (Had the party won…) is left out of this lesson on purpose: students need the time to prepare the final task.

Exercise 6D

Build a role card

Choose a role: Washington political reporter, Canadian or Mexican business reporter, Puerto Rico reporter, host.

My contributionMy notes
Two verified facts
The source of each fact
One reasonable hypothesis
How could this lead to the possible consequence?
One limit
One question for another speaker

A partner challenges the card: “Which part is established, and which part is your interpretation?”

Answer key

  • the facts match their sources;
  • the hypothesis is labelled as a hypothesis;
  • the route from fact to consequence is explained;
  • the speaker states a limit.

Accept different perspectives if they meet these criteria. Do not accept a national stereotype as a substitute for evidence.

Written record

US elections matter beyond US borders, but consequences are not automatic. A responsible explanation connects a verified development to a possible route to a decision and states a limit. We must separate established facts from hypotheses.

Exit ticket

Write one fact and one hypothesis about US trade with Canada or Mexico.

Homework

  1. Complete your role card.
  2. Prepare a 45-second opening contribution.
  3. Write two questions for other speakers.
  4. Bring a keyword card of no more than 40 words (source references not included).
  5. Draft your individual briefing note (see Lesson 7).
Source G · script for the correction

A US election can matter to Canada and Mexico, but an election result does not immediately change every trade rule. First, a reporter should explain what has changed in Congress. Next, the reporter should explain how this could affect a decision, for example through a new law. Finally, the reporter should say what is still uncertain. Congress is not the only actor. The president, existing laws and the other countries also matter. A careful report therefore separates what has happened from what may happen next.
