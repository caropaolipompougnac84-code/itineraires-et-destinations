Marked recap · 3 points

1. Give the dates of Magna Carta and of the Bill of Rights.

2. Who appoints the Prime Minister, and on what condition?

3. Which two nations voted Remain in 2016?

Recap answers

  1. 1215 and 1689.
  2. The King, if the person commands the confidence of the House of Commons.
  3. Scotland and Northern Ireland.

Option A. Oral (B1 to B2): a speech as a newly elected MP

You have just been elected MP. In three minutes, you explain to young voters who holds power in the United Kingdom and why their vote matters.

Step by step

  1. Greet your audience and introduce yourself.
  2. Explain how the King lost his power (1215, 1689).
  3. Explain who governs today: Parliament, the Prime Minister, the Commons.
  4. Give one recent example (2024, 2026 or 2016).
  5. Conclude with a call to vote.

Option B. Written (B2 to C1): an op-ed of 250 to 300 words

For a British student newspaper: “The King reigns, Parliament rules: is British democracy still built on Magna Carta?”

Step by step

  1. A title and a hook.
  2. Paragraph 1: Magna Carta and its limits.
  3. Paragraph 2: 1689 and the power of Parliament.
  4. Paragraph 3: today, the Prime Minister, the Commons and the citizens.
  5. A conclusion that answers the question with nuance.

Model op-ed (B2 to C1)

Model op-ed

Eight Centuries of Saying No to Power

Every year, a door is slammed in the face of the King's messenger at Westminster. It looks like theatre, but it tells the whole story of British democracy in one gesture: power has to knock and ask permission.

It started at Runnymede in 1215. King John, short of money and facing rebel barons, sealed a charter that put the king under the law. Magna Carta protected the barons rather than the people, and the Pope declared it void within weeks. Yet two of its clauses, no imprisonment without lawful judgment and no sale or delay of justice, are still law in England today.

The real turning point came in 1689. After a civil war and a king forced out, William and Mary accepted the Bill of Rights: no laws suspended, no taxes and no army in peace time without the consent of Parliament. From then on, the Crown reigned while Parliament ruled, and no monarch could govern alone again.

Today, the King reads a speech written by his Government and has not refused a law since 1708. Real power lies with the Prime Minister, who needs the confidence of the House of Commons. In July 2026, Andy Burnham became Prime Minister without a general election, simply because his party still held a majority. Voters choose MPs; MPs decide who governs.

So is British democracy still built on Magna Carta? Its spirit, yes: no one is above the law. But its walls were raised later, by Parliament and by voters, and they are still being built, as the debate on votes at sixteen shows.

Going further in Terminale

Who Rules Britain? Sovereignty, Crown and People is the advanced Terminale version of this sequence (tronc commun B2, with C1 tasks; a good bridge towards the spécialité LLCER, towards C1). It does not repeat these lessons: it starts from what students learnt here (Magna Carta, 1689, Bagehot, Royal Assent, the 2016 referendum, votes at sixteen) and takes them further, with unadapted primary sources, Dicey, the 2019 prorogation case, the 2022 Scottish referendum ruling, a mid-sequence test and a written exam.

A further sequence in Terminale, The Queen Who Had to Be Seen: Elizabeth II, Image and Power, starts from Bagehot’s dignified power (already studied here, especially in lesson 9) and asks how Elizabeth II used her image as its instrument. It does not repeat the two-column sort, the reign-or-rule exercise, or the extract read here.
