Getting there, getting around

France is far away, and Australia is a continent. This séance looks at the flight from France, at trains, buses, cars and domestic flights, and at the small (and not so small) planes that take mine workers from their home town to a remote site and back: fly-in fly-out, or FIFO.

Reading ★ B1

The flight from France. There is no regular direct flight from Paris to Sydney. You usually change planes once, for example in Dubai, Doha, Singapore, Hong Kong or Bangkok. The great-circle distance is about 17,000 kilometres. Door to door, the journey often lasts about 22 to 24 hours, including the stopover. Airlines publish slightly different times, so check before you book. The Department of Home Affairs also says: do not book your flights until your visa has been granted. In mid-November, Sydney is about ten hours ahead of Paris (the difference is 9 or 10 hours during the year, as your text on living there already notes).

A huge country. Once you land, the distances start again. By road, Sydney to Melbourne is about 880 kilometres (almost a full day's drive). Sydney to Perth is about 3,900 kilometres. Adelaide to Darwin is about 3,000 kilometres. Many backpackers take a long-distance bus, or a cheap domestic flight, rather than a car at the beginning.

Trains. Two famous trains cross the continent. The Ghan runs between Adelaide and Darwin, 2,979 km, in about 48 hours (three days, two nights on the 2026 timetable). The Indian Pacific runs between Sydney and Perth, 4,352 km, in about 68 hours (four days, three nights). They are beautiful journeys, and they are expensive: they are not the usual way to go to a farm job. (Journey Beyond, official distances and times)

On the road. Australians drive on the left. In the outback you meet "road trains" (very long trucks), animals on the road, and driver fatigue. Plan your rest, take water, and do not treat a seven-hour drive as a short hop.

Reading ★★ B2: FIFO, a job that flies

In the mining regions, especially in the Pilbara in Western Australia, many workers do not live next to the mine. They fly in and fly out (FIFO). They leave a hub, often Perth, on a company flight, work a "swing" of long shifts on site, then fly home for a block of rest.

The Western Australian Department of Transport explains that the resources industry mostly uses closed charter flights: they are not regular public flights, and they often go straight to a private airstrip at the mine. A company such as Fortescue describes typical rosters: 8 days on and 6 days off, 2 weeks on and 1 week off, or a week on and a week off. Shifts are often 12 or 12.5 hours. The employer usually organises, and pays for, the flight from the hub to the site, and camp and meals are often included. Getting to Perth itself is usually the worker's own cost.

Pros: wages are often high; food and a bed on site are typically provided; you get a real block of time at home between swings.

Cons: the hours are long; the site is isolated; you are far from friends and family; fatigue and mental health have been raised in official and health reviews. It is not a holiday with a salary.

And a working holiday visa? Some camp jobs (kitchen, cleaning, stores) are advertised, and some travellers do work in the resources sector. Many FIFO roles, however, need experience, safety tickets and a more stable visa. The Working Holiday visa also limits work with the same employer (usually six months, with some exemptions: always check Home Affairs, condition 8547). Never pay a doubtful "agent" for a job. This séance prepares the link with Finding a Job: FIFO is one option to know, not the first door for everybody.
