Lesson 5. Pipelines on Unceded Land

Wet’suwet’en territory and Trans Mountain.

Road map

Lesson of 55 minutes, timed for 52 minutes. Eight lessons in this sequence.

  1. Recap of lessons 1 and 4 0 to 4
  2. Goal and word box 4 to 8
  3. Coastal GasLink, Wet’suwet’en territory 8 to 22
  4. Trans Mountain, from Alberta to the Pacific 22 to 36
  5. Role play: who speaks for the nation? 36 to 44
  6. Written record and homework 44 to 52
  • Written record. Who said yes and who said no to each pipeline, and why consent is not the same as consultation.
  • Participation. Each student takes a role, or says one sentence from a role card.
  • Homework for next lesson. A two-column table: arguments for and against one of the two pipelines, with a source for each argument.

Stopping point. Minute 44, after a shorter role play than in the source dossier (the source gave 15 minutes; this official lesson keeps eight).

Emergency exit · 8 min. See the box at the end of the lesson.

Recap · 0 to 4 min

Remember lessons 1 and 4.

  1. What is the difference between treaty land and unceded land?
  2. Which nations objected to Site C, and in the name of what?

Answer key

  • Treaty land was the subject of a treaty with the Crown. Unceded land was never given up by treaty.
  • Treaty 8 First Nations, such as West Moberly, in the name of their treaty rights.

8 to 22 min

Case 1: Coastal GasLink, Wet’suwet’en territory

The project. A 670-km gas pipeline across northern British Columbia, to an export terminal for liquefied natural gas (LNG) in Kitimat.

Who said yes? Twenty First Nations band councils signed agreements, including five of the six band councils of the Wet’suwet’en Nation. In March 2022, sixteen First Nations signed options to buy a 10 per cent stake in the pipeline.

Who said no? Most Wet’suwet’en hereditary chiefs. They say that band councils, created under the Indian Act, are responsible for their reserves, but that the hereditary chiefs hold the title to 22,000 km² of traditional territory. This territory is unceded, as the Supreme Court of Canada recognised in 1997.

What happened? A court ordered protesters not to block the road. Police arrested 14 people in January 2019, and more in February 2020. Rail blockades in support of the hereditary chiefs then stopped trains in several provinces. The pipeline was mechanically complete in November 2023.

Optional video. Hereditary chiefs of Wet’suwet’en Nation oppose LNG pipeline | Power and Politics. CBC News · YouTube, 10 January 2019 · 10 min 04. Only the extract from 0:00 to 3:10 is proposed: 0:00 to 1:52, the news report with Justin Trudeau and John Horgan; 1:52 to 3:10, Peter Grant, lawyer for the hereditary chiefs. The questions on this page can also be answered from the short text beside them, if you cannot play the video. Open on YouTube.

  1. What did the court order, and what did the police do?
  2. Justin Trudeau compares today with “a hundred years ago”. What has changed, according to him? What does he add?
  3. Why does the Premier of British Columbia, John Horgan, think that the company did its job?
  4. According to Peter Grant, who holds the title to the land, and since when has this system existed?

Answer key

  • An injunction ordered protesters not to interfere with the project. Police arrested 14 protesters.
  • A hundred years ago, nobody was consulted. Today “that is not the way we do things anymore”. But he adds that Canada is “a country of the rule of law”, and the courts must be respected.
  • Because LNG Canada signed agreements with every First Nation along the pipeline corridor.
  • The hereditary chiefs of the house and clan system. This system existed long before contact with Europeans, and the Supreme Court recognised it in 1997.

Sources: CBC News, Coastal GasLink pipeline conflict, 2020 · TC Energy, mechanical completion of Coastal GasLink, 8 November 2023

22 to 36 min

Case 2: Trans Mountain, from Alberta to the Pacific

  • 2018. The federal government bought the pipeline and its expansion project for 4.5 billion dollars.
  • 30 August 2018. The Federal Court of Appeal quashed the approval. One reason: Canada had failed to consult Indigenous peoples properly. Officials “simply took notes” and did not engage in “meaningful two-way dialogue”.
  • 18 June 2019. After new consultations, the government approved the project again.
  • 1 May 2024. The expanded pipeline started operations. It cost about 34 billion dollars, and it carries about 890,000 barrels of oil per day, instead of about 300,000.
  • September 2026. Ottawa offered the 129 First Nations along the route a collective 15 per cent stake, plus 2.5 million dollars for each nation that invests. An Indigenous forum called the offer “everything we want”, but Grand Chief Stewart Phillip, of the Union of B.C. Indian Chiefs, said: “In British Columbia, consent is not for sale.” At the date of the teaching dossier (4 November 2026) the offer was still in negotiation.

Optional video. B.C. First Nations split on Kinder Morgan Trans Mountain pipeline. CBC News · YouTube, 16 April 2018 · 2 min 15. The questions on this page can also be answered from the short text beside them, if you cannot play the video. Open on YouTube.

  1. What is the “watch house”, and why was it built there?
  2. Some First Nations are opposed. What have dozens of others done?
  3. Why is the chief of the Whispering Pines/Clinton Indian Band frustrated?
  4. Why are the politics of the pipeline so complicated, according to the law professor?

Answer key

  • A house built out of one cedar tree, not far from where the pipeline ends: it is part of a long-standing protest to protect the water.
  • They have signed agreements with Kinder Morgan, the company that then owned the project.
  • His community is losing jobs and tax revenue: he wanted the project to start earlier.
  • The federal government has authority over the pipeline, but the environment is a shared jurisdiction.

Sources: Government of Canada, How TMX decisions were made · Reuters, Trans Mountain starts operations, 1 May 2024

Grammar spotlight

Obligation: the company has to respect the court order. Moral duty: the government ought to talk to the hereditary chiefs. Permission: protesters were not allowed to block the road. Concession: Although twenty band councils signed, most hereditary chiefs said no. Contrast: some nations want a stake, whereas others want no pipeline at all. It is a lot of money; yet some say consent is not for sale.

36 to 52 min

Role play: who speaks for the nation?

In groups of four, each student takes a role card. A pipeline company wants to build on a territory. Prepare for two minutes, then hold a meeting of about six minutes. The judge speaks last and decides what should happen next.

  • The elected chief. Your band council signed an agreement. Your community needs jobs, schools and money.
  • The hereditary chief. The territory has never been ceded. You say that no project can cross it without your consent.
  • The company representative. You signed agreements with every band council along the route and you offer a stake.
  • The judge. You listen, ask questions and remind everyone of the law and of the 1997 Supreme Court decision.

Useful language: We have to… / You ought to… We are not allowed to… Although you signed…, … Whereas the band council…, the hereditary chiefs…

Written record. On unceded Wet’suwet’en land, most hereditary chiefs said no to Coastal GasLink, while most band councils said yes. On Trans Mountain, the Federal Court of Appeal quashed the first approval in 2018 because consultation was not a “meaningful two-way dialogue”. Consultation is not the same as consent.

Emergency exit · 8 min

If time runs short, replace the unfinished plan with this condensed ending (inside the 52 minutes, not 52 plus 8).

  • 0 to 2. Everyone says this sentence aloud: “Consultation means asking; consent means that the project cannot go ahead without a yes.”
  • 2 to 5. Copy the written record: On unceded land, hereditary chiefs and band councils can disagree. Consent is not the same as consultation.
  • 5 to 7. Write down the homework: Make a for-and-against table with sources.
  • 7 to 8. Check one answer in the answer key.
