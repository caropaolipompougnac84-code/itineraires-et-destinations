Lesson 3. Rivers into Reservoirs

James Bay and the Cree.

Road map

Lesson of 55 minutes, timed for 52 minutes. Eight lessons in this sequence.

  1. Recap of lesson 2 0 to 4
  2. Goal and word box 4 to 8
  3. The James Bay project and the 1975 agreement 8 to 22
  4. Fort George, an island left behind 22 to 36
  5. A short testimony, then written record 36 to 48
  6. Homework 48 to 52
  • Written record. Why Fort George was relocated, and one cost and one gain of the agreement.
  • Participation. Each student reads one sentence of the testimony, or one answer about the project, aloud.
  • Homework for next lesson. Finish the 100-word testimony with used to, would and one past perfect. Optional: the CBC North video, and two points of view 50 years later.

Stopping point. Minute 36, after Fort George. If time runs short, dictate the written record and set the testimony for homework.

Emergency exit · 8 min. See the box at the end of the lesson.

Recap · 0 to 4 min

  2. What was the answer of the Indian Chiefs of Alberta?

8 to 22 min

A deal that changed northern Quebec

Optional video. How one deal led to the biggest project in Quebec history, but left a conflicted legacy. CBC News · YouTube, 11 November 2025 · 4 min 02. If you watch it, part 1 runs from 0:00 to 2:08 (the project and the court case) and part 2 from 2:08 to the end (the agreement and its legacy). The questions on this page can also be answered from the short text beside them, if you cannot play the video. Open on YouTube.

The Liberal Premier of Quebec, Robert Bourassa, announced the James Bay hydroelectric project in 1971. According to later reporting, he had not asked the people who lived on the land, the Cree and the Inuit. In 1973 the Cree and the Inuit obtained a historic judgment that ordered the province to stop all work and consult. They lost on appeal, but negotiations started. Hunting grounds as big as the American state of Connecticut were flooded or destroyed. In exchange, the Cree and the Inuit received cash compensation and more autonomy in key areas: education, justice, policing, economic development. Cree communities have mostly thrived; some Inuit say that the deal never gave them enough autonomy.

  1. Who announced the project, and when?
  2. What did he forget, according to the reporter?
  3. What happened in court in 1973?
  4. How big are the hunting grounds that were flooded or destroyed?
  5. What did the Cree and the Inuit receive in exchange? Have they seen the same benefits?

Answer key

  • The Liberal Premier of Quebec, Robert Bourassa, in 1971.
  • He forgot to ask the people who lived on the land, the Cree and the Inuit.
  • The Cree and the Inuit obtained a historic judgment that ordered the province to stop all work and consult. They lost on appeal, but negotiations started.
  • As big as the American state of Connecticut.
  • Cash compensation and more autonomy in key areas. No: Cree communities have mostly thrived, but some Inuit say that the deal never gave them enough autonomy.

22 to 36 min

Fort George, an island left behind

The James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement was signed in November 1975. The LG-2 dam, now the Robert-Bourassa generating station, was put into service in October 1979. The current of the La Grande River became twice as fast: from about 1,700 to more than 3,300 cubic metres per second.

Hydro-Québec feared that the island of Fort George would erode. Nearly 2,000 people had to move across the river, to a new community: Chisasibi. Portage trails and burial grounds disappeared under the reservoirs. Mercury levels rose in the fish, so many families stopped eating their traditional food. The agreement also brought economic development and Cree institutions, for example funding for higher education.

“We’re not anti-development, we’re anti-destruction.”

Daisy House, chief of Chisasibi, CBC News, 11 November 2025.

  1. Why did the people of Fort George have to move?
  2. What happened to the fish? Why is it serious for the Cree?
  3. Explain Daisy House’s sentence in your own words.

Answer key

  • Because the dam made the river flow much faster, and the island risked being eroded.
  • Mercury levels rose in the fish. Fish was an essential food, so families had to buy food trucked in from the south.
  • She is not against new projects, but she is against projects that destroy the land and the community’s way of life.

Sources: CBC News, 50 years later, Cree of Eeyou Istchee reflect on the cost of hydroelectricity, 11 November 2025

Grammar spotlight

Past habits: Cree families used to paddle the La Grande River. After long months on the land, they would gather on the island. Would for habits only works with actions, not with states: say “Fort George used to be a busy place”, not “would be”. Past perfect: Hydro-Québec had already started work when the Cree and the Inuit went to court.

36 to 52 min

A testimony, the written record, homework

Imagine that you are a young person from Fort George in 1979. Start a 100-word testimony in the first person: your life on the island, then the day you had to leave. Use used to, would and one past perfect. Finish it for homework if the stopping point has come.

Written record. After the LG-2 dam, the people of Fort George had to move to Chisasibi because the river flowed twice as fast. Hunting grounds and burial sites were flooded, and mercury rose in the fish; the 1975 agreement also brought Cree institutions and funding for education.

Emergency exit · 8 min

If time runs short, replace the unfinished plan with this condensed ending (inside the 52 minutes, not 52 plus 8).

  • 0 to 2. Everyone says this sentence aloud: “The people of Fort George had to move to Chisasibi when the river became twice as fast.”
