Summative assessment: Portrait of a Queen

Summative assessment (written comprehension, written expression, oral, each out of 20). It is not one of the nine timed lessons.

Written comprehension, written expression and oral, C1. Answer key hidden by default.

Summative assessment, C1. Three parts, each marked out of 20: written comprehension (40 minutes), written expression (60 minutes), oral (10 minutes per student: 5 minutes of continuous speech, 5 minutes of interaction). The final mark is the average of the three.

Formative checks along the way: the marked recap at the start of each lesson and the exit ticket at the end of each lesson.

1Written comprehension /20

The Queen who had to be seen

For seventy years, Elizabeth II was arguably the most frequently portrayed woman in the world. Her profile was printed on stamps and coins, her face was painted by artists as different as Pietro Annigoni and Lucian Freud, and her coronation in 1953 was the first ever to be broadcast live on television. Yet the woman behind these images remained, by design, remarkably difficult to read.

Part of the explanation lies in the nature of a constitutional monarchy. A sovereign who reigns but does not rule must never be seen to take sides. Elizabeth II never gave an interview, never voted and kept her weekly conversations with fifteen prime ministers entirely private. Her public image therefore had to speak for her, and she chose it with care.

Colour was one of her most effective tools. Her daughter-in-law explained in 2016 that the Queen needed to stand out, because crowds were often many rows deep and people wanted to be able to say that they had seen her, even if they had only glimpsed her hat. The bright coats and matching hats were not vanity: they were a way of being visible to everyone.

Some images, however, escaped her control. When Lucian Freud presented his tiny, unsparing portrait in 2001, critics were divided: some praised its honesty, others found it cruel. The Queen herself reacted with characteristic understatement, telling the painter that she had very much enjoyed watching him mix his colours. Half a century earlier, Winston Churchill had reacted very differently to Graham Sutherland's portrait of him, and his wife had had it destroyed.

Television drama has added yet another layer. The Crown, which Netflix describes as a "fictional dramatisation", has given millions of viewers a vivid but partly invented version of her inner life. Had she lived to see its final season, she might have recognised herself in some scenes and not at all in others. The real Queen, it seems, will always remain slightly out of frame.

Text written for this sequence from the sources quoted in lessons 1 to 8.

  1. (2 pts) Give two reasons why the Queen was so often portrayed.
  2. (2 pts) Explain "by design" in paragraph 1.
  3. (3 pts) Why does a constitutional monarch have to stay silent? Give three examples from the text.
  4. (2 pts) According to the text, why did she wear bright colours?
  5. (2 pts) Find a word in paragraph 4 meaning "showing everything without kindness".
  6. (3 pts) Compare the reactions of the Queen and of Churchill to their portraits.
  7. (2 pts) Identify the structure in "Had she lived to see its final season" and rewrite it with "if".
  8. (2 pts) What does the last sentence mean? Explain the metaphor.
  9. (2 pts) Give a title in French for the text and justify it in one sentence (in English).
Answer key
  1. Her long reign of seventy years; her image on stamps and coins; many painted portraits; the first televised coronation (any two).
  2. Deliberately: she chose to remain difficult to read.
  3. Because a monarch who reigns but does not rule must not take sides: she never gave an interview, never voted, kept her audiences with prime ministers private.
  4. To stand out and be visible to large crowds, so that people could say they had seen her.
  5. unsparing
  6. The Queen reacted with understatement and grace (she enjoyed watching him mix his colours); Churchill hated his portrait and his wife had it destroyed.
  7. An inverted third conditional: If she had lived to see its final season.
  8. Nobody will ever fully know the real woman: like a person partly outside a photograph's frame, she escapes every image.
  9. Example: « La reine qui devait être vue »: the text shows that visibility was a duty and a strategy.

2Written expression /20

Choose one subject (300 to 350 words).

  1. "A royal portrait should flatter, not tell the truth." Discuss, with at least two portraits studied in the sequence.
  2. You are a journalist on 1 July 1969 at Caernarfon. Write an article about the investiture, then add a final paragraph written in 2022, looking back.
  3. "Television drama rewrites history." Do you agree? Refer to The Crown and to verified facts.

3Oral /20

Continuous speech (5 minutes). The student draws one prompt and prepares for 10 minutes:

  1. A portrait of the Queen you find the most revealing, and why.
  2. Colour as a royal language.
  3. Duty and private life: Aberfan or the last scene of The Crown.
  4. "The Crown owns half of England": myth or fact?
  5. The royal family and Europe: a family tree as a map of history.

Interaction (5 minutes). The teacher plays a sceptical journalist and challenges the student's point of view.
