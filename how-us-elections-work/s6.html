Question: Who makes the laws, and how are they elected?

Objectives

  • Compare the House of Representatives and the Senate
  • Understand why the Senate is elected by thirds
  • Use numbers and durations: every two years, for six years
What we need to say

In this lesson, you need to imagine how things would change if the rules or the numbers were different.

To explain how a law is made, we need these words: Congress, representative, senator, district, bill, veto.

To imagine a result, we need the second conditional: If she won Texas, she would get forty electoral votes. What would happen if the president vetoed a bill?

Documents

Key document

Congress has two chambers. The House of Representatives has 435 voting members: each represents a district, and big states have more districts. Representatives are elected for two years, so all 435 seats are elected every two years. A representative must be at least twenty-five. The Senate has 100 senators, two per state, whatever the size of the state. Senators are elected for six years, and every two years one third of the Senate is elected. A senator must be at least thirty. A bill must be passed by both chambers; then the president signs it or refuses it with a veto. Only the House can impeach a president; the Senate holds the trial and confirms judges and ministers. The Speaker of the House is Mike Johnson; the Vice President, JD Vance, presides over the Senate and can break a tie.

Read the source: house.gov, The House explained · USAGov, Congressional elections · National Archives, The Constitution, Article I

Grammar to say it: the conditional (2), imaginary situations

Why we need it

To imagine a result, we need the second conditional: If she won Texas, she would get forty electoral votes. What would happen if the president vetoed a bill?

Grammar point: board explanation

Teacher script
  1. Ask: What would happen if a candidate won Texas? Write: If she won Texas, she would get forty electoral votes.
  2. Explain: the past form does not mean the past here; it means imaginary or unlikely.
  3. Contrast on the board with the first conditional (lesson five): real versus imaginary.
  4. Teach If I were: were for all persons in careful English.
  5. Explain the position of adverbs of frequency: before the main verb, after be: Senators are rarely young; the House usually votes first.
  6. Students copy, then do the quick check.
Board: students copy

The second conditional; adverbs of frequency

Rule
Imaginary or unlikely situation: If + past simple, would + verb.
Form
If a candidate won Texas, she would get forty electoral votes. If I were a senator, I would vote yes. Question: What would happen if the president vetoed a bill?
Use
To imagine, to give advice, to discuss other rules.
Examples from the lesson
  • If Wyoming had more people, it would have more representatives.
  • If I were in Congress, I would vote for this bill.
  • What would happen if the House and the Senate disagreed?
Common mistakes
  • If I would be a senator → If I were a senator.
  • If she wins Texas, she would get → If she won Texas, she would get (keep the pair).
  • He votes always → He always votes.
French and English
French: Si j'étais sénateur, je voterais oui. Same logic: si + imparfait = if + past; conditionnel = would. Never si je serais, never if I would be.
Also on the board: Word order: adverbs of frequency
always, usually, often, sometimes, rarely, never: before the main verb (The House usually votes first), after be (Independents are rarely elected).

Figures of speech: parody, hyperbole and personification

Learn

A parody copies famous words to make fun of something. Keppler's sign parodies Lincoln's Gettysburg Address (1863): government of the people, by the people, for the people. Hyperbole is exaggeration: the money bags are huge. Personification: the bags have faces and hats, like people.

  1. Which famous words does the sign parody?
  2. Why are the money bags drawn so big?
  3. Why is the People's Entrance closed?
  4. Which figure of speech gives the bags faces?
  5. Capitol Hill passed a bill. What is this figure (seen in lesson four)?
  6. Write your own parody: a school of the ______, by the ______, for the ______.
Answer key
  1. Lincoln's Gettysburg Address: government of the people, by the people, for the people.
  2. Hyperbole: to show that big companies were too powerful.
  3. It is a symbol: ordinary people had no access to the Senate.
  4. Personification.
  5. Metonymy (Capitol Hill = Congress).
  6. Example: a school of the students, by the students, for the students.

Finer grammar: mixed conditionals and inversionGoing further

Rule

Mixed conditional: past condition, present result: If the 17th Amendment had not been ratified, senators would still be chosen by state legislatures today. Formal inversion: Were the president to veto the bill, Congress would vote again. Should the Senate be split, the Vice President would vote.

  1. Rewrite with inversion: If the president vetoed the bill, Congress would vote again.
  2. Rewrite with inversion: If the Senate is split 50 to 50, the Vice President will break the tie. (Should ...)
  3. Mixed: The Constitution created two chambers in 1787. Congress has two chambers today. (If ... had not ..., ... would not ...)
  4. Mixed: The 17th Amendment was ratified. Voters elect their senators today.
Answer key
  1. Were the president to veto the bill, Congress would vote again.
  2. Should the Senate be split 50 to 50, the Vice President will break the tie.
  3. If the Constitution had not created two chambers in 1787, Congress would not have two chambers today.
  4. If the 17th Amendment had not been ratified, voters would not elect their senators today.

Text analysis: the Senate and the money bagsGoing further

When Joseph Keppler drew The Bosses of the Senate in 1889, senators were not elected by the people but chosen by state legislatures. Critics claimed that rich companies, the trusts, could buy influence in these assemblies. Reformers campaigned for years, and in the early twentieth century the 17th Amendment gave the choice of senators to the voters. Were Keppler alive today, he might draw new money bags: the debate about money in politics has never really ended.

  1. Find the conditional with inversion and rewrite it with if.
  2. Who chose the senators in 1889?
  3. What were the trusts?
  4. Find a reporting verb.
  5. Is the last sentence a fact or an opinion? Explain.
Answer key
  1. Were Keppler alive today, he might draw ... = If Keppler were alive today, he might draw ...
  2. State legislatures (state parliaments).
  3. Very large companies that controlled whole industries.
  4. claimed.
  5. An opinion: might and has never really ended show the author's view.

Argued task (200 to 250 words, or a two-minute oral)Going further

Is the American Congress more democratic than the French Parliament? Compare how members are elected and what powers they have.

Steps

  1. Introduction: two chambers in both countries.
  2. Elections compared (direct vote for both American chambers; the French Senate is elected indirectly).
  3. Powers compared.
  4. Your argued conclusion.
Sentence starters
  • Both countries have ...
  • Whereas American senators are elected directly, ...
  • Were France to elect its senators directly, ...
  • It could be argued that ...
  • In conclusion, ...
Key points

Key points: two chambers in both countries; in the USA both are elected by the people; in France, deputies are elected directly and senators by local elected officials; the American Congress has strong powers (budget, impeachment, confirmation); a nuanced conclusion.
