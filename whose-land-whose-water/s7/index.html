Lesson 7. The Grizzlies: Youth, Land and Hope

Goal: I can compare how the film, its official trailer and an interview present youth agency, and bring one voice from the film into the debate.

Road map

Lesson of 55 minutes, timed for 52 minutes. Lesson 7 of 8. The remaining three minutes allow for arrival, equipment and departure.

Student text

Read

The Grizzlies (2018) is a Canadian film directed by Miranda de Pencier. It is based on a true story involving young people and a lacrosse team in Kugluktuk, in what is now Nunavut.

You watched the whole film at home before this lesson: it is compulsory homework, set in lesson 6. The film opens up voices that you will use in the debate and in the lesson 8 hearing: students, parents, elders and a teacher from the south.

The interview presents young Inuit people as participants with their own relationships, abilities and ambitions. The story should not be reduced to one in which an outsider simply “saves” a community.

Today you compare three documents: the film you watched, its official trailer and an interview. A trailer is made to sell a film, so it selects moments. Always say which document your evidence comes from.

Our rule for discussing Inuit voices

Discuss what a named character says or does, or what the film presents.

Use:

“Adam’s grandparents are presented as…”

“Miranda’s actions suggest…”

“In this scene, the film invites viewers to…”

Do not use:

“All Inuit people think…”

Do not pretend to speak as an Inuit person, an Elder or a member of the community. In the debate, speak as a student analysing sources.

Attributing a viewpoint

Supporting an interpretation

Adding nuance

  • To some extent, …
  • This may suggest …, but it does not prove …
  • Although …, …
  • On the one hand, … On the other hand, …
  • This is true of this character, not necessarily of everyone.

Disagreeing respectfully

  • I understand your point, but …
  • I agree about …, although …
  • Could we distinguish … from …?
  • Which scene supports that interpretation?
  • Are we discussing what the character believes or what we believe?

11. Language focus: activities, concessions and careful claims

A. An activity as the subject

Use the -ing form.

Playing together can strengthen relationships.

Not:

Play together can strengthen relationships.

B. A concession

Use “although” to recognise a limit or a contrasting fact.

Although Russ starts the project, he does not control every decision.

Avoid combining “although” and “but” in the same structure.

C. A careful claim

Use “may” or “can” when an effect is possible, not guaranteed.

Sport can create opportunities for cooperation.

This is more careful than:

Sport always solves social problems.

13. Prepare the debate

Debate questions

Choose one main question and one question you are ready to answer.

  1. Who changes whom?
    Is the strongest interpretation that Russ changes the students, that the students change Russ, or that change comes through relationships?
  2. Support or control?
    When does help become a decision made for other people? Which moments show Russ learning this distinction?
  3. What counts as education?
    How should we compare classroom learning, learning on the land and learning through a team without ranking people’s knowledge as superior or inferior?
  4. Land and water: setting or relationship?
    What does the film show about land and water as sources of food, knowledge and connection? What does it not establish about legal ownership or water rights?
  5. What makes a promise responsible?
    Is a good intention enough, or must a promise include resources, consultation and a realistic plan?
  6. Who gets heard?
    Compare Miranda’s organising, the council’s authority, the grandmother’s intervention and Russ’s position. Who can act, and who must persuade others?
  7. Who defines success?
    Do the teacher, the players and the competition use the same measure of success?
  8. Who speaks for whom?
    Does the film give young characters room to influence the story, or does it still centre the outsider? Can both interpretations be partly true?
  9. Hope and structural change
    Can a hopeful ending recognise continuing material and institutional problems? What would be lost if we discussed only personal resilience?

A boundary for the sequence

The film can help us question who decides, who belongs, who listens and who keeps commitments. It cannot, on its own, establish the facts of a separate land dispute, a treaty claim or a water-rights controversy.

For those claims, use the relevant sequence document. Name it accurately. Do not turn a thematic connection into invented factual evidence.

3. Interpretation guidance

There is no fixed answer to an interpretation question. Award credit for an identifiable moment, a clear explanation and an appropriately limited claim.

QuestionsPossible lines of interpretation
P1.5 to P1.7Russ initially reads absence or resistance through his expectations of school. The opening gives viewers substantial access through his arrival, while also introducing young people’s lives. Visual analysis should describe framing, space or atmosphere without claiming that a landscape determines everyone’s feelings.
P2.6 to P2.8Miranda advises and organises; players choose to participate; young people bring relationships and knowledge Russ lacks. Practice can create contact, cooperation and a shared purpose.
P3.9School learning and learning through hunting need not be treated as mutually exclusive. Students should recognise the family’s knowledge and the institutional history affecting trust.
P3.10The hunting scene connects land and water with food, knowledge and relationships. Accept other accurately described moments.
P3.11The first statement is attributed and historically contextualised within the story. The second overgeneralises from particular characters to all Inuit families.
P4.5 to P4.7The gathering shows peer relationships and collective mourning. Commitments affect people who rely on them. The continuing losses and pressures prevent a simple “sport fixes everything” reading.
P5.6Russ initially offers participation as a sufficient achievement; Kyle asserts the players’ wish to keep striving and score. His response gives a young person authority over the meaning of the match.
P5.7 to P5.8Hope can be read as continued action and connection rather than a guarantee. The closing material links drama to real lives but does not turn the film into a complete community history.

The interpretation of Russ’s role should remain debatable. Published reviews differ in how successfully they think the film moves beyond an outsider-centred rescue narrative. Students may defend either judgement with film evidence.

6. After-viewing and debate models

A1 model

Before viewing, I thought the coach would define the team’s success. After viewing, I would qualify this because Kyle challenges Russ during the final match and encourages the players to keep trying.

A2 model

The moment that challenged my expectations most was Kyle’s response during the final match. I expected the coach to decide what counted as success, but Kyle insists that the players should continue trying. Miranda’s fundraising also matters because it makes her an organiser rather than simply someone receiving help. Although lacrosse creates a shared purpose, the film does not show that sport removes every difficulty. Adam’s responsibilities and his grandparents’ distrust of schooling reveal pressures that a team cannot resolve alone. The hunting scene connects with our sequence because land and water are linked with knowledge and livelihood, not only scenery. In my view, the film offers hope through relationships. However, it presents selected experiences, and I would not use it to claim that all Inuit people share one viewpoint.

This is a model of structure, not a required opinion.

D1 model debate card

  • Question: Who defines success?
  • Position: The players should have a voice in defining their own achievement.
  • Evidence 1: Kyle challenges Russ during the final match.
  • Evidence 2: Miranda influences the conditions that allow the team to travel.
  • Different interpretation: Russ’s encouragement helps the players recognise what they have already achieved.
  • Response: That support matters, although it should not replace listening to their ambitions.
  • Sequence connection: Participation in decisions affecting one’s future.
  • Further source needed: A named sequence document showing who participates in a land or water decision.
  • Limit: A sports decision and a legal rights dispute are not identical.
  • Question: Can encouragement become limiting if adults decide what is enough?

Answer key

Trailer: 1. “6,000 years.” Inuit people have lived there for thousands of years: the teacher is the newcomer. 2. Lacrosse. 3. “We don’t need to defend our way of life to a southerner.” “Family comes first. Not school, not some white man’s game.” Also accept the man who asks the teacher who he is to say what they need. 4. It moves the focus from the outsider to the young people: they have made sacrifices, they have been dealing with difficulties for years and they are “still here”. 5. proud, strong, hope. 6. Accept any accurate moment from the film, labelled as coming from the film, not from the trailer.

Listening: an inspiring true story; the teacher says the young people changed him more than he changed them; hope and the emergence of a talented younger generation; justified reference to their actions, talents or mutual support. Only award direct-listening credit for material heard in the selected extracts. Wider information in the teaching dossier must be labelled as reading support.

Comparison: accept justified analysis. Do not accept “all Inuit teenagers are isolated” or “sport solves every problem”.

Written record

The Grizzlies, its trailer and the interview highlight relationships, youth agency and hope. In the trailer, a student tells the teacher: “This is not about you.” Playing together can create support, but one story does not represent every Inuit experience. A trailer selects moments to sell a film, so evidence must say whether it comes from the film, the trailer or the interview.
