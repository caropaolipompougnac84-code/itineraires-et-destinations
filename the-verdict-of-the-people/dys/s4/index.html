Lesson 4. A Promise Is Not a Result

Goal: I can recognise a promise and ask for evidence.

Road map

Lesson of 55 minutes, timed for 52 minutes. Lesson 4 of 7. Minutes 52 to 55: no new task. Working at home and need longer? Split the lesson into two blocks: this is a classroom structure, not a speed test.

  1. Say one sentence from the previous lesson (Lesson 1: what you already know about elections) 4 min (minutes 0 to 4)
  2. Read the goal and the word box 4 min (minutes 4 to 8)
  3. Read or listen to your route’s text 8 min (minutes 8 to 16)
  4. Exercise 1, then check it 8 min (minutes 16 to 24)
  5. Pause: look away from the text or move 2 min (minutes 24 to 26)
  6. Exercise 2, then check it 8 min (minutes 26 to 34)
  7. Exercise 3, then check it 6 min (minutes 34 to 40)
  8. Say your final answer aloud with the speaking frame 4 min (minutes 40 to 44)
  9. Written record 4 min (minutes 44 to 48)
  10. Progress record 2 min (minutes 48 to 50)
  11. Read and write down the homework 2 min (minutes 50 to 52)
  • Written record. One sentence for your route (see below), copied or typed.
  • Participation. Say at least one answer aloud. In class, use a quiet voice; at home, say it to yourself. You do not need to record yourself.
  • Homework for next lesson. Make two cards: promise and evidence. Write one question you can use to check a public claim.

Stopping point. Minute 44: stop the main exercises. Keep the written record and the homework, even if an exercise is unfinished.

Emergency exit · 8 min. If only eight working minutes remain, this replaces the unfinished plan; it is not added to it.
  1. Minutes 0 to 2: say the emergency sentence of the lesson aloud.
  2. Minutes 2 to 5: complete the short written record.
  3. Minutes 5 to 7: write down the homework.
  4. Minute 7 to 8: check one answer with the key. Do not rush through all the unfinished exercises.

All the messages in this lesson are invented classroom examples. They are not quotations from real candidates.

Word box

WordMeaning
promisesomething a person says they will do
claimsomething presented as true
evidenceinformation that supports or challenges a claim
checkexamine to see if it is true
reportexplain what someone said or what happened

Choose your route: Route S · SEGPA · Route C · CAP · Route P · Bac pro · CFA. Do only your route.

Route S · SEGPA

Short text

A fictional candidate says: “I will improve the bus service.”

This is a promise.

The improvement has not happened in this example.

We can ask: “How will you do it?”

Exercise S1

Choose

Worked example. “I will improve the bus service” is a promise.

Now you.
“It has already happened.”
A. Yes.
B. The text does not say that.

Self-check

B.

Exercise S2

Complete

Worked example. “A promise is not a finished result.”

Now you.
Complete: “We can ask: How will you ?”

Self-check

do it.

Exercise S3

Ask a question

Worked example. “How much will it cost?”

Now you.
Write or say one question about the promise.

Self-check

Accept: How will you do it? How much will it cost? When will it happen?

Route C · CAP

Short text

A fictional candidate promises a better bus service.

The message sounds positive.

However, it gives no price and no timetable.

A careful reader asks for more information.

A promise may be useful, but it is not evidence that the plan will succeed.

Exercise C1

Find missing information

Worked example. Missing information: the price.

Now you.
Name one other missing detail.

Self-check

The timetable.

Exercise C2

Choose

Worked example. “Positive language can persuade.” True.

Now you.
Choose the accurate statement.
A. A positive message proves success.
B. A positive message can still need evidence.

Self-check

B.

Exercise C3

Write a reply

Worked example. “The proposal sounds useful. What will it cost?”

Now you.
Write two sentences: “The proposal sounds . How ?”

Self-check

Your answer should recognise the proposal and ask a relevant question. It must not claim that the plan has already succeeded.

Route P · Bac pro

Short text

A fictional campaign message promises a better evening bus service.

It gives no budget or timetable.

The proposal may be useful.

However, the message does not show that the plan is affordable or effective.

A reporter should separate the candidate’s promise from an achieved result.

Attribution helps the audience understand who made the claim.

Exercise P1

Report

Worked example. “I support the proposal.” becomes “The candidate said that they supported the proposal.”

Now you.
Report: “We will publish the cost.” Begin: “The campaigners said…”

Self-check

“The campaigners said that they would publish the cost.”

Exercise P2

Correct

Worked example. “The candidate promised success” is not the same as “The plan succeeded.”

Now you.
Correct: “The candidate promised a better service, so the service has improved.”

Self-check

“The candidate promised a better service, but the promise does not show that an improvement has happened.”

Exercise P3

Write a short fact-check

Worked example. Structure: “The message claims… It does not provide… We need…”

Now you.
Write 60 to 80 words.

Self-check

Include the promise, one missing detail and one relevant question. Do not invent costs or outcomes.

CFA pathway

CAP apprentice: use Route C. Bac pro apprentice: use Route P. Do Exercises 1 and 2 of your route, then do this task instead of Exercise 3. Keep the same written record and homework.

CFA task

Check a service promise

Worked example. Invented workplace message: “Our new delivery service will solve every problem.” Question: “What delivery time do you promise?”

Now you.
Write two questions before recommending the service.

Self-check

Good questions are about price, timing, coverage, evidence or conditions. No invented company information is needed.

Written record

S: “A promise is not a result.”

C: “A positive message may still need evidence.”

P: “A reporter must separate promises, evidence and achieved results.”

Speaking frame. “The message promises . I would ask .”

Emergency sentence. “We need evidence before claiming success.”

Homework

Make two cards: promise and evidence. Write one question you can use to check a public claim.
